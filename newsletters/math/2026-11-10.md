Greetings from "My Daily Clippings," your daily dose of mathematical insights for the curious engineer. Today, we're diving into two thought-provoking discussions from the mathematical community, both highlighting the profound impact of artificial intelligence. We'll explore how AI is reshaping what and how we teach our aspiring mathematicians, and hear directly from early career researchers about their hopes and anxieties in this rapidly evolving landscape.

## Rethinking Undergraduate Math Education in the Age of AI

The rapid advancement of artificial intelligence has sent ripples through the academic world, and mathematics is no exception. A recent guest post on Terence Tao's widely read blog, penned by Jana Gevertz and Steffen Marcus, tackles a crucial question: "What should we teach our (undergraduate) students?". This isn't just an academic exercise; it's about preparing the next generation of mathematicians and professionals for a world where AI is an increasingly powerful tool.

Gevertz and Marcus highlight that AI has impacted the discipline with a "frightening force". Historically, mathematical understanding has been built through rigorous practice: wrestling with challenging problems, constructing proofs, and performing computations by hand. These activities are fundamental to developing intuition and deep comprehension. However, AI tools can now automate many of these very processes.

The core challenge, as the authors explain, is how students will build this essential understanding when AI can generate solutions or proofs. Mathematicians are concerned about maintaining the rigor and critical thinking skills that define the discipline. The consensus emerging is that we cannot simply ignore AI. Instead, students must learn to work *with* these systems. This means developing skills in **prompting** AI models, **interpreting** their outputs, **checking** for accuracy, and **communicating** mathematical ideas that AI helped generate.

The authors emphasize that the goal is not to offload students' ability to think independently but to train them to critically validate AI outputs. This involves fostering mathematical intuition, reasoning, and computational skills *in concert* with AI technology, rather than treating them as separate entities. For engineers, this resonates deeply with the need to understand the underlying principles of complex systems, even when automated tools handle the heavy lifting. The mathematical community is grappling with how to adapt its foundational teaching methods to ensure future graduates are both proficient in traditional mathematics and adept at leveraging cutting-edge AI.

## Early Career Mathematicians Weigh in on AI's Future

Complementing the discussion on education, another significant guest post on Terence Tao's blog, this one by Anna Brandenberger, Hanna Mularczyk, and Dora Woodruff from MIT, shares insights from their "Survey of Early Career Mathematicians". Conducted in late September and early October of this year, the survey aimed to take a "temperature check" of the community and provide a platform for younger mathematicians to voice their perspectives on AI.

The motivation behind this survey is particularly compelling. While established figures, including Fields medalists and prominent professors, have actively discussed AI's role in mathematics, the unique viewpoints of early career researchers, such as graduate students and postdocs, have often been underrepresented. These individuals face significant personal stakes, including job insecurity and funding instability, making their concerns about AI's impact on their careers especially acute. They worry that their perspectives might be "deprioritized, or even misrepresented" without collective efforts like this survey.

The survey, which gathered responses from over 260 early career mathematicians across nearly 60 institutions and at least five countries, included both multiple-choice and open-ended questions. It was not intended as a rigorous statistical analysis but rather as a means to capture the sentiment and experiences of this crucial demographic. The timing of the survey is also notable, coinciding with a period of rapid AI advancement in mathematics. For instance, the authors mention that in May 2026, a large language model reportedly disproved Erdös's unit distance problem, followed by ten AI-generated advances in mathematics and computer science in August, and an AI-generated solution to Navier Stokes in September.

The MIT researchers hope that the survey results will help senior mathematicians and university administrators understand the concerns of younger generations and prompt them to take appropriate action. This initiative underscores the mathematical community's commitment to understanding and navigating the profound changes brought about by AI, ensuring that the voices of those most directly impacted by these shifts are heard and considered.
