My Daily Clippings

The world of mathematics is buzzing, and perhaps a little bewildered, this week, as artificial intelligence systems continue to make unprecedented strides in solving long-standing problems. In the past few days alone, we've seen major announcements from leading AI labs, including a claimed proof of the elusive Riemann Hypothesis and a flurry of new research papers generated entirely by AI. These developments are not just academic curiosities; they are sparking intense debate about the future of mathematical research, human comprehension, and even the security of our digital world.

## The Riemann Hypothesis: A Century-Old Puzzle "Solved" by AI

One of the most significant pieces of news comes from Anthropic, where mathematician Levent Alpöge, working with an unreleased version of the company's **Claude** AI model, announced a formal proof of the **Riemann Hypothesis** on November twentieth. This conjecture, first proposed by Bernhard Riemann in eighteen fifty-nine, is a cornerstone of number theory, deeply connected to the distribution of **prime numbers**. It's also one of the seven **Millennium Prize Problems**, each carrying a one million dollar reward for its solution.

The proof itself is a monumental achievement, formalized in **Lean**, a specialized programming language used for machine-checkable mathematical proofs. The initial version of Alpöge's proof reportedly comprised about seventeen million lines of code. Crucially, on November twenty-first, the Lean Focused Research Organization and the Mathlib Initiative independently verified this massive proof, stating they had full confidence in its correctness. A more streamlined version, reduced to three hundred thousand lines, was released by Anthropic researcher Luke Sernau on November twenty-fifth, with an even shorter seventy-six thousand line version also confirmed correct by November twenty-sixth.

For mathematicians, the Riemann Hypothesis is more than just a prize problem; its truth would unlock deeper understanding of how prime numbers are scattered along the number line, impacting fields from pure number theory to cryptography. While the computational verification is a huge step, the sheer scale of the AI-generated proof means that a complete, human-readable explanation is still pending. This raises a profound question: can a proof truly be "understood" if no human mind can fully grasp its entire logical flow? Some mathematicians, like Cornell University's Steven Strogatz, have voiced concerns about this comprehension gap, likening the challenge of understanding AI-generated proofs to teaching calculus to a dog. Despite these concerns, the computational verification by the Lean community is a strong indicator of the proof's validity.

## Google's Cogentic AI: Autonomous Research at Scale

Not to be outdone, Google Research made its own significant announcement on November twenty-fifth, releasing six mathematical research papers produced entirely by **Cogentic**, its multi-agent AI system built on **Gemini 4**. These papers, posted to Google's public GitHub repository, cover diverse areas including number theory, cryptography, game theory, and computational complexity.

This release is particularly noteworthy because Google explicitly states that there was "no human interaction other than the initial prompt for Cogentic to generate a full proof and formalization". This marks a qualitative shift, positioning AI not just as a tool for human mathematicians, but as an autonomous research producer. Among the claims is a new theoretical bound for integer factoring, suggesting a deterministic subexponential algorithm. While this is a significant theoretical advance, Google clarifies it's not a practical threat to current encryption methods like RSA, as its constant is far larger than existing practical algorithms. Google has also indicated that complete **Lean 4** formalizations for these papers will follow, allowing for machine-checkable verification by the broader mathematical community.

## The Shifting Landscape of Mathematical Discovery

These recent breakthroughs are part of a larger trend in two thousand twenty-six, a year many are calling a turning point for mathematics due to AI's rapid progress on hundreds of open problems. Earlier this year, OpenAI also announced a solution to the **Navier-Stokes problem**, another Millennium Prize Problem, though its verification process has faced some scrutiny regarding the translation from human-readable concepts to formal code.

The implications of AI's growing prowess are being widely debated. Fields medalist Terence Tao has called for a transition to "Math 2.0," envisioning a future where mathematicians use AI imaginatively to push the boundaries of research. However, he also warns against "misaligned math" if human understanding fails to keep pace with AI's discoveries. To address this, Tao's blog is hosting a **Polymath project** aimed at extracting a human-readable and understandable proof of the Riemann Hypothesis from Alpöge's Lean certificate.

There are also discussions about the potential impact on **cryptography**. While the Riemann Hypothesis proof itself doesn't immediately compromise current encryption, the rapid advancements in AI have reignited fears about the mathematical assumptions underpinning modern security systems. Experts generally agree that while AI's progress warrants attention, a practical threat to systems like Bitcoin's cryptography has not yet been demonstrated.

The competitive landscape among AI labs is also evident, with Epic Games CEO Tim Sweeney claiming just yesterday, November twenty-eighth, that Google's **Gemini Neon** model is currently leading in mathematical and theoretical reasoning tasks among publicly available models. This ongoing race to demonstrate advanced reasoning capabilities highlights the strategic importance of mathematical breakthroughs for these technology giants. The coming months will undoubtedly continue to reshape how we approach and understand the very foundations of mathematics.
