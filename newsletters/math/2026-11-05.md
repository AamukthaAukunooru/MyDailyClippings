Good morning, fellow explorers of the quantitative world! ## The Long Shadow of Kindergarten on Math Skills

New research from the University of Toronto's Ontario Institute for Studies in Education, or OISE, reveals a striking connection between a child's readiness for kindergarten and their math achievement nearly a decade later, through ninth grade. Led by Dr. Fatemeh Ameli, a Canadian Postdoctoral Research Award Fellow and sessional lecturer at OISE, this study followed over twenty-five thousand Ontario students, tracking their developmental readiness in kindergarten and their subsequent performance on standardized provincial math tests in grades three, six, and nine.

The findings, published in the *Canadian Journal of Behavioural Science*, indicate that a child's early developmental readiness, encompassing not just language and cognitive skills but also physical well-being and social competence, is a strong predictor of their long-term mathematical success. While the gaps in math scores between children who were developmentally ready and those who were less so did narrow slightly over time, they never fully disappeared. This suggests that the foundational skills and experiences gained in those crucial early years can set a trajectory for quantitative abilities that persists well into high school.

Why does this matter to mathematicians and engineers? For mathematicians, understanding the developmental pathways of mathematical thinking is key to appreciating how abstract concepts are built upon intuitive understandings. This research underscores the critical importance of early childhood education, not just for general development, but specifically for cultivating the cognitive groundwork necessary for advanced mathematical reasoning and problem-solving. For engineers, who rely heavily on robust quantitative skills, this highlights that the roots of complex technical proficiency are planted far earlier than many might assume, emphasizing the societal importance of strong early education systems.

## Peeking Inside the AI Mind

In a development that blurs the lines between computer science and philosophy, new research is exploring whether artificial intelligence models can "know their own thoughts." As reported by *Quanta Magazine*, researchers are attempting to map out the capacity for **introspection** in large language models by directly injecting "thoughts" into their neural networks.

Jack Lindsey, a researcher at Anthropic, devised a method akin to "targeted digital neurosurgery" in 2025. He and his colleagues developed ways to identify specific patterns of activity within a language model's "neurons" that correspond to particular concepts, such as "the Golden Gate Bridge" or "inner conflict." By artificially amplifying these patterns, they could influence the model's outputs towards the associated concept. Intriguingly, Lindsey's experiments showed that these models could sometimes detect and even describe these injected thoughts, a behavior that strongly resembled introspection. Furthermore, the models demonstrated an ability for "anomaly detection," sensing when something unusual occurred in their internal processing, even if they couldn't pinpoint the exact details.

For mathematicians, this work is fascinating because it delves into the theoretical underpinnings and emergent properties of complex computational systems. Large language models are built on intricate mathematical structures called neural networks, and understanding how these networks process information, and potentially develop forms of self-awareness, pushes the boundaries of what we thought possible for algorithms. For engineers, this research is vital for developing more sophisticated and potentially more transparent AI. If AI models can gain some form of introspection, it could lead to systems that are better at explaining their reasoning, identifying their own limitations, and perhaps even learning more effectively, moving us closer to truly intelligent machines.

## Guiding the Next Generation of Mathematicians

The Association for Human Mathematics, or AHM, through its Teaching Working Group, has released a significant set of recommendations aimed at improving **doctoral advising** in mathematics. Titled "Preserving Our Humanity, Supporting Our Students: Doctoral Advising in Mathematics," these guidelines address the evolving landscape of mathematical education, particularly in light of the widespread adoption of generative AI and large language models.

The document outlines five key guidelines designed for departments, advisors, and students. These include prioritizing student training in human-authored technical writing and mathematical practices, explicitly avoiding overreliance on AI systems, and confronting the ethical concerns surrounding AI use in academic work. The AHM also recommends establishing clear, written agreements between students and advisors regarding the use of AI, ensuring students are provided with supportive communities and clear expectations, and crucially, emphasizing empathy, compassion, and care, with faculty trained to address student mental health concerns.

Mathematicians care deeply about these guidelines because the quality of doctoral advising directly impacts the future of the field. Effective mentorship is essential for cultivating rigorous research skills, fostering intellectual independence, and ensuring the well-being of emerging scholars. In an era where AI tools are rapidly changing research practices, these guidelines aim to safeguard the core human elements of mathematical discovery and education. For engineers, who also navigate complex and rapidly evolving technical fields, these recommendations resonate with the importance of robust mentorship and ethical considerations in advanced technical training, ensuring that the next generation of innovators is not only skilled but also thoughtful and well-supported.
