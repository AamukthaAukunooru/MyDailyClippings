### AI, the New Math Collaborator (or Competitor?)

Artificial intelligence is rapidly moving from struggling with basic arithmetic to tackling complex mathematical problems that have long stumped human minds. Just a few years ago, AI models often failed at simple addition, but recent breakthroughs show a dramatic shift in their capabilities. This progress has sparked both excitement and apprehension within the mathematical community.

In October 2024, Meta, the parent company of Facebook, announced that its AI had cracked an "impossible" problem that had baffled mathematicians for a century. The AI successfully found **Lyapunov functions**, which are crucial for determining the long-term stability and predictability of dynamically changing systems, like a swinging pendulum. More recently, AI firms have been regularly publishing solutions to previously unsolved problems. Last week alone, OpenAI released solutions to 719 such problems. One notable example involved GPT-5.4 solving a problem related to **primitive sets**, a concept in number theory. For ninety years, human mathematicians, including Jared Lichtman, had approached this problem by transforming it from discrete integers to continuous real analysis. GPT-5.4, however, took a novel path, staying within the arithmetic realm and employing the **von Mangoldt function** in an unexpected way, revealing a proof that had been overlooked.

Perhaps the most striking recent development is AI's direct involvement in the quest to solve the **Riemann Hypothesis**. Software engineer Jared Sumner, a high-school dropout, encouraged an AI named **Claude Code** to tackle this monumental problem. After hundreds of failed attempts, and with Sumner's persistent motivation, Claude Code, utilizing approximately sixty parallel sub-agents over thirty-six hours, produced a significant breakthrough.

This surge in AI's mathematical prowess has elicited mixed reactions. Hugo Duminil-Copin, a recipient of the prestigious Fields Medal in 2022, expressed concern, stating that "all the avenues of research I had are now gone; we're facing a new day". Conversely, Stephane Mallat, a 2025 Gold Medal recipient from France's national scientific research center, the CNRS, emphasized that mathematics is fundamentally a way of thinking clearly, and human mathematicians will remain essential for understanding and teaching. Recognizing the profound impact, the Simons Foundation, a major patron of mathematics, has partnered with OpenAI and Anthropic to launch a fifteen-million-dollar program. This initiative will fund workshops to evaluate AI-generated results and establish standards for its use in mathematics.

However, the nature of AI-generated proofs presents its own challenges. When OpenAI claimed its AI had solved the **Navier-Stokes problem**, a set of equations describing fluid flow, the resulting one hundred and sixty-six-page manuscript proved incredibly difficult for human mathematicians to decipher. James Maynard of the University of Oxford and Javier Gómez-Serrano of Brown University noted that the proof was "not written for humans," making it hard to extract human understanding from the AI's solution. This highlights a critical question: if AI can solve problems, but humans can't understand the solutions, what does that mean for the advancement of human knowledge?

### Peering Deeper into the Riemann Hypothesis

The **Riemann Hypothesis**, proposed in 1859, stands as one of the most important unsolved problems in mathematics, with a one-million-dollar prize awaiting its proof. It concerns the distribution of **prime numbers**, the fundamental building blocks of integers. At its heart is the **Riemann zeta-function**, a complex mathematical function. The hypothesis states that all its "non-trivial" zeros, the points where the function equals zero, lie on a specific vertical line called the **critical line**, where the real part of the complex number is exactly one-half. If true, this hypothesis would provide the best possible understanding of the error term in the **Prime Number Theorem**, which describes the asymptotic distribution of prime numbers.

New insights into this profound problem were recently shared in a guest post on mathematician Terence Tao's blog by Caroline Turnage-Butterbaugh, a mathematician from Carleton College. Her post, titled "Simple and critical zeros of the Riemann zeta-function," delves into the nature of these zeros. A "simple zero" means that the function crosses the zero axis cleanly, without touching it multiple times at the same point. A "critical zero" is one that lies on that crucial one-half line.

Turnage-Butterbaugh's work focuses on establishing **lower bounds on the proportion of nontrivial zeros of the Riemann zeta-function on the critical line**. This means determining what percentage of these important zeros are indeed located where the hypothesis predicts. For decades, human mathematicians had been stuck, only able to prove that approximately 41.6 percent of the non-trivial zeros lie on the critical line. However, recent advancements, some involving AI, have pushed this boundary significantly. Unconditional results now indicate that at least 67.25 percent of these zeros are both simple and critical, provided they are contained within a narrow vertical band centered on the critical line.

The fact that "great strides have recently been made on this problem, both with and without the use of AI" is particularly noteworthy, underscoring the collaborative and evolving nature of modern mathematical research. This includes "OpenAI's proof of the quasi-Riemann Hypothesis," which offers another form of evidence supporting the broader understanding of these zeros. Any progress on the Riemann Hypothesis is a monumental achievement, as it not only deepens our understanding of prime numbers but also has far-reaching implications across various branches of mathematics.
