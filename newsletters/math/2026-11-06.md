Greetings, fellow explorers of the mathematical universe!

This week, we're diving into some fascinating developments that span the theoretical frontiers of infinity, the practical implications of AI's mathematical underpinnings, and a new initiative to guide the future of our field. From understanding why AI can be so unpredictable to challenging long-held beliefs about the sizes of infinite sets, mathematicians are pushing boundaries and fostering crucial conversations about where our discipline is headed.

## Charting the Course for Math and AI

A significant new initiative, **Futures of Maths**, officially launched on November fifth, aiming to create a dynamic platform for research mathematicians to discuss the evolving relationship between mathematics and artificial intelligence. This "living platform" is designed to gather insights on how AI is transforming mathematical research and teaching, and to explore the future trajectory of the field.

The project is a guest post on renowned mathematician Terence Tao's blog, cross-posted from the **Proofs and Prompts** blog, a communal space for mathematicians to share their thoughts on AI. The team behind Futures of Maths includes Francesco Fournier-Facio, an Assistant Professor at Heriot-Watt University, Yara Kyrychenko, a Research Fellow at the University of Cambridge's St John's College, Jon Roozenbeek, an Associate Professor at the University of Cambridge and VU Amsterdam, and Julian Wykowski, a Postdoctoral Researcher at Karlsruhe Institute of Technology.

Participants in Futures of Maths are invited to rate short statements about mathematics and AI, and crucially, to propose their own statements, allowing the discussion to evolve with community input. To ensure a focus on active research perspectives, participation is open to individuals aged eighteen or older who have published at least one article in a mathematics journal indexed by zbMATH Open within the last five years. This initiative highlights the mathematical community's commitment to openly addressing the profound impact of AI and collectively shaping its future role in our work.

## The Surprising Equality of Infinities

One of the most mind-bending concepts in mathematics is the idea that there are different sizes of **infinity**. For over a century, mathematicians have grappled with questions about how these infinities relate. This week, new research has brought a surprising twist to this fundamental area.

Traditionally, we understand that the infinity of **real numbers** is "bigger" than the infinity of **natural numbers**, a concept famously demonstrated by Georg Cantor's diagonal argument. However, recent work by Maryanthe Malliaris of the University of Chicago and Saharon Shelah of the Hebrew University of Jerusalem and Rutgers University has resolved a seventy-year-old question about two specific types of infinity, known as 'p' and 't'. Contrary to long-held assumptions, their proof demonstrates that these two infinities are, in fact, equal in size.

This breakthrough challenges decades of conventional wisdom and opens up unexpected connections between the sizes of infinite sets and the complexity of mathematical theories. While the details involve advanced set theory, the core idea is that even within the established frameworks of mathematics, our understanding of infinity can still yield profound surprises. This ongoing exploration into the nature of infinity continues to expand the boundaries of what we thought was possible to prove and understand.

## Fractal Patterns Expose AI's Unpredictable Side

Engineers often strive for predictable systems, but new research from the University of Sydney reveals a fundamental mathematical reason why artificial intelligence systems can exhibit unpredictable behaviors, even when trained under nearly identical conditions. This discovery suggests that some forms of AI unpredictability are not simply due to engineering flaws or hardware inconsistencies, but are inherent to the underlying mathematics of how **neural networks** learn.

Professor Pulin Gong from the School of Physics and his PhD student Andrew Ly found that the "basins of attraction"—the regions in a model's training landscape that lead to a particular learned behavior—are organized as highly intricate **fractals**, specifically "riddled basins." Imagine a complex, infinitely detailed butterfly-like pattern where points leading to different outcomes are interwoven at every scale. This means that even minuscule differences in an AI model's initial setup can lead to vastly different final behaviors.

The implications are significant: simply increasing the precision of a network's starting conditions may do little to improve confidence in its eventual outcome. For instance, in one image-recognition network, making the starting settings ten billion times more precise produced no measurable improvement in reproducibility. This research, published in *Nature Machine Intelligence*, fundamentally shifts our understanding of AI capabilities by establishing mathematical boundaries to predictability. It suggests that rather than viewing unpredictability as a problem to be engineered away, it may be an intrinsic feature of many neural networks, prompting a need for new tools to measure this sensitivity and potentially redesign network architectures to mitigate instability.
