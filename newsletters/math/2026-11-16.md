This week, the world of mathematics is buzzing with news that highlights the rapidly evolving relationship between human ingenuity and artificial intelligence. We're seeing AI not just as a tool, but as a collaborator and even a distinct style of mathematical reasoner, pushing the boundaries of what's possible in research and discovery. ## AI's New Role: From Deciphering History to Redefining Research

Artificial intelligence is making waves by both unearthing lost mathematical treasures and prompting a re-evaluation of how mathematicians approach unsolved problems. In a remarkable development, the Finnish logician Jan von Plato at Helsinki University, with the assistance of the AI model **Claude**, has managed to decipher over one thousand pages of Kurt Gödel's densely written notebooks. Gödel, one of the twentieth century's most original thinkers, used an obsolete German shorthand called Gabelsberger, which made his unpublished work incredibly difficult to access. The AI's ability to process this material quickly revealed that Gödel had proven hundreds of theorems previously unknown to the mathematical community, demonstrating that he was far ahead of his time. This application of AI is not just about translation, but about unlocking historical insights that could influence contemporary research.

Looking forward, mathematician Michael Freedman of Harvard University's Center of Mathematical Sciences and Applications and Logical Intelligence, in an essay posted on November fourteenth, two thousand twenty-six, has explored how the profession should adapt to these powerful new tools. Freedman suggests that since automated systems are becoming adept at performing routine deductions and verifying proofs, human mathematicians should shift their focus to "deeply intractable questions" that currently stump machines. He specifically points to famous unsolved problems like the **Riemann Hypothesis**, the smooth four-dimensional **Poincaré conjecture**, and the **separation of P and NP** as areas where human intuition and creativity will remain paramount. This perspective suggests a future where AI handles the computational heavy lifting, freeing human minds for the most profound conceptual leaps.

## The Unique "Style" of AI in Mathematical Proofs

The way AI constructs mathematical proofs is proving to be fundamentally different from human approaches, a finding that has significant implications for how we understand and evaluate automated reasoning. On November twelfth, two thousand twenty-six, researchers from Beijing Normal University, Microsoft Research Asia, and Renmin University of China published an arXiv preprint detailing their study on AI mathematical proving styles.

The team tested frontier language models, including Anthropic's **Claude Opus 4.6**, **Claude Fable 5.1**, and OpenAI's **GPT-6 Astra**, across all four hundred sixty-five propositions of Euclid's Elements. They discovered that AI systems systematically bypass established intermediate theorems, preferring instead to reconstruct proofs directly from foundational axioms. This contrasts sharply with human mathematicians, who typically leverage a network of previously proven theorems to build new proofs. The study found that AI-generated proofs had a much denser network of citations to foundational elements, with models like Fable and GPT-6 averaging over four thousand nine hundred edges in their citation graphs, compared to one thousand eight hundred eighty-nine edges in the human baseline. This distinct "AI style" of proving raises important questions for mathematics departments, peer reviewers, and journal editors about how to evaluate and integrate these novel forms of automated reasoning into the established scientific process.

## A Best Paper Award for Advancing the Four Color Theorem

In a more traditional, yet equally significant, mathematical achievement, researchers from the National Institute of Informatics (NII) have been recognized for their work on the **Four Color Theorem**. Three papers co-authored by NII researchers were accepted at the sixty-seventh IEEE Symposium on Foundations of Computer Science, or **FOCS 2026**, a leading conference in theoretical computer science. Among these, a paper on the Four Color Theorem by Professor Ken-ichi Kawarabayashi of NII and his collaborators received the conference's **Best Paper Award**, acknowledging its outstanding contribution to the field.

The Four Color Theorem is a famous problem in graph theory, stating that any map can be colored using no more than four colors such that no two adjacent regions share the same color. It was famously the first major mathematical theorem to be proven with significant computer assistance in nineteen seventy-six, a method that initially sparked debate within the mathematical community. This new award-winning research indicates continued progress and deeper understanding of this fundamental theorem, showcasing ongoing human ingenuity in tackling complex combinatorial challenges, even those with a history of computational involvement.
