My Daily Clippings

The world of mathematics has been buzzing over the past few days, largely due to a remarkable surge in artificial intelligence-assisted breakthroughs. From tackling decades-old conjectures in number theory to shedding light on the fundamental limits of quantum computing, AI is proving to be an increasingly powerful, and at times unsettling, partner in mathematical discovery. These developments are not just incremental steps; they represent significant leaps that are reshaping how mathematicians approach complex problems and even how the field itself is perceived.

## AI Takes on Hilbert's Tenth Problem and Mazur's Conjecture

One of the most significant recent developments comes from Elliot Glazer, who, with substantial assistance from artificial intelligence, claims to have proven that the integers are **Diophantine over the rationals**. This highly technical result has profound implications for two long-standing open problems in mathematics: a widely believed conjecture by Barry Mazur and a different way of answering Hilbert's tenth problem for the rationals.

To understand why this is such a big deal, let's rewind a bit. In 1900, mathematician David Hilbert posed a list of 23 unsolved problems, with the tenth asking for a general algorithm to determine if a given **Diophantine equation** (an equation where only integer solutions are sought) has any integer solutions. Decades later, in 1970, Yuri Matiyasevich, building on the work of others, proved that no such general algorithm exists for integers. This was a monumental negative answer.

The question then shifted to whether such an algorithm exists for rational numbers, which include fractions. This has proven far more elusive. Barry Mazur, a prominent mathematician, conjectured that certain sets of rational solutions to Diophantine equations would have a limited, finite number of "connected components" when viewed geometrically. Glazer's AI-assisted proof, if verified, provides a negative answer to Mazur's conjecture, which in turn implies a negative answer to Hilbert's tenth problem for the rationals. This means that, just like with integers, there's no universal method to tell if a Diophantine equation has rational solutions.

The scale of Glazer's proof is staggering: it involves an equation of an "insanely high degree," with an upper bound of two raised to the power of two, then again raised to the power of seven in Knuth's up-arrow notation, and nearly 293 billion variables. This complexity underscores the critical role of AI in navigating such an immense mathematical landscape, a feat that would likely be impossible for humans alone.

## Quantum Computing's Limits Defined by AI

In another exciting development, a US startup called Physical Superintelligence, or PSI, announced on November sixth that its AI system, running an unreleased Anthropic model, has settled the **Aaronson-Ambainis conjecture**. This problem in **quantum complexity theory** had been open for nearly two decades, since computer scientists Scott Aaronson and Andris Ambainis first posed it around 2008.

The conjecture is crucial for understanding the true power of **quantum speedup**, the idea that quantum computers can solve certain problems exponentially faster than classical computers. In plain terms, the theorem shows that when a quantum computer is asked a yes-or-no question about random data with no hidden patterns, an ordinary computer can predict its answer for almost every input without needing exponentially more reads of the data.

This means that the significant commercial promise of quantum computing, which rests on these exponential speedups, only truly materializes when the data has a pattern to exploit, such as in Shor's factoring algorithm. For messy, unstructured data, quantum computers may not offer the dramatic advantage once hoped for. The proof generated by PSI's AI system was rigorously checked step-by-step using **Lean**, a software tool for formal verification of mathematical arguments. This combination of AI generation and formal verification is becoming a powerful new paradigm in mathematical research.

## AI's Expanding Reach: Solving Erdős Problems and Reshaping Research

The influence of AI in mathematics extends further, with an OpenAI model, specifically GPT-5.4 Pro, reportedly settling a 60-year-old problem posed by the legendary mathematician Paul Erdős. This problem, known as problem 1196, concerns **primitive sets**, which are collections of whole numbers where no member divides another evenly. The AI generated the initial proof in approximately 80 minutes. Beyond this, the same team, including researchers like Boris Alexeev and Kevin Barreto, used similar AI-driven methods to prove a second 1966 conjecture about divisibility chains and settle a revised Banks-Martin conjecture.

These rapid successes highlight a broader trend: AI is lowering the barriers to mathematical discovery. OpenAI itself announced on October sixth that its models had presented solutions to more than 300 long-standing unsolved problems across various areas of mathematics. This influx of AI-generated proofs is prompting mathematicians to rethink their roles. Frank Calegari, a math professor at the University of Chicago, suggests that the hiring process in academia will need to become "much more about personal interactions than a paper file," emphasizing a candidate's vision and communication skills over just a list of proofs.

While AI is incredibly effective at producing correct answers, the human element of understanding, reorganizing, connecting results, and generating new questions remains crucial. The "industrialization of mathematics" is upon us, bringing with it questions about verification, attribution, and transparency in an era where AI can autonomously generate complex proofs. The mathematical community is now grappling with how to best integrate these powerful AI tools while preserving the core human endeavor of seeking deep understanding.
