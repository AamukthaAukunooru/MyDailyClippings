Greetings, curious engineers!

This past weekend has delivered a seismic event in the world of mathematics, one that promises to reshape our understanding of discovery and the very nature of proof. A mathematician, working with an artificial intelligence, has claimed to solve the **Riemann Hypothesis**, a problem that has eluded the brightest minds for over a century and a half. This monumental announcement has not only sent ripples through the mathematical community but has also ignited a fervent debate about the evolving role of AI in scientific breakthroughs.

## The AI-Powered Breakthrough on Prime Numbers

On November twentieth, two thousand twenty-six, Levent Alpöge, a mathematician at the artificial intelligence company Anthropic, announced that he and Anthropic's AI model, **Claude**, had discovered a proof for the **Riemann Hypothesis**. This 167-year-old conjecture, first proposed by German mathematician Bernhard Riemann in 1859, is one of the seven **Millennium Prize Problems** designated by the Clay Mathematics Institute, each carrying a one million dollar prize for its solution. At its core, the Riemann Hypothesis offers a profound insight into the mysterious, unpredictable structure of **prime numbers**, those fundamental building blocks of arithmetic divisible only by one and themselves. Understanding its truth would unlock deeper secrets about how these numbers are distributed along the number line.

Alpöge's groundbreaking work also claims to resolve the even broader **Generalized Riemann Hypothesis**. Rather than a traditional paper, the proof was released as an astonishing seventeen million lines of **Lean code**, a formal programming language designed to allow computers to check mathematical arguments step by step. Alpöge noted that while he set the research direction, Claude played a significant role, particularly in the final stages, where the AI "far outpaced" him. This announcement came just a day after Anthropic's record-setting stock market debut, adding another layer of intrigue to the unfolding story.

## Rapid Verification and the Human Debate

The mathematical world wasted no time in scrutinizing this unprecedented claim. Just two days after Alpöge's announcement, on November twenty-second, two thousand twenty-six, the **Lean Focused Research Organization** and the **Mathlib Initiative** issued a joint statement certifying the correctness of Alpöge's proof. This rapid verification followed an intensive twenty-four-hour review of the massive Lean codebase, with MIT confirming that all one hundred seventy-five thousand, one hundred eighty-seven modules compiled with zero errors under Lean 4.33.1, satisfying every formal correctness criterion. This marks the first time a major unsolved mathematical conjecture has been proven using AI-assisted methods and then formally verified by machines.

However, the story doesn't end there. Hours after Alpöge's initial claim, Ravi Vakil, president of the American Mathematical Society and a professor at Stanford University, revealed a proposed proof of the Riemann Hypothesis by mathematician Alessandro Masullo, developed entirely without AI. Masullo's draft, a substantial nine hundred forty pages, is still in need of significant revisions and contains apparent errors, highlighting the immense difficulty of such a human-only endeavor.

This confluence of events has intensified a broader philosophical debate within the mathematical community about the role of artificial intelligence. Some mathematicians, like Erik Hoel, express a sense of unease, questioning the future of human mathematical pursuit when AI can tackle such profound problems. The **Association for Human Mathematics (AHM)** voiced concerns that this new model of proof production, driven by AI corporations, prioritizes technical validity over human accountability, clarity, and understanding. They argue that no human can reasonably claim to understand a seventeen-million-line proof.

Conversely, Fields Medalist Ngô Bảo Châu has offered a more optimistic perspective, suggesting that AI will not end mathematical research but rather promote its prosperity and democratize the field. He believes the focus for mathematicians will shift from building complex proof chains to asking insightful questions and exploring new avenues. Similarly, mathematician Ken Ono argues that human creativity, intuition, and the ability to formulate meaningful questions remain irreplaceable, emphasizing that understanding *why* a proof works is as crucial as the proof itself. This ongoing discussion underscores a pivotal moment, as mathematics grapples with what some have termed the "mathocalypse," a period of rapid change driven by AI's accelerating capabilities.
