What a week for mathematics! The field is abuzz with news that a long-standing, monumental problem, the **Riemann hypothesis**, has reportedly been proven with the help of artificial intelligence. This development, alongside other significant advancements, underscores a pivotal moment where AI is not just assisting, but fundamentally reshaping the landscape of mathematical discovery. From unraveling the mysteries of prime numbers to understanding the chaotic dance of fluids and even learning from single experiments, mathematicians are navigating a new era of collaboration with intelligent machines.

## The Riemann Hypothesis: A Human-AI Breakthrough and Its Aftermath

The biggest news rocking the mathematical world this week is the announcement that the **Riemann hypothesis**, a problem that has eluded mathematicians for one hundred and sixty-seven years, has reportedly been proven. On November twentieth, two thousand twenty-six, Turkish American mathematician Levent Alpöge, an employee at the AI company Anthropic, released a formal proof of this critical conjecture, developed with the assistance of Anthropic's unreleased frontier AI model, Claude.

The Riemann hypothesis is considered one of the most important open problems in mathematics, often referred to as a "sacred object" in the field. It deals with the distribution of **prime numbers**, those fundamental building blocks of arithmetic that are only divisible by one and themselves. Intuitively, the hypothesis provides a deep insight into how these primes are scattered among all numbers, offering the tightest possible bounds for the error in estimating their count. Its truth would have profound implications across number theory and other mathematical domains, influencing our understanding of many arithmetic functions.

What makes this achievement particularly striking, and somewhat controversial, is that the proof, formalized in the **Lean proof assistant**, consists of an astonishing one hundred seventy-five thousand, one hundred eighty-seven files and approximately seventeen million lines. While its correctness has been independently verified by groups like Lean FRO and the Mathlib Initiative, a complete human-readable account of the proof is not yet available. This raises questions about the nature of mathematical understanding and the role of human intuition when AI can produce proofs that are beyond immediate human comprehension.

## AI's Shifting Role in Mathematical Discovery

The proof of the Riemann hypothesis is a powerful illustration of a broader trend: artificial intelligence is rapidly changing how mathematicians discover patterns, build proofs, and tackle difficult problems. Throughout two thousand twenty-six, cutting-edge AI models from companies like OpenAI and Anthropic have made stunning progress on hundreds of open problems that have baffled mathematicians for decades, and even centuries. This includes breakthroughs on conjectures like the Erdős unit distance conjecture and the Jacobian conjecture.

This surge in AI-driven mathematical discovery has led to what some describe as an "identity crisis" for the field. While AI can suggest mathematical approaches and formalize proofs, the human element of evaluating results and understanding their significance remains central. Fields Medalist Terence Tao, along with twenty-five other Fields medalists, issued a declaration in September two thousand twenty-six, stating that the goals of AI companies and the mathematical community are "severely misaligned." They warn that the rapid production of "true/false" statements by AI could "destroy fertile ground instead of breathing life into new ideas," emphasizing the importance of human understanding and the potential for issues around attribution and plagiarism. The challenge now is to adapt to these changes, finding ways to leverage AI's power while preserving the human-centric values of mathematical reasoning and insight.

## Unraveling Fluid Dynamics: Singularity Formation in Euler Equations

Beyond the realm of pure number theory, significant progress is also being made in understanding the complex behavior of fluids. Matei P. Coiculescu, along with Levent Alpöge and Tristan Buckmaster, has contributed to a new understanding of **finite-time singularity formation** for the **three-dimensional Euler equations**. These equations are fundamental to fluid dynamics, describing the motion of an **inviscid fluid**, meaning a fluid without internal friction or viscosity.

While simplified, the Euler equations are crucial for modeling low-viscosity fluids like water and alcohol, and for understanding phenomena such as the lift generated by an airplane wing. The concept of a **singularity** in this context refers to a point where the fluid's properties, such as its velocity or vorticity, become infinite within a finite amount of time. Such singularities are intimately linked to the phenomenon of **turbulence**, one of the most challenging unsolved problems in classical physics. The new work constructs smooth initial conditions for these equations that lead to such a finite-time blow-up, with the velocity remaining bounded while the **vorticity magnitude** at the origin tends to infinity. This provides critical insights into the mathematical behavior of fluid flows and helps mathematicians and engineers better grasp the conditions under which chaotic fluid behavior can emerge.

## Learning from a Single Experiment: A Mathematical Proof

Finally, a fascinating development from Cornell researchers offers a new mathematical foundation for learning about physical systems from remarkably little data. They have provided a mathematical proof demonstrating that in certain scenarios, a single experiment can be enough for scientists to learn the underlying equations governing a physical system.

This "one random shot or none" principle, as described by the researchers, is a powerful insight for situations where experiments are costly, destructive, or difficult to repeat. Co-author Alex Townsend, an associate professor of mathematics at Cornell, explains that their theory suggests that for a broad class of models, if any single experiment can identify the underlying equations, then almost any randomly chosen experiment will do the trick. This is a significant simplification in experimental design, moving away from the need to meticulously choose the "perfect" experiment. The team's work, published in the *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences*, proves a **zero-one law**, meaning that either the system parameters can be identified with one data input, or it's impossible to identify them due to a fundamental problem with the setup – there's no middle ground. This has broad applicability, from understanding how parts of a bridge are displaced under stress to modeling chemical reactions.
