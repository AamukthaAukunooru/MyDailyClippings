Today's mathematical landscape is buzzing with developments that could reshape how we approach some of the deepest questions in the field. We're seeing a significant stride in the formal verification of a proof for the **Generalized Riemann Hypothesis**, a problem with profound implications across number theory. Meanwhile, the growing prowess of **artificial intelligence** in tackling complex mathematical challenges is prompting a serious re-evaluation of the human role in discovery. And in the realm of fluid dynamics, new insights are emerging regarding the notoriously difficult **Euler equations**, pushing our understanding of turbulence and chaos.

## A Formally Verified Proof for the Generalized Riemann Hypothesis

This is arguably the biggest news in mathematics: a potential breakthrough on one of the most important unsolved problems. The **Generalized Riemann Hypothesis** (GRH) is an extension of the famous Riemann Hypothesis, a cornerstone of modern number theory and one of the Millennium Prize Problems, carrying a one million dollar prize for its proof. At its heart, the GRH is about the distribution of **prime numbers**, the fundamental building blocks of arithmetic. If proven true, it would provide a deeper understanding of how primes are scattered among the integers, impacting fields from cryptography to physics. Specifically, it states that all non-trivial zeros of the Riemann zeta function, and its generalizations, lie on a specific vertical line in the complex plane, known as the **critical line**. This implies the "tightest possible bound for the error of the logarithmic integral in estimating the number of primes".

On November twentieth, two thousand twenty-six, Levent Alpöge, a mathematician at the AI company Anthropic, announced a formal proof of the Riemann Hypothesis and the Generalized Riemann Hypothesis, developed with the assistance of Anthropic's AI model, Claude. The proof is formalized in **Lean**, a **proof assistant** software system that allows mathematicians to write down statements and proofs in a highly formal, unambiguous language, which the system then rigorously checks for logical correctness. This monumental work consists of approximately seventeen million lines of Lean code across one hundred seventy-five thousand, one hundred eighty-seven files.

A day after its release, independent verification was announced by the Lean Focused Research Organization and the Mathlib Initiative, stating they have "full confidence" in the proof's correctness within the Lean system. Andrew Sutherland, a mathematician at MIT, also ran checks confirming the Lean source compiles without errors and the theorems depend only on standard axioms.

However, the nature of this AI-assisted proof raises new questions for the mathematical community. While the Lean system has verified its logical steps, a complete human-readable account of the proof is not yet available. Alpöge himself noted that he is "presenting the world with a complete argument before having mastered a massive neighbourhood around its mathematics," and an attempt to render the argument in prose resulted in over two hundred fifty thousand pages. The Clay Mathematics Institute still lists the Riemann Hypothesis as unsolved. This has led to some criticism, with the Association for Human Mathematics reportedly calling the proof "little more than publicity" for AI labs, arguing that no human could reasonably understand it. This development highlights a fascinating tension between machine-verified certainty and human comprehension in the pursuit of mathematical truth.

## AI's Growing Footprint in Mathematical Discovery

The year two thousand twenty-six is rapidly becoming a pivotal moment for mathematics, as **artificial intelligence** models demonstrate unprecedented capabilities in solving complex problems, leading to what some describe as an "identity crisis" for the field. Cutting-edge AI models from companies like OpenAI and Anthropic have made "stunning progress on hundreds of open problems" that have baffled mathematicians for decades, and even centuries.

For engineers, the idea of AI automating problem-solving might seem like a natural progression, offering tools for automated theorem proving, conjecture generation, and symbolic computation. Indeed, OpenAI recently announced a solution to the Navier-Stokes problem, another Millennium Prize Problem, and released over seven hundred papers claiming solutions, proofs, or counter-examples to three hundred seventy-two open problems at the forefront of mathematical research. These AI systems, particularly large language models, are trained on the entire human corpus of mathematical literature and can deploy "AI agents" to work collaboratively on problems, as seen with the ten thousand agents OpenAI used for Navier-Stokes.

However, this rapid advancement has sparked significant debate within the mathematical community. Terence Tao, a Fields medallist, along with twenty-five other Fields medallists, issued a declaration stating that "the goals of the AI companies and the goals of the mathematical community are severely misaligned". Tao likened the actions of AI companies to "dumping carcasses of raw meat onto our table and saying 'here you go, I solved your food problem'," suggesting that while solutions are provided, the deeper understanding and the human process of discovery are bypassed. Hugo Duminil-Copin, another Fields medallist, expressed concern that the sheer scale of AI-generated results could "annihilate my field of research" and "dismantle" the discipline of mathematics.

Mathematicians care deeply about *why* a result is true, not just *that* it is true. The process of constructing a proof often reveals profound connections and opens new avenues for exploration. If AI provides answers without this explanatory power, or if its reasoning is too complex for humans to fully grasp, it challenges the very nature of mathematical research and the value of human intuition. This ongoing discussion is prompting a critical re-evaluation of the future role of human mathematicians in a world where machines can increasingly outperform them in specific problem-solving tasks.

## New Insights into the Chaotic Dance of Fluids

Fluid dynamics, the study of how liquids and gases move, is a field rich with complex mathematical challenges that directly impact engineering applications. Among the most famous are the **Euler equations**, which describe the motion of an ideal, inviscid fluid, meaning a fluid with no internal friction. While a simplification, these equations are fundamental to understanding phenomena from ocean currents to airflow over airplane wings.

One of the most vexing questions surrounding the three-dimensional Euler equations is whether they can develop **finite-time singularities**. Imagine a smooth, well-behaved flow of water. A singularity would mean that at a specific point in space and time, some property of the fluid, like its velocity or pressure, becomes infinitely large. This would represent a breakdown in the smooth behavior of the fluid and is mathematically equivalent to the formation of extreme turbulence or a "blow-up". The existence, or non-existence, of such singularities has profound implications for our understanding of turbulence, a phenomenon that remains one of the greatest unsolved problems in classical physics.

Matei P. Coiculescu, a Simons Junior Fellow at the Courant Institute at New York University, whose research interests include mathematical fluid dynamics, recently presented new work on this problem. His findings, detailed in a guest post on Terence Tao's blog, indicate progress on the finite-time singularity formation for the three-dimensional Euler equations. Crucially, this work, undertaken with Laurent Alpöge and Tristan Buckmaster, involved "heavy AI assistance" and was formalized in Lean. The approach involved numerically building an approximate solution and then using rigorous perturbation theory to convert it into an exact solution.

This development is a significant step towards understanding the more complex Navier-Stokes equations, another Millennium Prize Problem that governs viscous fluids. While previous work, such as that by Thomas Hou and Guo Luo in two thousand fourteen, provided numerical evidence for a three-dimensional Euler blowup in a cylindrical boundary, and a proof by Hou and Jiajie Chen in two thousand twenty-two for that specific scenario, Coiculescu's work appears to address the problem in a more general setting, potentially for "unforced" flows in free space. Engineers, who constantly grapple with fluid behavior in design and analysis, will appreciate the pursuit of these fundamental insights into the very nature of fluid motion and the onset of chaos.
