Today's "My Daily Clippings" brings a fascinating, and perhaps unsettling, look at the accelerating pace of mathematical discovery, largely driven by artificial intelligence. We'll delve into the community's reaction to these powerful new tools, explore a bold challenge to our understanding of the universe's expansion, and marvel at how a century-old problem is finally being "seen" in the lab. It's a day where the very foundations of mathematical practice and cosmic physics are being re-examined.

## The AI Tsunami and the Call for "Maths 2.0"

The mathematical world is grappling with a profound shift, as artificial intelligence models are not just assisting, but actively generating solutions to long-standing problems at an unprecedented scale. OpenAI recently announced a deluge of new findings, including claimed proofs for 722 problems across more than 350 subfields. Among these are significant conjectures like **Sogge's local smoothing conjecture**, the **Kakeya conjecture**, and the **Stein restriction conjecture**, all resolved "in all dimensions". Perhaps most strikingly, OpenAI has put forward a claimed proof for the **Navier-Stokes problem**, one of the seven Millennium Prize problems, whose solution carries a one million dollar award.

This explosion of AI-generated mathematics has prompted a strong reaction from the community. Fields Medallist Terence Tao, a renowned mathematician at UCLA, has emerged as a leading voice calling for a "Maths 2.0" era. Tao, who was initially an enthusiastic user of AI assistants, now urges caution, stating, "We have to slow down. It's insane, the pace". His concern stems from the sheer volume and complexity of these AI-generated proofs. For instance, the claimed Navier-Stokes proof spans 165 pages, produced by approximately ten thousand AI agents over 88 hours, yet "no expert I've talked to has actually understood the whole argument". This raises fundamental questions about what constitutes a "proof" if it cannot be fully comprehended by human experts.

The rapid advancements are also causing significant unease among early-career mathematicians. A survey conducted by MIT researchers found that nearly 76 percent of graduate students and postdoctoral scholars felt less motivated to do research and were increasingly unlikely to stay in academic mathematics due to AI developments. This sentiment highlights an existential crisis within the field, as the traditional path of incremental human discovery is being overshadowed. In response, the Simons Foundation, Anthropic, and OpenAI have collectively pledged 15 million dollars in funding and resources to help the mathematics community evaluate these AI results and develop shared standards for this new landscape. Frank Calegari, a number theorist at the University of Chicago, has also contributed to the discussion on Tao's blog, proposing six rules for adapting math careers in this AI-driven future, touching on aspects like hiring and journal publications.

Beyond pure mathematics, OpenAI also reported a disproof of the **Exponential Time Hypothesis (ETH)** in computer science, a long-standing assumption about the inherent difficulty of certain computational problems. If confirmed, this could have profound implications for finding more efficient algorithms across various fields, from databases to computational biology.

## Rethinking the Universe: A Challenge to Dark Energy

In a development that could shake the foundations of cosmology, mathematicians at the University of California, Davis, have published research in the *Proceedings of the Royal Society A* suggesting that the universe's accelerating expansion might not require the mysterious force known as **dark energy**. For decades, dark energy has been the leading explanation for why the universe is expanding at an ever-increasing rate, a phenomenon observed through distant supernovae.

The UC Davis team argues that there's a fundamental instability in the standard model of the expanding universe. By re-examining the **Einstein-Euler equations**, which combine Einstein's theory of general relativity with equations describing fluid behavior, they found that cosmic acceleration could emerge naturally from Einstein's original theory of gravity. Their calculations indicate that the conventional mathematical descriptions of the expanding universe, known as **Friedmann spacetimes**, are inherently unstable to disturbances at both small and large scales. According to researcher Blake Temple, "Unstable solutions in physics and science are considered not physical. You'll never observe them in nature". This instability, they propose, could account for the observed acceleration without needing to invoke dark energy, potentially challenging the widely accepted **Lambda-cold dark matter model** of the universe's evolution. This work could lead to a significant re-evaluation of our cosmic models and the very nature of space-time.

## Visualizing a Century-Old Problem: The Thomson Problem

Scientists have achieved a remarkable feat by making a mathematical problem, first posed over 120 years ago, visible for the first time in a laboratory setting. The **Thomson problem**, originating from Nobel laureate J. J. Thomson's 1904 attempts to understand atomic structure, asks how identical electrical charges, confined to the surface of a sphere, will arrange themselves to minimize their total energy. While simple to state, finding a general formula for the lowest-energy arrangement for an arbitrary number of charges has remained elusive. Its significance was further highlighted when mathematician Stephen Smale included a closely related problem in his list of 18 major mathematical challenges for the 21st century.

A collaborative research team, including Professor Shengfu Yang at the University of Leicester, Professor Paul Scheier at the University of Innsbruck, and experts from the University of Cambridge, has developed a method to experimentally reveal these charge arrangements. They used **superfluid helium nanodroplets** as tiny spherical containers for electrical charges, allowing them to observe how these charges organize on the surface. This breakthrough transforms an abstract optimization problem into a physical system that can be directly investigated. The ability to physically realize these configurations means researchers can now observe patterns that might challenge existing mathematical theories, opening new avenues for understanding this classic problem and its connections between mathematics, physics, and nanoscale science.
