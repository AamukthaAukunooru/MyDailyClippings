My Daily Clippings

The world of mathematics is currently navigating a fascinating crossroads, with both technological advancements and a re-evaluation of core research values shaping its future. This week, we're looking at two significant discussions: the profound impact of artificial intelligence on mathematical discovery, and the launch of a new journal aiming to redefine how mathematical contributions are valued and verified. These developments highlight a community grappling with how to best leverage new tools while preserving the essence of human understanding and rigorous inquiry.

## The AI Revolution and Mathematics

A thought-provoking discussion by Francesco Maggi, Chair of Mathematics at the University of Texas at Austin, has sparked conversation about whether we are heading for a "golden age" or a "dark age" for mathematics and science, driven by the rise of **Large Language Models**, or LLMs. Maggi's guest post on Terence Tao's blog, published on October eighteenth, two thousand twenty-six, explores the remarkable ability of LLMs to generate mathematical proofs.

In the "golden age" scenario, these AI tools would accelerate discovery, empowering a new generation of scientists and fostering an open, collaborative research environment. Imagine LLMs as incredibly powerful assistants, capable of sifting through vast amounts of information and suggesting potential proof pathways at speeds unimaginable to humans. However, Maggi also warns of a "dark age," where scientific output might increase dramatically in the short term, but independent human scientific activity could weaken. In this darker future, the production of new knowledge might become concentrated in a few hands, and crucially, this knowledge could become "alien to human understanding".

Mathematicians care deeply about this because it touches the very core of their discipline: understanding. While LLMs excel at writing proofs in formal systems like **Lean**, a proof assistant that allows computers to verify arguments, the sheer length of these machine-generated proofs, sometimes hundreds of thousands of lines, makes their natural language translations messy and difficult for humans to grasp. Maggi emphasizes that proofs without human understanding are insufficient, arguing that society will need *more* mathematicians, not fewer, to interpret and assimilate these complex, machine-generated results. The concern isn't just about efficiency, but about maintaining human creativity, intellectual independence, and preventing the control of this fundamental research infrastructure from falling into private hands.

## A New Voice in Mathematical Publishing

In a related and equally significant development, Shaoming Guo, a chair professor at the Chern Institute of Mathematics, Nankai University, along with several colleagues, is preparing to launch a new mathematics journal. Announced on Terence Tao's blog on October eighteenth, two thousand twenty-six, this initiative aims to reconsider long-standing conventions in mathematical publishing, especially as new tools like LLMs change how mathematics is developed and shared.

The new journal will uphold two core values that stand out from traditional publishing models. First, it will prioritize **understanding over being first**. This means placing greater value on how deeply a paper enhances our comprehension of its results and proofs, rather than simply being the initial publication of those findings. This encourages thoroughness, clarity, and deep insight, fostering a culture of profound mathematical exploration.

Second, and directly addressing the challenges posed by AI, the journal will ensure **rigor through formal verification**. Given the increasing use of LLMs to develop mathematical proofs, there's a growing concern about subtle, hard-to-detect errors. To be accepted, papers submitted to this new journal will require their proofs to be formally verified in **Lean**. This commitment to formal verification is a proactive step to maintain the highest standards of accuracy and reliability in an era where machine assistance is becoming commonplace.

Mathematicians are paying close attention because this new journal represents a forward-thinking approach to adapting publishing to the age of AI. It's about setting new benchmarks for quality and trustworthiness, ensuring that mathematical knowledge remains robust and accessible to human understanding, even as the methods of its creation evolve.
