## The One-Shot Stock Market Challenge

Imagine you're trying to make a quick buck in the stock market. You're given a list of stock prices for a company, one price for each day. Your goal is simple: you want to buy the stock on one day and sell it on a later day to maximize your profit. You can only make *one* transaction, meaning one buy and one sell. If it's impossible to make any profit, you should aim for zero.

This isn't about complex financial models or predicting the future. It's about looking at past data and finding the best single opportunity. This problem, known as **Best Time to Buy and Sell Stock**, is a classic introduction to data structures and algorithms for a good reason. It teaches us a fundamental pattern for optimizing solutions.

Let's take a small example. Suppose the stock prices over six days were: seven dollars, one dollar, five dollars, three dollars, six dollars, and four dollars. That's `[7, 1, 5, 3, 6, 4]`. What's the biggest profit you could make with just one buy and one sell?

## The Brute Force Approach: Trying Every Option

When faced with a problem like this, a natural first thought is to try every single possibility. This is often called the **brute force** approach.

How would that work here? You could pick any day as your buying day. Then, for each buying day, you'd consider every subsequent day as a potential selling day. You'd calculate the profit for each buy-sell pair and keep track of the largest profit you find.

Let's trace this with our example: `[7, 1, 5, 3, 6, 4]`.

*   **If you buy on Day 1 (price 7):**
    *   Sell on Day 2 (price 1): Profit = 1 - 7 = -6 (a loss)
    *   Sell on Day 3 (price 5): Profit = 5 - 7 = -2
    *   Sell on Day 4 (price 3): Profit = 3 - 7 = -4
    *   Sell on Day 5 (price 6): Profit = 6 - 7 = -1
    *   Sell on Day 6 (price 4): Profit = 4 - 7 = -3
*   **If you buy on Day 2 (price 1):**
    *   Sell on Day 3 (price 5): Profit = 5 - 1 = 4
    *   Sell on Day 4 (price 3): Profit = 3 - 1 = 2
    *   Sell on Day 5 (price 6): Profit = 6 - 1 = 5
    *   Sell on Day 6 (price 4): Profit = 4 - 1 = 3
*   **If you buy on Day 3 (price 5):**
    *   Sell on Day 4 (price 3): Profit = 3 - 5 = -2
    *   Sell on Day 5 (price 6): Profit = 6 - 5 = 1
    *   Sell on Day 6 (price 4): Profit = 4 - 5 = -1
*   And so on, until you consider buying on Day 5. You can't buy on Day 6 because there are no subsequent days to sell.

After checking all these pairs, you'd find the maximum profit is five dollars, by buying on Day 2 (price one dollar) and selling on Day 5 (price six dollars).

This approach works, but it's inefficient. If there are `N` days, for the first day, you check `N-1` selling days. For the second day, `N-2` selling days, and so on. This adds up to roughly `N` times `N` operations, which we call **O(N-squared)** time complexity. For a list of, say, one hundred thousand prices, `N-squared` would be ten billion operations, far too slow for a typical computer to calculate in a reasonable amount of time. We need a smarter way.

## The Key Insight: Tracking the Minimum

The problem asks us to find the maximum difference between two numbers in an array, where the second number appears *after* the first. The brute force approach tries to find both the optimal buy and optimal sell day simultaneously. But what if we simplify?

The key insight here is that to maximize profit, we always want to sell at the highest possible price *after* buying at the lowest possible price. As we iterate through the prices day by day, we don't need to look back at *all* previous prices. We only need to know the **lowest price encountered so far** up to the current day.

Think of it this way: if you're standing on a particular day, and you're considering selling the stock today, what's the best day you could have bought it? It's simply the day with the lowest price *before or on* your current day.

This leads to a **one-pass** or **greedy** approach. We'll iterate through the prices just once. As we go, we'll keep track of two things:
1.  The **minimum price** we've seen from the beginning of the list up to the current day.
2.  The **maximum profit** we've been able to achieve so far.

Every time we look at a new day's price, we do two things:
1.  We check if this new price is lower than our current minimum price. If it is, we update our minimum price, because this new lower price now becomes the best potential buying point for any future sales.
2.  We calculate the potential profit if we were to sell *today* using our current minimum price. This potential profit is `current_price - minimum_price_seen_so_far`. We then compare this potential profit with our overall maximum profit found so far and update if it's higher.

This strategy is greedy because at each step, we're making the locally optimal choice: always tracking the lowest buy price and the highest profit possible with that lowest buy price. This local optimality leads to the global optimal solution.

## Tracing the Optimal Approach Step-by-Step

Let's re-examine our example: `[7, 1, 5, 3, 6, 4]`.

We'll initialize two variables:
*   `min_price`: We'll set this to a very large number, or the first price, to ensure any initial price updates it. Let's use the first price, seven.
*   `max_profit`: We'll set this to zero, as we can't have a negative profit (if no profit is possible, we return zero).

Now, let's go through the prices day by day:

*   **Day 1: Price is 7**
    *   `min_price` is 7.
    *   `max_profit` is 0.
    *   (No calculation yet, as we need at least one buy and one sell day.)

*   **Day 2: Price is 1**
    *   Is 1 less than `min_price` (which is 7)? Yes. Update `min_price` to 1.
    *   Calculate potential profit if we sell today: `current_price (1) - min_price (1) = 0`.
    *   Is 0 greater than `max_profit` (which is 0)? No. `max_profit` remains 0.
    *   Current state: `min_price = 1`, `max_profit = 0`.

*   **Day 3: Price is 5**
    *   Is 5 less than `min_price` (which is 1)? No. `min_price` remains 1.
    *   Calculate potential profit if we sell today: `current_price (5) - min_price (1) = 4`.
    *   Is 4 greater than `max_profit` (which is 0)? Yes. Update `max_profit` to 4.
    *   Current state: `min_price = 1`, `max_profit = 4`.

*   **Day 4: Price is 3**
    *   Is 3 less than `min_price` (which is 1)? No. `min_price` remains 1.
    *   Calculate potential profit if we sell today: `current_price (3) - min_price (1) = 2`.
    *   Is 2 greater than `max_profit` (which is 4)? No. `max_profit` remains 4.
    *   Current state: `min_price = 1`, `max_profit = 4`.

*   **Day 5: Price is 6**
    *   Is 6 less than `min_price` (which is 1)? No. `min_price` remains 1.
    *   Calculate potential profit if we sell today: `current_price (6) - min_price (1) = 5`.
    *   Is 5 greater than `max_profit` (which is 4)? Yes. Update `max_profit` to 5.
    *   Current state: `min_price = 1`, `max_profit = 5`.

*   **Day 6: Price is 4**
    *   Is 4 less than `min_price` (which is 1)? No. `min_price` remains 1.
    *   Calculate potential profit if we sell today: `current_price (4) - min_price (1) = 3`.
    *   Is 3 greater than `max_profit` (which is 5)? No. `max_profit` remains 5.
    *   Current state: `min_price = 1`, `max_profit = 5`.

We've reached the end of the list. The final `max_profit` is five dollars. This matches the result from our brute force approach, but we achieved it much more efficiently.

## Efficiency: Time and Space Complexity

Let's analyze the efficiency of this one-pass approach.

**Time Complexity:** We iterate through the list of prices exactly once. For each price, we perform a constant number of operations: one comparison to update `min_price`, one subtraction, and one comparison to update `max_profit`. Because the number of operations scales directly with the number of prices `N`, the time complexity is **O(N)**, or "linear time." This is a huge improvement over the O(N-squared) brute force. For one hundred thousand prices, this would be roughly one hundred thousand operations, which is almost instantaneous.

**Space Complexity:** We only used a few variables to store `min_price` and `max_profit`. The amount of memory used does not depend on the number of prices `N`. Therefore, the space complexity is **O(1)**, or "constant space." This is as efficient as it gets for memory usage.

## Common Misconceptions and Edge Cases

*   **Selling Before Buying:** The problem explicitly states you must buy before you sell. Our one-pass approach naturally handles this because `min_price` always reflects a price from a day *on or before* the current day.
*   **No Profit Possible:** What if the prices are always decreasing, like `[7, 6, 4, 3, 1]`?
    *   `min_price` would keep updating to the current price.
    *   The calculated profit `current_price - min_price` would always be zero (if `current_price` is the new `min_price`) or negative.
    *   Since `max_profit` is initialized to zero and only updated if a *greater* profit is found, it will correctly remain zero. This is the desired outcome.
*   **Empty or Single-Day Price List:** If the input list is empty or has only one price, you can't make a transaction. The problem constraints usually specify this, but if not, the algorithm would typically handle it by returning the initialized `max_profit` of zero, which is correct.

## Why This Pattern Matters

This problem is a fantastic introduction to a powerful problem-solving pattern: the **one-pass greedy algorithm**. It teaches you to look for opportunities to simplify a problem by maintaining key information as you iterate through data. Instead of re-calculating or re-examining past data, you carry forward the essential summary of what you've seen so far.

The idea of keeping track of a "minimum so far" or "maximum so far" is very common. It's a foundational concept that helps optimize many problems from quadratic to linear time. It's also a good example of how a simple change in perspective can drastically improve performance.

## Beyond One Transaction: Follow-up Questions and Related Problems

Interviewers often use this problem as a warm-up, then ask follow-up questions to see how you adapt your thinking:

*   **Best Time to Buy and Sell Stock II (LeetCode 122):** What if you could make *multiple* transactions? This changes the strategy significantly, as you can buy and sell as many times as you like, but you must sell before buying again. The greedy approach here is to capture every small profit.
*   **Best Time to Buy and Sell Stock III (LeetCode 123):** What if you could make at most *two* transactions? This is much harder and often involves dynamic programming.
*   **Best Time to Buy and Sell Stock with Cooldown (LeetCode 309) or Transaction Fee (LeetCode 712):** These add more constraints, requiring more sophisticated dynamic programming solutions.

The core idea of maintaining a "best so far" value is also seen in other problems:

*   **Maximum Subarray (LeetCode 53):** Given an array of numbers, find the contiguous subarray (a sequence of numbers next to each other) which has the largest sum. This problem uses a similar greedy approach, keeping track of the current subarray sum and the maximum subarray sum found so far.
*   **Product of Array Except Self (LeetCode 238):** Given an array of numbers, return an array `answer` such that `answer[i]` is equal to the product of all the elements of `nums` except `nums[i]`. This often involves two passes, one from left to right and one from right to left, accumulating products.

## Recap

The "Best Time to Buy and Sell Stock" problem asks for the maximum profit from a single buy and sell transaction given daily stock prices. While a brute force approach checks every buy-sell pair in O(N-squared) time, the optimal solution uses a **one-pass greedy algorithm**. By tracking the **minimum price seen so far** and the **maximum profit achieved**, we can find the answer in highly efficient O(N) time and O(1) space. This problem is a fundamental lesson in optimizing solutions by maintaining essential information as you iterate through data.
