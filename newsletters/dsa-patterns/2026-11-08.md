Have you ever looked at a set of stairs and wondered, "How many different ways can I get to the top?" It sounds like a silly question, right? You just walk up. But what if you had a rule: you can only take one step at a time, or two steps at a time? Now, the question gets a little more interesting. This isn't just a quirky thought experiment; it's the core of a classic coding interview problem called "Climbing Stairs," and it's a fantastic introduction to a powerful problem-solving technique in computer science.

## The Problem: Reaching the Top

Let's formalize our staircase dilemma. You are climbing a staircase. It takes `n` steps to reach the very top. Each time, you can either climb one step or two steps. The goal is to figure out how many distinct ways you can climb to the top.

Let's try a small example. Imagine the staircase has three steps, so `n` equals three.
How many ways can we reach the third step?
One way is to take one step, then another one step, then a final one step. That's `one, one, one`.
Another way is to take one step, then two steps. That's `one, two`.
And a third way is to take two steps, then one step. That's `two, one`.
Are there any other ways? If you try to think of them, you'll find there aren't. So, for three steps, there are three distinct ways.

What about two steps? `n` equals two.
You can take one step, then one step: `one, one`.
Or you can take two steps at once: `two`.
That's two ways.

And for just one step? `n` equals one.
You can only take one step: `one`.
That's one way.

Notice a pattern emerging? One step gives one way, two steps gives two ways, three steps gives three ways. It almost seems too simple, but let's dig deeper into how we arrive at these numbers.

## The Brute Force Approach: Too Many Choices

When faced with a problem like this, a natural first thought is to try every possible combination. This is often called a **brute force** approach. Since we can choose to take either one or two steps at each point, this sounds like a job for **recursion**.

Let's define a function, let's call it `ways(k)`, which calculates the number of ways to reach step `k`.
If we are at step `k`, and we just took one step, it means we were previously at step `k-1`.
If we are at step `k`, and we just took two steps, it means we were previously at step `k-2`.
So, the total number of ways to reach step `k` is the sum of the ways to reach `k-1` and the ways to reach `k-2`.
This gives us a recursive relationship: `ways(k) = ways(k-1) + ways(k-2)`.

We also need **base cases** for our recursion.
For `ways(1)`, there's only one way: take one step. So, `ways(1) = 1`.
For `ways(2)`, there are two ways: one-one or two. So, `ways(2) = 2`.

Now let's trace this for `n` equals four steps.
`ways(4)` would be `ways(3) + ways(2)`.
To find `ways(3)`, we need `ways(2) + ways(1)`.
To find `ways(2)`, we already know it's two.
To find `ways(1)`, we already know it's one.

So, `ways(3) = ways(2) + ways(1) = 2 + 1 = 3`.
Then, `ways(4) = ways(3) + ways(2) = 3 + 2 = 5`.
This matches our earlier observation that for four steps, there are five ways.

This recursive approach works, but it's inefficient. Imagine a tree of function calls. To calculate `ways(5)`, we need `ways(4)` and `ways(3)`. Both of these branches will then need `ways(2)` and `ways(1)`. We end up recalculating the number of ways for the same intermediate steps many, many times. This redundant computation makes the brute force recursive solution very slow, especially for larger `n`. Its time complexity is exponential, roughly proportional to two raised to the power of `n`, which quickly becomes impractical.

## The Key Insight: Dynamic Programming and Fibonacci

The inefficiency of our brute force recursion comes from **overlapping subproblems**: we're solving the same smaller problems repeatedly. This is a tell-tale sign that we can use **Dynamic Programming**. Dynamic Programming is an optimization technique that stores the results of expensive function calls and returns the cached result when the same inputs occur again. It often involves building up solutions to larger problems from solutions to smaller, already solved problems.

If you look closely at the recurrence relation we found, `ways(k) = ways(k-1) + ways(k-2)`, it's exactly the definition of the **Fibonacci sequence**! The Fibonacci sequence starts with zero, one, then each subsequent number is the sum of the two preceding ones: zero, one, one, two, three, five, eight, thirteen, and so on.

Our base cases are slightly different from the standard Fibonacci sequence (where `fib(0)=0, fib(1)=1`), but the pattern is identical. For `Climbing Stairs`, `ways(1)=1` and `ways(2)=2`. If we map this to Fibonacci, `ways(n)` corresponds to `fib(n+1)` in the standard sequence (where `fib(1)=1, fib(2)=1, fib(3)=2, fib(4)=3, fib(5)=5`, etc.). The important thing is the recurrence relation.

## The Optimal Approach: Bottom-Up Dynamic Programming

Instead of starting from `n` and recursively breaking it down, we can start from the base cases and build up to `n`. This is called a **bottom-up dynamic programming** approach.

We can use an array, let's call it `dp`, where `dp[i]` will store the number of distinct ways to reach step `i`.
First, we handle our base cases:
`dp[1] = 1` (one way to reach step one)
`dp[2] = 2` (two ways to reach step two)

Now, we can iterate from `i` equals three up to `n`. For each `i`, we apply our recurrence relation:
`dp[i] = dp[i-1] + dp[i-2]`

Let's trace this for `n` equals five:
1.  Initialize `dp` array.
2.  `dp[1] = 1`
3.  `dp[2] = 2`
4.  For `i = 3`: `dp[3] = dp[2] + dp[1] = 2 + 1 = 3`
5.  For `i = 4`: `dp[4] = dp[3] + dp[2] = 3 + 2 = 5`
6.  For `i = 5`: `dp[5] = dp[4] + dp[3] = 5 + 3 = 8`

The final answer is `dp[n]`, which for `n` equals five is eight. This approach systematically fills in the `dp` array, ensuring that when we calculate `dp[i]`, `dp[i-1]` and `dp[i-2]` have already been computed and stored.

### Space Optimization

Notice that to calculate `dp[i]`, we only ever need the values from `dp[i-1]` and `dp[i-2]`. We don't need the entire history of the `dp` array. This means we can optimize our space usage. Instead of an array, we can use just two variables to keep track of the previous two results.

Let's call them `waysToReachPrevTwoSteps` and `waysToReachPrevOneStep`.
1.  Initialize `waysToReachPrevTwoSteps = 1` (representing `dp[1]`)
2.  Initialize `waysToReachPrevOneStep = 2` (representing `dp[2]`)

Now, we iterate from `i` equals three up to `n`:
For each step `i`:
1.  Calculate `currentWays = waysToReachPrevOneStep + waysToReachPrevTwoSteps`. This is the number of ways to reach the current step `i`.
2.  Update our "previous" variables:
    *   `waysToReachPrevTwoSteps` now becomes what `waysToReachPrevOneStep` was.
    *   `waysToReachPrevOneStep` now becomes `currentWays`.

Let's trace this for `n` equals five:
*   If `n` is one, return one.
*   If `n` is two, return two.
*   Otherwise, for `n` greater than two:
    *   Initialize `waysToReachPrevTwoSteps = 1` (for step 1)
    *   Initialize `waysToReachPrevOneStep = 2` (for step 2)
    *   Loop from `i = 3` to `n`:
        *   `currentWays = waysToReachPrevOneStep + waysToReachPrevTwoSteps`
        *   If `i = 3`: `currentWays = 2 + 1 = 3`.
        *   Update: `waysToReachPrevTwoSteps = 2`, `waysToReachPrevOneStep = 3`.
        *   If `i = 4`: `currentWays = 3 + 2 = 5`.
        *   Update: `waysToReachPrevTwoSteps = 3`, `waysToReachPrevOneStep = 5`.
        *   If `i = 5`: `currentWays = 5 + 3 = 8`.
        *   Update: `waysToReachPrevTwoSteps = 5`, `waysToReachPrevOneStep = 8`.
    *   After the loop, `waysToReachPrevOneStep` holds the final answer, which is eight.

## Complexity and Edge Cases

The time complexity for both the array-based dynamic programming and the space-optimized approach is **O(n)**. This is because we perform a constant amount of work for each step from three up to `n`, meaning the number of operations grows linearly with `n`. This is a vast improvement over the exponential time of the brute force recursion.

The space complexity for the array-based dynamic programming is **O(n)**, as we need an array of size `n` to store our results. However, with the space-optimized approach, we only use a few constant variables, making its space complexity **O(1)**. This is incredibly efficient, as the memory usage does not grow with the input size `n`.

**Edge cases** to consider are `n` equals one and `n` equals two. Our base cases handle these directly. If `n` is one, there's one way. If `n` is two, there are two ways. The loop for the optimized solution would only run if `n` is greater than two, correctly falling back to these initial values. The problem statement usually implies `n` will be at least one.

## Why This Matters and Related Problems

The Climbing Stairs problem is a fundamental introduction to **Dynamic Programming**. It beautifully illustrates the core concepts:
1.  **Optimal Substructure**: The optimal solution to the problem can be constructed from optimal solutions to its subproblems. The number of ways to reach step `n` depends on the number of ways to reach `n-1` and `n-2`.
2.  **Overlapping Subproblems**: The same subproblems are encountered multiple times when solving the problem recursively. Dynamic Programming solves each subproblem only once and stores its result.

Mastering this problem helps you recognize these patterns in more complex scenarios. It's a stepping stone to understanding how to break down problems into smaller, manageable parts and build up a solution efficiently.

Here are a few related problems that use similar dynamic programming patterns:
*   **Min Cost Climbing Stairs (LeetCode 746)**: A direct variation where each step has an associated cost, and you want to find the minimum cost to reach the top. The recurrence relation is very similar.
*   **House Robber (LeetCode 198)**: You are a robber planning to rob houses along a street. Each house has a certain amount of money, but you cannot rob adjacent houses. You want to maximize the total amount you can rob. This problem also involves making a choice at each step (rob or don't rob) and depends on the results from previous choices, leading to a dynamic programming solution.
*   **N-th Fibonacci Number (LeetCode 509)**: This is essentially the same problem, just framed differently. If you can solve Climbing Stairs, you can find the N-th Fibonacci number.

## Recap

The Climbing Stairs problem asks us to find the number of distinct ways to climb a staircase of `n` steps, taking either one or two steps at a time. While a direct recursive approach is intuitive, it's inefficient due to repeated calculations of the same subproblems. Recognizing this as a dynamic programming problem, specifically a variant of the Fibonacci sequence, allows us to build up the solution efficiently from the bottom up. This leads to an optimal solution with linear time complexity and constant space complexity, demonstrating the power of dynamic programming for solving problems with overlapping subproblems and optimal substructure.
