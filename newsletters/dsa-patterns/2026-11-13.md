## The Curious Case of the Upside-Down Tree

Imagine you're looking at a family tree, but something's amiss. Every person's children are swapped: what was on their left is now on their right, and vice versa. Or perhaps you're looking at your reflection in a funhouse mirror, and everything is perfectly flipped horizontally. This is precisely what we mean when we talk about **inverting a binary tree**. It's a fundamental operation in data structures, surprisingly simple yet deeply illustrative of how we navigate and manipulate tree-like data.

Today, we're tackling LeetCode problem 226, "Invert Binary Tree," often considered an "easy" problem, but one that perfectly introduces the power of recursion for tree traversals.

## What Does It Mean To Invert A Tree?

Let's formally define our task. You're given the **root** of a **binary tree**. Your goal is to invert the tree, and then return its new root. To "invert" a binary tree means that for every single node in the tree, you must swap its **left child** and its **right child**. This operation applies not just to the root, but recursively to all its descendants.

Consider a tiny example:
Suppose we have a node, let's call it `A`.
`A` has a left child, `B`, and a right child, `C`.
After inverting `A`, its left child will become `C`, and its right child will become `B`.
Crucially, this same swapping operation must then be applied to `B` (if it has children) and `C` (if it has children) as well.

Let's use a slightly larger example, the one often seen in this problem:
Imagine a tree with `4` at the root.
`4` has `2` as its left child and `7` as its right child.
`2` has `1` as its left child and `3` as its right child.
`7` has `6` as its left child and `9` as its right child.

Visually, it looks like this:
```
      4
     / \
    2   7
   / \ / \
  1  3 6  9
```

After inverting, we want it to look like this:
```
      4
     / \
    7   2
   / \ / \
  9  6 3  1
```
Notice how `4`'s children `2` and `7` swapped places. But also, `2`'s children `1` and `3` swapped, and `7`'s children `6` and `9` swapped. This recursive nature is key.

## The Intuition: A Recursive Dance

How do we approach this? To invert the entire tree, we can break it down into smaller, identical problems. This is the core idea behind **recursion**.

Think of it this way:
1. To invert the tree rooted at `4`:
   a. First, we need to invert the entire left subtree (the one rooted at `2`).
   b. Second, we need to invert the entire right subtree (the one rooted at `7`).
   c. Finally, once both subtrees are inverted, we swap the left and right children of the current node, `4`.

This self-referential definition is incredibly powerful. To invert a subtree, you do the exact same three steps: invert its left subtree, invert its right subtree, then swap its children. This continues until you reach a node that has no children, or a `null` node, at which point the recursion stops.

This pattern is a classic example of **Depth-First Search (DFS)**, specifically a post-order traversal variant, where we process the children first, then the current node.

## The Mechanism: Depth-First Search (DFS) Step-by-Step

Let's trace this recursive DFS approach with our example tree: `[4,2,7,1,3,6,9]`.

Our recursive function, let's call it `invert(node)`, will work like this:
1. **Base Case:** If the `node` we are currently looking at is `null` (meaning there's no node there, like a missing child), we simply return `null`. This is how our recursion knows when to stop.
2. **Recursive Step:**
   a. Recursively call `invert()` on the `node`'s left child. Let's store the result of this call, which will be the *inverted* left subtree, in a temporary variable, say `invertedLeft`.
   b. Recursively call `invert()` on the `node`'s right child. Similarly, store the result, the *inverted* right subtree, in `invertedRight`.
   c. Now that both subtrees are inverted, we perform the actual swap at the current `node`: Set the `node`'s new left child to `invertedRight`, and its new right child to `invertedLeft`.
   d. Finally, return the current `node` (which now has its children swapped and its subtrees inverted).

Let's walk through our example:

- We start by calling `invert(4)`:
  - `4` is not `null`.
  - **Step 2a:** Call `invert(2)` (the left child of `4`).
    - `2` is not `null`.
    - **Step 2a:** Call `invert(1)` (the left child of `2`).
      - `1` is not `null`.
      - **Step 2a:** Call `invert(null)` (left child of `1`). This returns `null` (base case). `invertedLeft` for `1` is `null`.
      - **Step 2b:** Call `invert(null)` (right child of `1`). This returns `null`. `invertedRight` for `1` is `null`.
      - **Step 2c:** At `1`, swap its children. Its left child (which was `null`) becomes `invertedRight` (`null`). Its right child (which was `null`) becomes `invertedLeft` (`null`). No visible change.
      - **Step 2d:** Return `1`. So, `invertedLeft` for `2` is now `1`.
    - **Step 2b:** Call `invert(3)` (the right child of `2`).
      - `3` is not `null`.
      - **Step 2a:** Call `invert(null)`. Returns `null`. `invertedLeft` for `3` is `null`.
      - **Step 2b:** Call `invert(null)`. Returns `null`. `invertedRight` for `3` is `null`.
      - **Step 2c:** At `3`, swap its children. No visible change.
      - **Step 2d:** Return `3`. So, `invertedRight` for `2` is now `3`.
    - **Step 2c:** Now back at `2`. Its `invertedLeft` is `1`, its `invertedRight` is `3`. Swap them:
      - `2`'s new left child becomes `3`.
      - `2`'s new right child becomes `1`.
    - **Step 2d:** Return `2` (which now has `3` on its left, `1` on its right). So, `invertedLeft` for `4` is now this modified `2`.
  - **Step 2b:** Call `invert(7)` (the right child of `4`).
    - `7` is not `null`.
    - **Step 2a:** Call `invert(6)` (the left child of `7`).
      - ... (similar to `invert(1)` and `invert(3)` above, `invert(6)` will return `6` unchanged). So, `invertedLeft` for `7` is `6`.
    - **Step 2b:** Call `invert(9)` (the right child of `7`).
      - ... (`invert(9)` will return `9` unchanged). So, `invertedRight` for `7` is `9`.
    - **Step 2c:** Now back at `7`. Its `invertedLeft` is `6`, its `invertedRight` is `9`. Swap them:
      - `7`'s new left child becomes `9`.
      - `7`'s new right child becomes `6`.
    - **Step 2d:** Return `7` (which now has `9` on its left, `6` on its right). So, `invertedRight` for `4` is now this modified `7`.
  - **Step 2c:** Now back at `4`. Its `invertedLeft` is the modified `2`, its `invertedRight` is the modified `7`. Swap them:
    - `4`'s new left child becomes the modified `7`.
    - `4`'s new right child becomes the modified `2`.
  - **Step 2d:** Return `4`. This is our fully inverted tree!

This recursive process ensures that every node in the tree has its children swapped, resulting in the complete inversion.

## Complexity and Edge Cases

Let's analyze the performance of this recursive solution:

-   **Time Complexity:** The time complexity is **O(N)**, where N is the total number of nodes in the binary tree. Why? Because our algorithm visits each node exactly once. For each node, we perform a constant amount of work: checking if it's null, making two recursive calls, and swapping two pointers. Since the work per node is constant, the total time is directly proportional to the number of nodes.

-   **Space Complexity:** The space complexity is **O(H)**, where H is the height of the binary tree. This space is used by the **recursion stack**. Each time we make a recursive call, a new stack frame is added. The maximum number of active stack frames at any given time corresponds to the longest path from the root to a leaf, which is the height of the tree.
    -   In the worst-case scenario, if the tree is completely skewed (like a linked list, where each node only has one child), the height H can be N. So, space complexity would be O(N).
    -   In the best-case scenario, if the tree is perfectly balanced, the height H is approximately log N (logarithm base 2 of N). In this case, space complexity would be O(log N).

**Edge Cases:**
-   **Empty Tree:** If the initial `root` is `null`, our base case immediately handles it by returning `null`. This is correct; an empty tree remains an empty tree when inverted.
-   **Single Node Tree:** If the tree consists only of a root node with no children, the recursive calls for its left and right children will both hit the base case and return `null`. The root will then swap its `null` children, effectively remaining unchanged. This is also correct.
-   **Skewed Trees:** Whether the tree is left-skewed, right-skewed, or perfectly balanced, the recursive logic holds true. Each node's children are swapped, and the process continues down the branches.

## Why This Matters: Beyond Just Flipping

While "Invert Binary Tree" might seem like a simple problem, it's a cornerstone for understanding **tree recursion** and **tree traversal**. It teaches you a fundamental pattern: to solve a problem on a tree, you often "do something" at the current node, and then recursively "do the same thing" on its children.

This problem is a fantastic stepping stone for many other tree-related challenges:

1.  **Symmetric Tree (LeetCode 101):** This problem asks you to determine if a binary tree is symmetric, meaning it's a mirror image of itself. Solving it often involves comparing the left subtree with the *inverted* right subtree, or a similar recursive comparison pattern.
2.  **Same Tree (LeetCode 100):** Here, you need to check if two binary trees are structurally identical and have the same values at corresponding nodes. This again uses a recursive traversal to compare nodes and their children.
3.  **Maximum Depth of Binary Tree (LeetCode 104):** Finding the longest path from the root to a leaf node is another classic recursive tree problem, where the depth of a node is one plus the maximum depth of its children.

Understanding how to invert a binary tree provides a solid foundation for tackling these and many more complex tree problems in coding interviews. It highlights the elegance and efficiency of recursion for problems with self-similar substructures.

## Recap

Inverting a binary tree means swapping the left and right children of every node, recursively applying this operation throughout the tree. The most intuitive and elegant way to solve this is using a recursive Depth-First Search, where we first invert the subtrees and then swap them at the current node. This approach visits each node exactly once, resulting in linear time complexity, and uses space proportional to the tree's height for the recursion stack.
