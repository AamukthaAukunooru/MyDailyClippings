## Peering Back to the Cosmic Dawn

Astronomers have shattered a record, discovering the most distant **quasar** ever observed, a luminous beacon dating back to a mere 630 million years after the **Big Bang**. This ancient object, detected by the European Space Agency's (ESA) **Euclid Space Telescope**, offers an unprecedented glimpse into the universe's infancy. A quasar is the incredibly bright core of a galaxy, powered by a **supermassive black hole** actively devouring surrounding matter. As gas and dust spiral into the black hole, they heat up to extreme temperatures, emitting vast amounts of energy that can outshine an entire galaxy.

This newly discovered quasar, found at a redshift of z=8.13, pushes the boundary of our understanding of how quickly supermassive black holes could have grown in the early universe. Its existence so early in cosmic history challenges current models of black hole formation and evolution, suggesting that these colossal cosmic engines might have formed and accumulated mass much faster than previously thought. The Euclid telescope, designed to map the large-scale structure of the universe, is proving to be a powerful tool for uncovering these distant, fundamental objects, helping us piece together the story of our universe's earliest moments.

## The Cradle of Worlds: Witnessing a Solar System's Birth

In a remarkable first, astronomers have detected **titanium oxide** in gas form around a young, massive star, providing a rare and invaluable window into the very earliest stages of **solar system formation**. This discovery offers direct evidence of the raw materials and processes at play before planets even begin to coalesce. Titanium oxide is a refractory material, meaning it can withstand extremely high temperatures. Its presence as a gas indicates a region of intense heat, likely close to the nascent star, where rocky material is being vaporized.

This detection is crucial because it allows scientists to study the chemical composition of the protoplanetary disk, the swirling cloud of gas and dust from which planets are born, at a stage preceding the formation of planetesimals, the building blocks of planets. Understanding the distribution and state of these elements, like titanium, helps researchers refine models of how rocky planets, including our own Earth, initially formed. It's a direct observation of the cosmic crucible where future worlds are being forged, offering profound insights into our own origins.

## Planetary Defense in Action: Hera Reveals DART's Impact

Humanity's first full-scale planetary defense test, the **Double Asteroid Redirection Test (DART)** mission, has yielded new insights thanks to the ESA's **Hera mission**. On November 25, 2026, ESA released images captured by Hera, revealing the dramatic aftermath of DART's impact on the asteroid **Dimorphos**. In September 2022, the DART spacecraft intentionally collided with Dimorphos, a small moonlet orbiting a larger asteroid named Didymos, to test whether a kinetic impactor could alter an asteroid's trajectory.

Hera's close-up observations now show a newly formed crater on Dimorphos, providing crucial data on the effectiveness of the impact and the material properties of the asteroid. This detailed visual evidence is vital for validating the models and simulations used to predict the outcomes of such deflection attempts. The success of the DART mission, now further illuminated by Hera, marks a significant step forward in our ability to protect Earth from potential asteroid threats, transforming science fiction into a tangible defense strategy.
