Welcome to My Daily Clippings, your daily dose of cosmic wonder and scientific insight. Today, we're diving into a universe that is far from static, from the restless dance of planets within our own solar system to the dramatic lives of distant stars and the birth of new worlds. We'll explore new insights into how our celestial neighbors are changing, witness the earliest moments of a "baby" planet's life, and celebrate a powerful new eye on the cosmos that promises to redefine our understanding of the universe.

## Our Ever-Changing Cosmic Neighborhood

Our solar system, often perceived as a stable clockwork, is in fact a dynamic arena of ongoing change. Take Mercury, our Sun's innermost companion. New research reveals that this small, rocky world is shrinking at a rate previously underestimated by a significant 30%. Its heavily cratered surface has effectively masked the true extent of this contraction, which is driven by the cooling of its interior. This discovery reminds us that even seemingly ancient celestial bodies are still evolving.

Meanwhile, our own Earth is also subtly, yet constantly, reshaping itself. Satellite tracking has unveiled that the planet's center of mass shifts by fractions of an inch each year. This fascinating oscillation is caused by the seasonal redistribution of water, as snow melts, monsoons rage, and oceans swell and recede. NASA scientists are diligently tracking these movements, offering a unique perspective on the global water cycle and its impact on our planet's fundamental balance.

Even our Moon, a seemingly inert celestial body, is proving to be far from static. NASA's Lunar Reconnaissance Orbiter (LRO) recently spotted a new, "once-in-a-century" crater. This impressive impact feature, which started as a routine data-quality check, highlights the ongoing bombardment of our natural satellite and the dynamic processes that continue to sculpt its ancient face. And speaking of dynamic atmospheres, Saturn, the jewel of our solar system, has surprised astronomers with the formation of a newly visible atmospheric decagon at its south pole. While Saturn's north pole has long been famous for its hexagonal storm, this new decagon at the opposite pole adds another layer of mystery to the gas giant's complex weather systems.

## Peering into Stellar Lives and New Worlds

Beyond our solar system, the cosmos continues to reveal breathtaking stories of creation and destruction. Astronomers have just confirmed the youngest known planet, a "baby" world named Elias 2-24 b, less than a million years old. This nascent giant is still embedded within its natal disk of dust and gas, offering an unparalleled opportunity to study planetary formation in its earliest stages. This discovery pushes the boundaries of our understanding of how planets coalesce from protoplanetary disks.

In another remarkable feat of cosmic detection, scientists have found a new way to tell if a star has "eaten" a rocky planet. The key lies in observing the star's beryllium content. This element is not synthesized inside stars, meaning any anomalous presence of beryllium on a star's surface could be the telltale sign of a consumed planetary meal. This ingenious method opens a new window into the dramatic interactions between stars and their planetary systems. The James Webb Space Telescope (JWST), always pushing the frontiers of observation, is also busy searching for brown dwarfs in stunning star-forming regions. These enigmatic objects, too massive to be planets but too small to ignite sustained nuclear fusion like true stars, are a crucial missing link in our understanding of the cosmic census. JWST's observations are helping to refine the very definition of these "failed stars" and uncover new classes of them.

And for tales of stellar drama, new research sheds light on how some of the universe's most extreme dead stars, neutron stars, can form from "vampire" white dwarfs. These white dwarfs, the dense remnants of Sun-like stars, can siphon material from a companion star, like cosmic vampires. If the conditions are "just right," this accretion process can trigger an extreme makeover, causing the white dwarf to collapse into an even denser neutron star.

## A New Eye on the Universe

Finally, a major milestone in space exploration is unfolding with the Nancy Grace Roman Space Telescope. NASA has begun the delicate process of powering on the instruments aboard the observatory. As Roman journeys nearly a million miles to its final orbital destination, this critical step prepares it for its scientific mission. The excitement around Roman is further amplified by the fantastic news that its mission has been extended. Initially planned for a 5-year primary mission, the telescope now has enough fuel to potentially operate for 22 years, more than doubling its projected lifespan. This extended mission promises an unprecedented wealth of data, allowing Roman to conduct deep and wide surveys of the universe, uncovering thousands of exoplanets and shedding light on the mysteries of dark energy and dark matter. The universe is about to get a much clearer picture, thanks to this powerful new eye.
