## Euclid Unveils a Universe of Cosmic Magnifying Glasses

The European Space Agency's **Euclid** satellite, designed to map the dark universe, has delivered an astounding new dataset, revealing a record-breaking fifteen thousand potential strong **gravitational lenses**. These cosmic "magnifying glasses" are massive foreground objects, like galaxy clusters, whose immense gravity bends and magnifies the light from more distant background galaxies. This phenomenon allows astronomers to peer into the early universe and study objects that would otherwise be too faint to observe. Researchers from the University of British Columbia (UBC) played a crucial role in this discovery, helping to uncover this unprecedented collection. The sheer number of these newly identified lenses provides an invaluable tool for investigating **dark matter**, the mysterious substance that makes up about twenty-seven percent of the universe but doesn't interact with light, making it invisible to telescopes. By studying how these lenses distort light, scientists can map the distribution of dark matter and gain critical insights into its nature and its role in the formation and evolution of cosmic structures. This bounty of gravitational lenses from Euclid promises to unlock new secrets about the universe's hidden components.

## The Blaze Star's Imminent Eruption and a Challenging Look at Early Black Holes

Astronomers are holding their breath for a celestial spectacle: the anticipated eruption of **T Coronae Borealis**, affectionately known as the "Blaze Star." This recurrent nova, located approximately three thousand light-years from Earth in the constellation Corona Borealis, is expected to brighten dramatically, becoming visible to the naked eye for a brief period. T CrB is a binary system where a dense **white dwarf** star siphons material, primarily hydrogen gas, from a bloated **red giant** companion. This accumulated material eventually triggers a thermonuclear runaway on the white dwarf's surface, causing a sudden, brilliant outburst.

The Blaze Star erupts roughly every eighty years, with its last observed outburst occurring in 1946. While a statistically most likely date of June 25, 2026, was suggested for its next eruption, it has yet to occur, keeping stargazers on high alert for late 2026 or 2027. When it does erupt, T CrB is expected to brighten from its usual tenth magnitude to a dazzling second or third magnitude, making it as bright as the North Star and visible even from light-polluted areas for a few days or weeks. To spot it, look towards the constellation Corona Borealis, which appears as a small arc of stars, ideally after 10:00 PM when it is higher in the sky.

In other news that highlights the dynamic nature of scientific inquiry, new observations are challenging a previous groundbreaking claim about an "outsize" black hole in the early universe. Three years ago, the galaxy **UHZ1**, located 13.2 billion light-years away and seen when the universe was only about 470 million years old, made headlines for reportedly hosting an unusually large black hole. This initial discovery, made using NASA's Chandra X-ray Observatory and the James Webb Space Telescope, suggested a black hole whose mass was comparable to that of its host galaxy's stars, implying it formed from massive "heavy seeds" through direct collapse. A recent reanalysis of UHZ1, utilizing existing X-ray data and new infrared observations, is now challenging this initial interpretation. Such re-evaluations are crucial in astronomy, as they refine our understanding of extreme cosmic phenomena and the conditions of the early universe, demonstrating that scientific knowledge is continuously built upon and refined.
