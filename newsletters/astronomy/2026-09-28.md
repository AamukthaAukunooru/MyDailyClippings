What a week it's been in the cosmos! From black holes flexing their immense power across galaxies to a tiny asteroid revealing its dynamic secrets, and even our own planet playing a crucial role in a journey to Jupiter, the universe continues to unveil its wonders. Each new observation and analysis deepens our understanding of the intricate mechanisms that govern everything from the smallest icy rings to the largest galactic structures.

## Black Holes: The Universe's Architects of Destruction and Creation

Black holes, often perceived as cosmic devourers, are increasingly revealing their dual nature as powerful architects that profoundly influence the evolution of galaxies. Recent discoveries highlight just how far-reaching their impact can be, from repeatedly blasting jets of energy into intergalactic space to even affecting star formation in their host galaxies.

Astronomers have identified what might be the first example of a new class of radio galaxy, one whose central supermassive black hole has unleashed powerful jets on no fewer than four separate occasions. High-resolution observations, combining data from the upgraded Giant Metrewave Radio Telescope (GMRT), MeerKAT, and the Very Large Array, show four distinct pairs of radio structures symmetrically arranged around the central galaxy. This "quadruple-double" radio galaxy, known as J023721.13−010528.5, located at a redshift of 0.372, offers a unique window into the episodic activity of these colossal black holes, demonstrating that their periods of intense energy output are not singular events but rather a recurring phenomenon that can span millions of years. The jets, in the most powerful class known as FR II, can extend for millions of light-years, making them some of the largest objects in the universe. Each burst of activity leaves behind its own pair of "hotspots" where the jets impact surrounding gas, with older outbursts located further from the galaxy's core.

Closer to home, within our very own Milky Way galaxy, scientists have made a groundbreaking discovery: the first confirmed "microblazar" with a jet pointed directly at Earth. Named IRAS 18293-0941 and located about 12,000 light-years away, this system consists of a stellar-mass black hole, roughly 10 times the mass of our Sun, siphoning material from a companion star every 11.38 days. As this material spirals into the black hole, it unleashes two powerful jets of plasma, traveling at about 75% the speed of light. One of these jets happens to be aimed almost directly at us, making it a microblazar, a smaller version of the more distant blazars powered by supermassive black holes in other galaxies. The other jet, though not directly observable, can be detected by its impact on a nearby molecular cloud, which it heats and ionizes, creating particles with energies 100 times greater than those produced by the Large Hadron Collider. This discovery not only confirms a theoretical prediction made over three decades ago but also provides a unique, nearby laboratory to study the extreme physics of relativistic jets and their interaction with the interstellar medium, potentially shedding light on the origin of some of the most energetic cosmic rays in our galaxy.

These powerful jets are not just cosmic fireworks, they play a critical role in shaping the evolution of entire galaxies. New research suggests that supermassive black holes at galactic centers can influence the vast reservoirs of gas surrounding their host galaxies, known as the circumgalactic medium (CGM). This diffuse gas envelope, extending 10 to 20 times the size of the visible galaxy, is the raw material for star formation. However, if all this gas were to cool and collapse, galaxies would be far more star-rich than they are. Astronomers have long suspected that energy from black holes prevents this cooling, and now, evidence shows that the powerful jets of hot plasma blasted out by black holes can directly heat and disturb the CGM along their path. This energetic "punch" inhibits the gas from cooling and clumping, thereby suppressing star formation and ultimately determining whether a galaxy continues to be active and star-forming or becomes quiescent.

## Dynamic Rings and Martian Mysteries

Our own solar system continues to surprise us with its hidden complexities. The James Webb Space Telescope (JWST) has revealed dynamic structural evolution in the rings of Chariklo, a small asteroid-like body known as a Centaur. Chariklo, about 250 kilometers (155 miles) wide, orbits the Sun between Saturn and Uranus, at about 17 times Earth's distance. It's the only known Centaur with its own ring system, a discovery made in 2013. Recent JWST observations, employing a technique called stellar occultation, where Chariklo passes in front of a distant star, have shown significant changes in its two rings over just a few years. The inner ring has become significantly more opaque, while the outer ring has become less opaque, suggesting that material is being gained and lost by each ring. These unexpected changes indicate that ring systems around small solar system bodies may be far more dynamic than previously thought, adding to the mystery of how these rings formed and evolved.

Meanwhile, on Mars, a new geochemical modeling study offers a compelling explanation for the planet's "missing" carbonate rocks, which are far less abundant on the surface than expected given Mars' watery past and thick, carbon dioxide-rich atmosphere. Researchers from JAXA and the University of Tokyo suggest that the type of rock water interacts with is crucial, and that percolating groundwater could be dissolving carbonates near the surface and re-precipitating them deeper underground. This means Mars may harbor substantial stores of carbonate rocks, and thus locked-up carbon dioxide, beneath its surface, hidden from orbital and rover observations. The study also helps explain why the carbonates found on Mars fall into two distinct groups, calcium- and iron-rich, and magnesium-rich, with feldspar-rich rocks readily producing the former. This insight points to feldspar-rich terrains and subsurface drilling as promising targets for future missions seeking evidence of Mars' wetter, and potentially more habitable, past.

## JUICE Mission's Earth Flyby

On September 28, the European Space Agency's (ESA) Jupiter Icy Moons Explorer (JUICE) mission will perform a crucial Earth flyby, the third of four planned gravity assists designed to slingshot the spacecraft toward Jupiter. Launched in April 2023, JUICE is on an eight-year cruise to the Jovian system, where it is expected to arrive in July 2031. The flyby, with its closest approach at 13:45 CEST (11:45 UTC) over the Indian Ocean, will deflect JUICE's trajectory by about 20 degrees and increase its velocity by approximately 3.5 kilometers per second. These gravity assist maneuvers are essential for the spacecraft to gain the necessary speed and adjust its path without expending excessive amounts of fuel, allowing it to reach Jupiter and its icy moons, Ganymede, Callisto, and Europa, where it will eventually orbit Ganymede. During this flyby, JUICE's ten science instruments will be activated to collect data on Earth and the Moon, providing a valuable opportunity for instrument calibration and data analysis tool preparation before its arrival at Jupiter.
