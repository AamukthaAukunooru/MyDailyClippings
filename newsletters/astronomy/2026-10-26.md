## Simulating the Universe's First Light

Imagine peering back in time to the universe's infancy, just a few hundred million years after the Big Bang. That's precisely what astronomers are striving to do with powerful new tools like the James Webb Space Telescope (JWST) and the upcoming Nancy Roman Space Telescope. To help interpret the wealth of data these observatories are collecting, researchers at the Center for Astrophysics, Harvard and Smithsonian, have developed the **Lumina simulation**, a monumental effort to model the early universe on an unprecedented scale. This simulation brings to life the cosmic dawn, a period when the first stars and galaxies began to ignite, reionizing the neutral hydrogen gas that pervaded the universe. By simulating a vast volume of space, approximately 300 million light-years across, Lumina allows scientists to track the evolution of millions of galaxies and billions of stars, providing a crucial framework for understanding the observations from JWST and Roman. This work is vital because it helps us connect the theoretical models of cosmology with the actual light we detect from the most distant, and therefore earliest, objects in the universe, offering profound insights into how cosmic structures first emerged.

## The Milky Way's "Upside-Down" Growth

Our home galaxy, the **Milky Way**, is a majestic spiral, a cosmic island containing hundreds of billions of stars. For decades, astronomers have worked to unravel its complex formation history. New research suggests a fascinating twist: the Milky Way's disk may have grown "upside down," with younger stars forming in progressively thinner layers over billions of years. This finding challenges some previous assumptions about how spiral galaxies assemble. The study, published in *The Astrophysical Journal* by researchers from the Instituto de Astrofísica de Canarias (IAC) and the University of La Laguna (ULL), analyzed data from the **Gaia mission**, which has precisely mapped the positions and motions of billions of stars in our galaxy. They found that the oldest stars in the disk are distributed in a thicker, more puffed-up layer, while younger stars, born more recently, are concentrated in a much thinner plane. This "upside-down" growth implies a dynamic and evolving process, where the galactic disk gradually settled and flattened over cosmic time, continually adding new generations of stars to its ever-slimmer central plane. Understanding this process is key to comprehending the evolution of not just our own galaxy, but spiral galaxies across the universe.

## NASA Accelerates Lunar Ambitions

The spirit of Apollo is alive and well, as NASA is moving with renewed urgency to establish a permanent human presence on the Moon. The agency has announced ambitious plans to build a **moon base** and land astronauts on the lunar surface within as little as two years. This accelerated timeline underscores a significant shift in strategy, aiming to rapidly advance the **Artemis program** and its goal of sustainable lunar exploration. The vision extends beyond mere flag-planting; it involves constructing habitats, developing infrastructure for resource utilization, and conducting long-duration scientific research. This push for a sustained lunar presence is driven by both scientific curiosity and strategic imperatives, including preparing for future human missions to Mars. By establishing a foothold on the Moon, NASA aims to develop critical technologies and operational experience in a deep-space environment, paving the way for humanity's next giant leaps. This rapid advancement signals a thrilling new chapter in human spaceflight, promising a return to the Moon that is not just a visit, but a stay.
