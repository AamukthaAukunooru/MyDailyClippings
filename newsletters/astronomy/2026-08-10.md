## Sky-Gazing Spectacles: Eclipse and Meteors

Prepare for a truly rare event just around the corner: a total solar eclipse on August 12. For those in its narrow path, primarily Greenland, Iceland, and parts of northern Spain and Portugal, this will be an unforgettable experience. The moon will perfectly obscure the sun, revealing the sun's ethereal corona, its outermost atmosphere, a sight rarely visible to the naked eye. Scientists are particularly excited as this eclipse promises to unveil phenomena never before seen, offering a unique window into the sun's dynamic and mysterious outer layers. From the initial "first contact" when the moon begins its journey across the sun, through the dramatic "diamond ring" effect, to the breathtaking totality, each stage offers a distinct and awe-inspiring view of our star.

And for those of us not directly in the eclipse's path, the universe has another gift: the Perseid meteor shower. This annual summer light show is already underway and is expected to peak around the mornings of August 13. Conditions are especially promising this year for observers in the Northern Hemisphere, with the moon waning and providing darker skies for optimal viewing. These "shooting stars" are actually tiny dust and rock particles shed by Comet Swift-Tuttle, burning up as they hit Earth's atmosphere at incredible speeds, creating brilliant streaks across the night sky. Finding a dark spot away from city lights will offer the best chance to witness dozens of meteors per hour.

## Martian Mysteries Unveiled

Our robotic explorers continue to peel back the layers of Mars's past and present, offering tantalizing clues about its evolution. NASA's MAVEN (Mars Atmosphere and Volatile Evolution) mission has recently made a significant breakthrough in understanding the Red Planet's auroras. Scientists have uncovered a key piece of the puzzle, finding that certain types of Martian auroras form in a similar way to those seen here on Earth. Unlike Earth's vibrant polar lights, which are typically confined to the magnetic poles, Mars's auroras can appear more widespread due to its patchy, crustal magnetic fields. Understanding these auroras helps us piece together how Mars lost much of its atmosphere over billions of years, a crucial factor in its transition from a potentially habitable world to the arid planet we see today.

Adding to our understanding of Mars's deep history is the discovery of a rare Martian meteorite, now identified as 1.27 billion years old. Boston College scientists determined this ancient space rock originated from a previously unsampled, pristine source within the Red Planet. Meteorites from Mars are incredibly valuable, acting as natural core samples that bring fragments of the planet's interior directly to us. This particular meteorite offers unprecedented insights into Mars's deep interior processes and volcanic activity from over a billion years ago, providing a direct window into a period of Martian geological history that has been largely a mystery until now.

## Peering into the Cosmos and Future Technologies

Beyond our immediate cosmic neighborhood, scientists are pushing the boundaries of fundamental physics. New research, led by David Dunsky, has utilized cosmic filaments to set the first limits on dark matter's decay into gravitons. Dark matter, which makes up about 85% of the universe's total mass, remains one of the greatest enigmas in science, detectable only through its gravitational influence. By studying the vast, web-like structures of cosmic filaments, the largest known structures in the universe, researchers are gaining new insights into the properties of this elusive substance. This work helps constrain theoretical models of dark matter, taking us a step closer to understanding what it is made of and how it interacts with the rest of the universe.

Closer to home, a groundbreaking technological development could soon transform how we operate in Earth orbit. The world's first air-breathing satellite thruster is poised for a test run, potentially opening up a new frontier: "very low Earth orbit" (VLEO). Currently, satellites in VLEO, typically below 250 kilometers (about 155 miles), quickly lose altitude due to atmospheric drag, requiring frequent, fuel-intensive boosts. This innovative electric propulsion system is designed to scoop up the thin atmospheric gases present even at these altitudes and use them as propellant, essentially "breathing" the atmosphere to maintain orbit. This technology could allow satellites to operate for much longer without carrying heavy fuel tanks, enabling new scientific missions, more persistent Earth observation, and potentially cheaper access to this underutilized orbital regime.

And speaking of groundbreaking observations, the Daniel K. Inouye Solar Telescope in Hawaii has delivered the highest-resolution images of the sun's surface ever captured. These incredibly detailed views reveal the intricate, boiling plasma on the sun's surface, showing instabilities that may help explain why the sun's outer atmosphere, the corona, is millions of degrees hotter than its surface. These images are not just aesthetically stunning, they are critical data points for understanding the fundamental processes that drive solar weather and influence space throughout the solar system.
