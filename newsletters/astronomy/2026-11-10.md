What a time to be alive for anyone with an eye on the cosmos! From groundbreaking discoveries by our most powerful telescopes to ambitious missions pushing the boundaries of human and robotic exploration, the universe continues to unveil its wonders. This week, we're marveling at distant galaxies, charting new courses to Mars, and innovating the very foundations of space travel and orbital operations.

## Unveiling Cosmic Secrets

The James Webb Space Telescope, or JWST, continues to redefine our understanding of the distant universe. Astronomers have used JWST to identify an astonishing **104 magnified stellar events** in a galaxy nicknamed the **Dragon**, located approximately 6.5 billion light-years away. This discovery, more than doubling the previous record of 44 detections in the same galaxy, offers an unprecedented look at massive stars and the intricate structure of a foreground galaxy cluster, Abell 370, which acts as a powerful gravitational lens. These temporary "flickers" of light, caused by individual stars in the distant galaxy crossing highly magnified regions, could also provide new insights into the mysterious nature of **dark matter**. The research, led by astronomer José María Palencia of the Institute of Physics of Cantabria, was published in *Astronomy & Astrophysics*.

Closer to home, but still in the realm of deep space, a new study of 37 nearby **dwarf galaxies** has revealed a surprising lack of oxygen. Oxygen, the third most abundant element in the cosmos, is a crucial tracer for understanding how heavy elements, the building blocks of planets and life, are distributed throughout galaxies. Researchers, using data from the Hubble Space Telescope, found that these small galaxies, containing anywhere from a few thousand to hundreds of millions of stars, retain only a small fraction, typically around 11 to 15 percent, of the oxygen produced by their stars over cosmic time. This suggests that energetic radiation from massive stars and powerful galactic winds unleashed by supernovae are far more effective at expelling oxygen-rich gas from low-mass galaxies than current models predict, challenging our understanding of galaxy evolution. Grace Telford, an assistant professor at the University of Utah's Department of Physics & Astronomy and second author on the paper published in *The Astrophysical Journal*, highlighted that this implies we don't yet have a complete understanding of how massive stars interact with their host galaxies.

Meanwhile, the elusive nature of **dark matter** continues to be probed. New research has effectively ruled out "stupendously large" primordial black holes as a significant source of this invisible cosmic component. Primordial black holes are hypothetical objects that could have formed in the very early universe from dense clumps of matter. Some theories suggested that these immense black holes, far more massive than those found at the centers of galaxies, could account for a substantial portion of dark matter. However, a new analysis by researchers at Johannes Gutenberg University Mainz, including Christopher Gerlach, Yann Gouttenoire, Antonio J. Iovino, and Nicholas Leister, used constraints from the cosmic microwave background, the afterglow of the Big Bang, and the distribution of galaxies to show that such a population would have left a distinct observational signature that exceeds existing limits. Their study, published in *Physical Review Letters*, narrows the search for dark matter's true identity.

## Advancing Space Exploration

Humanity's journey to Mars took another significant step forward as NASA's twin **ESCAPADE** (Escape and Plasma Acceleration and Dynamics Explorers) spacecraft successfully completed powered Earth flybys. These maneuvers, combining Earth's gravity with sustained engine burns, have slingshotted the two identical spacecraft toward the Red Planet. Launched in November 2025, ESCAPADE took a unique, kidney-bean-shaped path around the Earth-Sun Lagrange point 2 before returning to Earth for these critical boosts. The spacecraft, named Red and Blue, are now en route to Mars, where they are expected to arrive in September 2027. Their mission is to study how the solar wind interacts with Mars' unique "hybrid" magnetosphere, providing vital insights into how the planet lost its once-thick atmosphere, which may have supported liquid water and possibly life.

Looking ahead to the next frontier in reusable rocketry, SpaceX is gearing up for its ambitious **Starship Flight 15** test mission, targeted for as early as Friday, November 13th, from Starbase, Texas. This flight will attempt a groundbreaking "double catch," aiming to recover both the **Super Heavy booster** and the **Starship upper stage** using the launch tower's mechanical arms, affectionately known as "chopsticks". While Super Heavy boosters have been successfully caught before, this will be the first attempt to catch the Starship upper stage, which will return from orbit after more than 22 hours in flight. This unprecedented feat is crucial for achieving the full and rapid reusability that is the economic cornerstone of the Starship program, paving the way for more frequent and affordable access to space, including future lunar and Mars missions.

Innovations in propulsion are also accelerating. NASA has launched the **Advanced Mid-Power Electric Rocket (AMPERE) Challenge**, inviting U.S. companies to design and build a new class of electric propulsion thruster. These mid-power (3 to 7 kilowatt) **Hall effect thruster systems** are identified as critical capabilities for a range of missions, from satellites to defense and deep-space exploration. Such electric propulsion systems offer significant advantages in fuel efficiency for long-duration missions. Complementing this, research is exploring how a common **food emulsifier** could solve one of rocketry's oldest fuel problems, potentially bridging the gap between the advantages and disadvantages of solid and liquid rocket fuels.

## Building Our Future in Space

As our orbital environment becomes increasingly crowded, new solutions are emerging to manage the thousands of inactive or "zombie" satellites in Low-Earth Orbit. Researchers are developing a system called **CORTEX**, an artificial intelligence-driven guide designed to help rescue satellites autonomously. CORTEX aims to guide repair satellites to dock with these non-communicative objects, addressing the growing challenge of space debris and extending the operational life of valuable assets. This technology could be vital for maintaining a sustainable orbital environment.

Looking to the Moon, NASA has launched a **$1 million Moon Base Student Challenge**, inviting high school and post-secondary students across the United States to contribute innovative solutions for humanity's future lunar outpost. The challenge focuses on real-world engineering problems for establishing a sustained human presence at the lunar South Pole, covering areas like power generation, habitat construction, life-support systems, and astronaut safety. This initiative aims to engage the next generation of explorers and innovators, with registration open until January 25, 2027.

In a direct call to those aspiring to join the ranks of space explorers, NASA is currently accepting applications for its next class of astronauts. The agency is seeking U.S. citizens with a master's degree in a STEM field and at least three years of related professional experience, with applications due by 11:59 p.m. EST on Thursday, December 3, 2026. These new astronauts will play a crucial role in the **Artemis program**, which aims to return humans to the Moon, build the Moon Base, and lay the groundwork for the first crewed missions to Mars. This call comes shortly after the successful completion of **CHAPEA Mission 2**, where four research volunteers, Ross Elder, Ellen Ellis, James Spicer, and Matthew Montgomery, spent 378 days in NASA's 3D-printed Mars Dune Alpha habitat in Houston. This year-long simulation, which concluded on October 31st, provided invaluable data on crew health and performance, resource limitations, and communication delays, all critical for preparing for the immense physiological and psychological demands of a real human mission to Mars.
