We also get a glimpse of Mercury through the eyes of a UK-built X-ray instrument.

## Voyager 1: A Light-Day from Home

Our venerable **Voyager 1** spacecraft, a true pioneer of interstellar space, has achieved another breathtaking milestone: it is now officially one **light-day** from Earth. This means that light, traveling at nearly 300,000 kilometers per second, takes a full 24 hours to bridge the vast distance between us and the probe. To put that into perspective, one light-day is approximately 16.1 billion miles, or 25.9 billion kilometers.

Launched over 49 years ago, in 1977, Voyager 1 has journeyed farther from Earth than any other human-made object, venturing beyond the heliosphere, the protective bubble of particles and magnetic fields generated by our Sun. The team at NASA's Jet Propulsion Laboratory continues to celebrate the probe's epic journey, even as they work through a recent communication anomaly that has cropped up. Despite its age and the immense distances involved, Voyager 1 continues to send back invaluable data, offering us a direct window into the uncharted territory of interstellar space. Its mission reminds us of the incredible longevity and ingenuity of human engineering, and the insatiable drive to explore the unknown.

## Mapping the Invisible: Euclid and Hera's Cosmic Insights

Two European Space Agency missions are currently making significant strides in understanding our universe, from the grandest cosmic structures to the intimate details of an asteroid impact.

The **Euclid space telescope**, launched to create a detailed 3D map of the universe, is off to a strong start in its hunt for **strong gravitational lenses**. These cosmic magnifying glasses occur when massive objects, such as galaxy clusters, bend the light from more distant background galaxies, distorting and amplifying their images. By studying these warped images, scientists can infer the distribution of **dark matter**, the mysterious, invisible substance that makes up about 27 percent of the universe's mass but does not interact with light. Euclid's observations are crucial for constraining cosmological models and helping us understand the elusive nature of dark matter and **dark energy**, which together constitute about 95 percent of the universe's total mass and energy. Imagine trying to map a city where 95 percent of the buildings are invisible, yet their presence subtly warps the light from the visible ones, that's the challenge Euclid is tackling.

Meanwhile, ESA's **Hera probe** is employing its "superhuman" vision to conduct a detailed inspection of the impact site left by NASA's **Double Asteroid Redirection Test (DART)** mission. Approximately four years ago, humanity intentionally collided a spacecraft with the asteroid moonlet **Dimorphos** in a groundbreaking planetary defense experiment. The DART mission was a resounding success, demonstrating our ability to alter the trajectory of an asteroid. Hera's role is to provide a close-up, post-impact survey, meticulously examining the crater, measuring Dimorphos' mass, composition, and even its internal structure. This detailed analysis is vital for validating and refining our planetary defense models, ensuring that if a truly threatening asteroid were ever discovered, we would have the knowledge and capability to protect our home planet.

## Mercury's X-ray Glimpse from BepiColombo

After eight years in space, the **Mercury Imaging X-ray Spectrometer (MIXS)**, a U.K.-built instrument aboard the joint ESA/JAXA **BepiColombo** spacecraft, has delivered its first images. While BepiColombo is still on its complex journey to Mercury, these initial observations were taken from the inner solar system, providing crucial data on one of the brightest X-ray sources in the sky. This "first light" for MIXS is a significant milestone, confirming the instrument's health and functionality. Developed by teams at the University of Leicester and University College London's Mullard Space Science Laboratory, MIXS is designed to map the elemental composition of Mercury's surface once BepiColombo enters orbit around the innermost planet. These early results pave the way for a deeper understanding of Mercury's geological history and formation, offering tantalizing hints of the science to come.

### Night Sky Tip

For those with a clear view and a pair of binoculars, the ice giant **Uranus** is currently at opposition this Thanksgiving 2026. This means it's directly opposite the Sun in our sky, making it brighter and more easily observable throughout the night. Look for it in the evening sky, a faint, greenish-blue dot that reveals its planetary nature with a little optical aid.
