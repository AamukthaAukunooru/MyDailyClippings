Greetings, fellow stargazers and cosmic adventurers!

What a week it's been for our understanding of the universe, from groundbreaking missions reaching their destinations to powerful new insights into the lives of stars and the potential for future human endeavors beyond Earth. We're seeing our solar system reveal more of its secrets, while distant galaxies continue to paint a picture of cosmic evolution. It's a daily reminder that the cosmos is a dynamic, ever-unfolding story, and we are privileged to witness its chapters.

## Mercury's Embrace and Europa's Hidden Depths

After a journey spanning eight years and billions of kilometers, the European Space Agency and Japan Aerospace Exploration Agency's **BepiColombo** mission has successfully entered orbit around Mercury on November twenty-first. This incredible achievement marks a new era in our study of the solar system's innermost planet, a world of extreme temperatures and enigmatic geology. BepiColombo consists of two orbiters, ESA's Mercury Planetary Orbiter and JAXA's Mercury Magnetospheric Orbiter, which will now begin their detailed scientific investigations, aiming to unravel the planet's composition, magnetic field, and formation history. Understanding Mercury is key to understanding the early solar system, as it provides a unique window into the conditions that shaped our planetary neighborhood.

Meanwhile, a new revelation from Jupiter's moon **Europa** suggests its famous sodium cloud, observed by telescopes on Earth, originates not from surface ice, but from its vast, subsurface ocean. Scientists using the W.M. Keck Observatory's **HIRISE spectrometer** have provided compelling evidence for this internal source. This finding is incredibly significant because it strengthens the case for Europa's ocean being a potentially habitable environment. If sodium, a key element, is actively escaping from the ocean, it implies a dynamic interaction between the moon's rocky core, its liquid water, and the surface, processes that could support life. This research adds another layer of excitement as we anticipate missions like NASA's Europa Clipper, which will further explore this tantalizing ocean world.

## Cosmic Fireworks and Stellar Alchemy

The **James Webb Space Telescope** continues to astound us with its unparalleled views of the cosmos. Its latest observations of the legendary **supernova remnant Cassiopeia A**, or **Cas A**, have painted a vibrant tapestry of gas and dust, revealing how these stellar explosions are crucial for spreading key elements throughout space. Supernovae are the universe's ultimate recycling plants, forging heavy elements like iron, gold, and uranium in their fiery deaths and then scattering them across vast distances, enriching the interstellar medium from which new stars and planets, and even life itself, are formed. Webb's detailed infrared vision allows astronomers to trace these elements, providing a clearer picture of stellar evolution and the cosmic origins of everything around us.

Adding to our understanding of how elements spread, an international team of astronomers, led by Wouter Vlemmings and Miora Andriantsaralaza from Chalmers University, has imaged a nearby star in unprecedented detail using the **ALMA telescope** in Chile. This record-breaking eruption from the star provides direct evidence of how massive stars can rapidly distribute newly formed elements into their surroundings. These observations are vital for understanding the chemical evolution of galaxies, showing us the powerful mechanisms that seed the universe with the building blocks for future generations of stars and planetary systems.

## Powering Our Lunar Future

Looking ahead to humanity's sustained presence beyond Earth, NASA has announced a critical step towards establishing a permanent foothold on the Moon: they are seeking industry partners to design and test the first **nuclear reactor** specifically for lunar electricity generation. This ambitious project aims to provide a reliable, continuous power source for future lunar bases, overcoming the challenges of long lunar nights and extreme temperatures. Current plans envision a small, fission-based reactor that could power habitats, scientific instruments, and resource extraction operations, paving the way for astronauts to live and work on the Moon for extended periods. This initiative underscores a significant shift in space exploration, moving beyond short-term visits to building a truly sustainable human presence on another celestial body. The development of such a reactor is a monumental engineering challenge, but one that promises to unlock the full potential of lunar exploration and resource utilization.
