Greetings, fellow travelers of the cosmos! Today, we're diving into a fascinating blend of strategic shifts in space exploration, breathtaking new views of stellar birth, and a surprising re-discovery from the Martian past. NASA is charting a new course for scientific missions, while the James Webb Space Telescope continues to unveil the universe's hidden wonders. Meanwhile, a fresh look at decades-old data from Mars is revealing secrets about the Red Planet's interior.

## Charting New Courses: NASA's Future and Artemis's Ascent

Big news from NASA this week, as the agency announced a significant shift in its approach to selecting and competing for scientific missions. In a letter to the science community, NASA Science outlined its intention to deliver science faster, streamlining the process for future endeavors. This move aims to accelerate the pace of discovery and bring cutting-edge research to fruition more quickly. The agency plans to elaborate on these changes during a virtual NASA Science Town Hall on Monday, October twenty-sixth. This strategic pivot could reshape how we explore the universe, from Earth-observing satellites to deep-space probes.

Meanwhile, the journey back to the Moon under the **Artemis program** continues its steady progress. NASA recently conducted a critical test of the **RS-25 engine** No. 10001 at the Fred Haise Test Stand at NASA’s Stennis Space Center near Bay St. Louis, Mississippi. This developmental engine, a vital component of the powerful **Space Launch System** rocket, fired for an impressive eight and a half minutes, or five hundred seconds. These rigorous tests are essential to ensure the reliability and safety of the engines that will propel astronauts towards lunar orbit and beyond. In related news, NASA has also provided updated timelines for the Artemis III mission, outlining the expected progress for the lunar landers being developed by commercial partners SpaceX and Blue Origin. These updates bring us closer to the day when humanity once again sets foot on the lunar surface.

## Peering into Stellar Nurseries and Galactic History

The **James Webb Space Telescope** continues to astound us with its unparalleled vision, offering a fresh glimpse into the very beginnings of stars. A stunning infrared image, released on October sixth, 2026, captures the vibrant **star-forming region NGC 7129**, located approximately three thousand three hundred light-years from Earth. At the heart of this cosmic nursery lies **LkHα 234**, a young star estimated to be between five and eight times the mass of our Sun. Webb's infrared capabilities allow us to peer through the dense veils of gas and dust that obscure these nascent stars, revealing the intricate outflows that carve pathways through their stellar cocoons. Observing these early stages of stellar evolution helps astronomers understand the fundamental processes that lead to the birth of stars like our own Sun.

Closer to home, new research is finally shedding light on a long-standing mystery: how the **Milky Way's thin and thick disks formed**. This has been a "stubborn question" in astronomy, primarily because it relies on accurately determining the individual ages of countless stars. The European Space Agency's **Gaia mission**, with its unprecedented precision in mapping stellar positions and motions, has proven instrumental in this endeavor. New research, leveraging Gaia's extensive dataset, is now providing crucial insights into the complex evolutionary history of our home galaxy, helping us understand the dynamic forces that shaped the Milky Way into the spiral we see today.

## Mars' Ancient Tremors: A Viking 2 Reawakening

Decades after its mission ended, NASA's **Viking 2 lander** is still yielding scientific treasures. A team of researchers has reexamined signals from the seismometer aboard Viking 2, which landed on Mars in 1976. What was once dismissed as noise or instrument interference is now being interpreted as a possible **marsquake**. Intriguingly, this potential seismic event originated from a region that became geologically active decades later. This reanalysis highlights the immense value of archived data and the potential for new discoveries hidden within old observations. Understanding marsquakes is crucial for deciphering the Red Planet's internal structure and its geological evolution, much like how earthquakes provide invaluable clues about Earth's interior. This unexpected insight from a mission launched nearly fifty years ago reminds us that the universe, and even our neighboring planets, still hold countless secrets waiting to be uncovered.
