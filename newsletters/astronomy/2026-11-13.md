Greetings, fellow stargazers and cosmic explorers!

What a week it's been for peering into the universe's deepest secrets and pushing the boundaries of human ingenuity. From the first grand data release of a telescope designed to unravel the mysteries of the dark cosmos, to astonishing new insights from samples returned from an asteroid and an ancient Martian meteorite, our understanding of life's origins and the universe's vastness continues to expand. And closer to home, a monumental launch from SpaceX promises to redefine spaceflight.

## Euclid Unveils the Dark Universe

The European Space Agency's (ESA) **Euclid Space Telescope**, our dedicated "Dark Universe Detective," has just released its first major batch of data, named **DR1-Foundation**, on November 12, 2026. This is a monumental step in its mission to create the largest and most detailed three-dimensional map of the universe, specifically targeting the elusive **dark energy** and **dark matter** that constitute about 95 percent of our cosmos.

Imagine capturing the light from over a billion objects across an area of the sky as vast as nine thousand five hundred full moons. That's precisely what Euclid has achieved in its initial thirteen months of observations. This incredible dataset includes more than nine hundred million galaxies, approximately fifteen thousand candidates for **strong gravitational lensing**—where massive objects bend light from background galaxies, distorting their appearance—and over sixty million quasar candidates, the brilliant cores of distant galaxies powered by supermassive black holes. It also provides spectra from more than one hundred sixty million sources, offering a chemical fingerprint of these celestial bodies.

While this release is already immense, it represents only about 14 percent of the mission's total six-year survey target. ESA's Director of Science, Carole Mundell, emphasized the significance, stating that these are "far more than beautiful pictures; today marks a scientific milestone in our study of the cosmos." This foundational data will be crucial for scientists worldwide to begin constructing a detailed 3D map, providing unprecedented insights into the large-scale structure of the universe and helping us understand the fundamental forces that shape it.

## Cosmic Clues to Life's Origins: Bennu and Mars

Our solar system continues to surprise us with tantalizing clues about the building blocks of life. Researchers have been meticulously analyzing samples from **asteroid Bennu**, returned to Earth by NASA's **OSIRIS-REx mission** in 2023. A recent comparison with the famous Murchison meteorite revealed new insights into the amino acid **β-alanine**. The findings suggest that some of the precursors to life may have formed under a wider range of conditions and locations within the early protoplanetary disk than previously thought.

The Bennu samples are a treasure trove, containing fourteen of the twenty protein-building amino acids used by life on Earth, all five of the nucleotide bases found in DNA and RNA, and abundant ammonia. Intriguingly, the amino acids in Bennu exhibit a different isotopic pattern compared to Murchison, hinting at chemically distinct origins for their parent bodies in the solar system. This research challenges previous assumptions, suggesting that some amino acids might have formed in icy, radiation-exposed environments, not solely in warm liquid water.

Meanwhile, a rare 4.1-billion-year-old Martian meteorite, **Teghaza 001**, discovered in the Sahara Desert in Mali in 2022, is being hailed as a "Rosetta Stone for Mars." This ancient fragment of Mars' primordial crust contains significant amounts of **manganese oxide**, a compound commonly produced by microbial life on Earth. Analysis of tiny zircon crystals within the meteorite indicates that liquid water interacted with the rock around 4.1 billion years ago, implying that Mars had an active water cycle much earlier in its history than many scientists had suspected. This remarkable discovery offers a unique window into Mars' early, potentially more habitable past, at a time when life was just beginning on Earth.

## Starship Soars: A Historic Launch Attempt

Today, November 13, 2026, marks a potentially historic day for spaceflight as SpaceX targets the launch of its **Starship** megarocket for its fifteenth test flight from Starbase, South Texas. The launch window opens at 5:45 p.m. Eastern Standard Time, or 2245 Greenwich Mean Time.

What makes this flight particularly ambitious is SpaceX's plan to attempt a dual recovery: catching both the massive **Super Heavy booster** and the **Ship upper stage** using the "chopstick" arms of its "Mechazilla" launch tower. If successful, this would be the first time an orbital launch system has achieved full recovery of both stages, a critical step towards making space travel routine and affordable.

The Ship upper stage, designated Ship 42, is designed to orbit Earth approximately fourteen times over twenty-two hours, during which it will deploy twenty-six new **Starlink V3 satellites**. For skywatchers, the sheer size of the Ship upper stage, standing at one hundred seventy-one feet, or fifty-two meters, means it should be visible in twilight hours, appearing considerably brighter than the Starlink satellites themselves. This mission represents a bold leap forward in reusable rocket technology, bringing us closer to a future where humanity's reach into space is dramatically expanded.
