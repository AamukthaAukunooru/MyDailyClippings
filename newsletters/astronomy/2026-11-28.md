## Black Hole Jets in Unprecedented Detail

The **Event Horizon Telescope (EHT)** Collaboration, the same international team that gave us the first-ever image of a black hole in 2019, has once again pushed the boundaries of astronomical observation. Their latest work provides an incredibly detailed look at the dynamics and geometry of a black hole jet. These powerful jets, streams of superheated plasma traveling at nearly the speed of light, are among the most energetic phenomena in the universe. Understanding their structure and how they're launched from the vicinity of a black hole is crucial for unraveling fundamental physics. The EHT's ability to resolve these features in such fine detail offers astronomers a unique window into the extreme environment around a black hole's event horizon, the point of no return. This ongoing work by the EHT continues to revolutionize our understanding of these enigmatic cosmic giants.

## Hera Reveals DART's Impact Crater on Dimorphos

The European Space Agency's **Hera mission** has arrived at the site of humankind's first planetary defense experiment, and its initial findings are nothing short of astounding. Hera has revealed a stadium-sized crater on **Dimorphos**, the small asteroid that was deliberately impacted by NASA's DART (Double Asteroid Redirection Test) spacecraft in September 2022. Dimorphos, which orbits a larger asteroid named Didymos, is a miniature world, only about 160 meters (525 feet) across. The DART mission successfully altered Dimorphos's orbit, proving that humanity has the capability to deflect an asteroid if one were ever found on a collision course with Earth. Hera's detailed observations of the impact crater provide invaluable data, allowing scientists to better understand the asteroid's composition and how it responded to the kinetic impact. This information is vital for refining our planetary defense strategies and ensuring we can protect our home planet from future cosmic threats.

## A Million New Satellites: A Growing Concern for Astronomy

While we celebrate incredible scientific advancements, a significant concern is emerging for the future of ground-based astronomy and the pristine view of our night sky. The **European Southern Observatory (ESO)** has expressed deep dismay over the approval of SpaceX's new satellite AI system, dubbed **Starmind**. This system reportedly calls for an additional one million satellites in Low Earth Orbit. The sheer number of these proposed satellites raises serious alarms for astronomers worldwide. Large constellations of satellites, even those designed to be less reflective, can interfere with astronomical observations by creating streaks of light across telescope images and contributing to overall sky brightness. This **light pollution** from orbital sources threatens to obscure our view of the distant universe, impacting everything from fundamental research into galaxy formation to the search for exoplanets. The ESO's concerns highlight a critical debate about the sustainable use of space and the balance between technological advancement and the preservation of our cosmic heritage.
