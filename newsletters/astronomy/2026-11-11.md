## Unveiling the Universe's Earliest Giants: Quasar Pair Challenges Black Hole Growth Theories

Imagine peering back in time to just 900 million years after the Big Bang, when the universe was still in its infancy. That's precisely what astronomers have done, discovering the earliest known pair of compact **quasars** ever observed. Quasars are incredibly luminous active galactic nuclei, powered by supermassive black holes voraciously feeding on surrounding gas and dust. This newly found duo presents a significant puzzle: scientists currently lack a theoretical explanation for how these two actively feeding black holes managed to grow so massive so quickly in the early universe.

This discovery challenges our current understanding of how **supermassive black holes** form and evolve. Typically, such colossal objects are thought to require billions of years to accumulate their immense mass. Finding a compact pair so early in cosmic history suggests that the mechanisms for black hole growth might be more efficient or different than previously theorized. This finding, reported on Phys.org Space, opens new avenues for research into the conditions of the early universe and the fundamental processes that shaped galaxies and their central black holes.

## Ripples in Spacetime: LISA to Hunt for Exoplanets with Gravitational Waves

The universe is a symphony of subtle vibrations, and some of the most profound are **gravitational waves**, tiny ripples in the fabric of **spacetime** caused by accelerating massive objects. While these waves are often associated with dramatic events like colliding black holes, new research led by NASA astrophysicists shows they could also be a powerful tool for discovering **exoplanets**.

The upcoming **LISA (Laser Interferometer Space Antenna) observatory**, currently in development, is designed to detect these elusive gravitational waves. Two recent studies highlight how LISA could identify exoplanets by observing the subtle gravitational ripples created by their orbital dance around their host stars. This innovative approach offers a completely new way to search for worlds beyond our solar system, complementing traditional methods that rely on light. By detecting the gravitational signature of orbiting masses, LISA could reveal exoplanets that are otherwise hidden from view, potentially revolutionizing our understanding of planetary systems across the galaxy.

## Voyager 1: A Light-Day Away and Still Exploring

In a testament to human ingenuity and perseverance, NASA's **Voyager 1** spacecraft is about to achieve another remarkable milestone: on November 18, it will become the first human-made object to reach one **light-day** from Earth. Launched way back in 1977, this venerable probe has been on an interstellar journey for nearly five decades, continuously sending back data from the farthest reaches of our solar system and beyond.

To put this into perspective, one light-day is an astounding distance, equivalent to more than 16 billion miles, or 25.9 billion kilometers. Voyager 1 is currently the most distant human-made object, a silent ambassador of Earth venturing into the vast unknown. Its journey continues to provide invaluable insights into the heliosphere, the protective bubble of particles and magnetic fields created by our Sun, and the interstellar medium beyond. This milestone serves as a powerful reminder of our insatiable drive to explore and understand our place in the cosmos. NASA is even inviting the public to join in activities to celebrate this incredible achievement.

### Night Sky Update

While there are no comets currently visible from India with the naked eye or binoculars, stargazers have a treat in store this month. Keep an eye out for bright **Jupiter** and red **Mars**, which are drawing closer together in the night sky. They will appear closest between November 15 and 17, offering a stunning celestial conjunction that is easily visible to the naked eye and even more impressive through binoculars.
