## Advancing Our Reach and Vision

Humanity's quest to explore and understand space is accelerating, with major milestones being reached in both robotic and crewed missions. NASA has officially committed to the **UltraViolet EXplorer, or UVEX**, a new space telescope slated for launch in 2031. This next-generation observatory promises to be about one hundred times more powerful than its predecessor, GALEX, which concluded its mission in 2013. UVEX will conduct an all-sky survey in both near and far ultraviolet light, offering unprecedented insights into the evolution of low-mass, low-metallicity galaxies, which are thought to resemble the universe's earliest galaxies. It will also be capable of rapidly responding to transient cosmic events, such as the bright, fast ultraviolet emission from neutron star mergers and cosmic explosions, providing crucial complementary data to observatories like the Vera C. Rubin Observatory and the Nancy Grace Roman Space Telescope. The mission's principal investigator is Fiona Harrison at Caltech in Pasadena, California.

Closer to home, NASA's **Artemis III Space Launch System (SLS) core stage** has been integrated with its twin solid rocket boosters at the Kennedy Space Center in Florida. This monumental 64-meter, or 212-foot, core stage was carefully lifted and positioned in the Vehicle Assembly Building's High Bay 3 on October twenty-eighth, marking a critical step toward the mission's planned launch in 2027. Artemis III aims to send NASA astronauts Randy Bresnik, Frank Rubio, Andre Douglas, and European Space Agency astronaut Luca Parmitano aboard the Orion spacecraft to test rendezvous and docking capabilities with commercial spacecraft, a vital precursor for future lunar landings.

Meanwhile, the **Vera C. Rubin Observatory** in Chile, even before its decade-long Legacy Survey of Space and Time officially begins, has already made a significant discovery: a new **dwarf planet candidate** named **2026 ED138**. This icy object, located beyond Pluto's orbit at about forty-six astronomical units from the Sun, is estimated to be between six hundred and one thousand five hundred kilometers in diameter. If confirmed, ED138 would be among the largest dwarf planets discovered beyond Neptune, offering a tantalizing glimpse into the outer reaches of our solar system. The discovery was made by Rubin Operations team member Matthew Holman from the Center for Astrophysics, Harvard and Smithsonian.

In a significant move for international collaboration, the Socialist Republic of **Vietnam** has become the seventy-eighth nation to sign the **Artemis Accords**. This growing coalition of nations is committed to the peaceful, transparent, and responsible exploration of space, guided by principles established in 2020 by NASA and the U.S. Department of State. Vietnam's Minister of Science and Technology, Vũ Hải Quân, signed the accords on October twenty-eighth, further extending the U.S.-Vietnam Comprehensive Strategic Partnership into this new frontier.

## Unraveling Deep Space Mysteries

Our understanding of the cosmos continues to deepen with remarkable scientific breakthroughs, challenging long-held theories and revealing the intricate workings of the universe. The **James Webb Space Telescope** has delivered another stunning image, this time of **N159**, a turbulent **star nursery** located one hundred sixty-one thousand light-years away in the **Large Magellanic Cloud**. This region, spanning about two hundred thirty light-years, is a vibrant H II region filled with ionized hydrogen gas, where massive, hot stars are actively forming. Webb's Near-Infrared Camera (NIRCam) reveals intricate structures carved by fierce stellar winds and dense filaments of cool molecular hydrogen, which shield newborn stars from harsh radiation.

In a significant advance for solar physics, a team of researchers has produced the first global, **three-dimensional estimates of the Sun's outer magnetic field**. This breakthrough, achieved by scientists from the U.S. National Science Foundation's National Solar Observatory, the National Center for Atmospheric Research, and Southwest Research Institute, uses a novel method to decode information from light and waves in the Sun's outer atmosphere, or corona. This new technique, which builds on previous two-dimensional maps and a method called IQUD inversion, allows scientists to reconstruct the elusive three-dimensional coronal magnetic field, including areas beyond the Sun's visible edge. This is a crucial step toward more accurate models of solar activity and better predictions of **solar magnetic eruptions**, which are the root cause of extreme space weather that can impact critical infrastructure on Earth. Lead author Alin Paraschiv, an NSF NSO researcher, noted that this allows us to "begin to see the Sun's hidden magnetic architecture in three dimensions" without new instruments, but by decoding existing data.

Further out in the galaxy, a promising new theory has emerged to explain the puzzling, close-in orbits of **hot Jupiters and Neptunes**. Led by J. J. Zanazzi, a team of astronomers in the U.S. used advanced simulations to propose that **supersonic waves** within these giant, gaseous exoplanets play a key role in their migration. These waves, excited by tidal forces during highly eccentric orbits, can shock the planets' gaseous envelopes, leading to orbital circularization and explaining the observed clustering of these planets in what are known as the "hot Jupiter pile-up" and "Neptune ridge," as well as the "Neptune desert" where such planets are scarce.

Finally, a new study challenges a long-standing hypothesis about the **origins of life on Earth**, suggesting that **comets probably did not seed our planet with organic compounds** after all. Researchers at the University of Cambridge, led by Dr. Richard Anslow, used computer modeling to investigate the delivery of molecules like hydrogen cyanide, a crucial ingredient for prebiotic chemistry. Their findings, published in *Scientific Reports*, indicate that comets typically travel too fast and at too steep an angle for fragile organic molecules to survive atmospheric entry and impact with Earth's surface, often vaporizing on collision. While rare successful deliveries remain possible, the simulations offered little support for a steady supply of these building blocks from comets, suggesting that the "romantic idea" of comets as life's couriers is "highly unlikely".
