Today, we celebrate a monumental leap in space exploration as Japan launches its MMX mission to Mars' moons, aiming to bring back samples that could rewrite our understanding of the solar system. Meanwhile, new research challenges our long-held assumptions about the formation of our own gas giants, Uranus and Neptune, revealing they might not be such identical twins after all. And further afield, astronomers have captured a stunning, rare glimpse of a "cosmic hamburger," offering unprecedented insights into the earliest stages of giant planet formation around a distant star.

## Japan's Historic Journey to Mars' Moons

This is a big one for space exploration! Japan's Aerospace Exploration Agency, **JAXA**, has successfully launched its **Martian Moons eXploration, or MMX, mission**. This ambitious spacecraft is now on its way to Mars' two enigmatic moons, Phobos and Deimos, with a groundbreaking goal: to collect samples from Phobos and return them to Earth. If successful, this would mark the first time humanity has ever brought samples back from the Martian system, a feat that promises to unlock profound secrets about the origins of our solar system and potentially even life itself.

The MMX mission, led by JAXA Project Manager Yasuhiro Kawakatsu, lifted off on Tuesday, October 19, 2026, at 3:41 p.m. Eastern time (4:41 a.m. local time on October 20 in Japan). This mission is a testament to international collaboration, involving contributions from NASA, the French Centre National d'Études Spatiales, or CNES, the German Aerospace Center, or DLR, the European Space Agency, and the Australian Space Agency. The spacecraft is expected to arrive at Mars in 2027, spending several years studying both Phobos and Deimos before attempting a daring landing on Phobos. The plan is to collect at least ten grams, or about one-third of an ounce, of surface material using a pneumatic sampling mechanism. These precious samples are then slated to return to Earth in 2031, where scientists worldwide, including Principal Investigator Kiyoshi Kuramoto, will meticulously analyze them.

Why is this so crucial? Phobos and Deimos are small, irregularly shaped bodies, with Phobos measuring about fourteen miles wide and Deimos about seven-and-a-half miles wide. Their exact origins remain a mystery. Are they captured asteroids, or did they form from debris ejected after a giant impact on Mars, similar to how our own Moon is thought to have formed? By analyzing pristine samples, scientists hope to distinguish between these theories, providing critical data on the early conditions of the solar system and the processes that led to planet formation. This mission isn't just about Mars; it's about understanding our cosmic neighborhood and our place within it.

## Uranus and Neptune: Not So Identical Twins After All

For decades, Uranus and Neptune have been considered planetary "twins," often grouped together as **ice giants** due to their similar size, mass, and distant orbits. However, new research is challenging this long-held assumption, suggesting these two icy worlds may have surprisingly different origin stories. A new study, with contributions from a scientist at the **Southwest Research Institute**, details how Neptune's deep atmosphere appears substantially richer in oxygen than Uranus's. This subtle but crucial difference hints at distinct formation pathways in the early solar system.

The researchers contrasted the two planets' chemical compositions using atmospheric chemistry models and observations of carbon monoxide levels, including recent measurements from the Atacama Large Millimeter/Submillimeter Array, or ALMA. They found that Neptune's deep atmosphere has more oxygen, resulting in significantly different carbon-to-oxygen ratios between the two planets. As Dr. Olivier Mousis, a planetary scientist, noted, "We can't directly access the deep atmospheres of Uranus and Neptune, but chemical tracers allow us to probe the conditions hidden beneath their visible clouds". Studying carbon monoxide, or CO, is particularly useful for modeling the chemical evolution of the protoplanetary disk and the origins of its materials.

While both planets likely formed from the same primordial disk of gas and dust, variations in the timing or location of their accretion, or even the types of planetesimals they incorporated, could account for this chemical divergence. Understanding these differences is vital for refining our models of giant planet formation, not just in our solar system but across the cosmos. It underscores the complexity and diversity of planetary evolution, reminding us that even seemingly similar worlds can harbor unique histories. This research pushes the boundaries of our knowledge about these distant, enigmatic worlds, which remain largely unexplored by spacecraft.

## The Cosmic 'Hamburger': A Glimpse into Giant Planet Nurseries

Peering into the vast cosmic kitchen where planets are cooked up, astronomers have made a remarkable discovery using the Atacama Large Millimeter/Submillimeter Array, or ALMA. They've captured a rare, edge-on view of a system known as **Gomez's Hamburger**, revealing what appears to be the earliest phases of giant planet formation. This celestial object, officially designated IRAS 18059-3211, is a young star surrounded by a thick, dusty disk, seen almost perfectly sideways from our vantage point, giving it its distinctive "hamburger" appearance.

What makes this observation so exciting is the detection of dense layers of gas and dust within this disk, where giant planets are thought to be actively taking shape. These layers are essentially the raw ingredients for planets, accumulating and coalescing under gravity. Gomez's Hamburger is a truly supersized system, with its gas disk stretching out to almost one thousand **astronomical units** in radius, making it one of the largest known planet-forming disks. For context, one astronomical unit is the average distance between the Earth and the Sun, about ninety-three million miles.

The ALMA observations, with their incredible resolution of about 0.2 arcseconds, allow scientists to map the distribution of various molecules and dust grains within the disk, including different forms of carbon monoxide and sulfur-bearing molecules like CS and SO. This detailed chemical mapping helps us understand the physical conditions, such as temperature and density, in these planet-forming regions. For instance, the detection of a narrow, one-sided arc of SO emission suggests localized heating around an early-stage giant protoplanet or disk fragment. Observing such a system edge-on provides an unparalleled opportunity to study the vertical structure of these **protoplanetary disks**, offering clues about how material is distributed and how it eventually forms massive worlds like Jupiter or Saturn. This direct evidence of giant planet formation in its nascent stages is a crucial piece of the puzzle in understanding the diversity of exoplanetary systems we observe throughout the galaxy.
