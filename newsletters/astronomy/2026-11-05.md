Welcome to My Daily Clippings, your personal dive into the cosmos. This week, we're marveling at fresh hints of a geologically active Mars, celebrating breakthroughs in mapping the universe's vast hydrogen networks, and tracking humanity's accelerating return to the Moon. We also bid a fiery farewell to a long-serving space telescope and welcome a new experimental aircraft designed to push the boundaries of flight.

## Martian Mysteries and Lunar Ambitions

Our understanding of Mars took an exciting turn this week with new evidence suggesting the Red Planet might not be as geologically inert as once thought. A European orbiter has detected fresh hints of **volcanic activity** on Mars, with gases seeping from fissures in the **Alba Mons region**. This suggests that Mars' interior might be merely sleeping, rather than entirely dead, a discovery that could reshape our understanding of the planet's evolution and potential for past or present life. Coincidentally, four participants in NASA's **Mars habitat simulation** emerged on October thirty-first after a year-long test, providing invaluable data for what future astronauts might encounter on a real mission to Mars.

Advancing our ability to explore other worlds, NASA successfully demonstrated new technology for **autonomous planetary landings**. An Alta-X drone, equipped with the agency's **Safe and Precise Landing, SPL, – Integrated Capabilities, IC,** system, showed promise for helping spacecraft navigate to safe landing sites on the Moon and other planets. This is crucial as global lunar ambitions intensify. Students and researchers at the University of Alabama in Huntsville also contributed to future lunar operations by successfully testing a prototype **lunar landing pad** this summer, demonstrating its ability to withstand rocket motor firings.

The race to the Moon is certainly heating up. Yesterday, on November third, China unveiled its detailed plans for a **crewed Moon landing**, outlining its ambitious program. Not to be outdone, NASA Administrator Jared Isaacman and other agency leaders provided an update on October twenty-third, sharing NASA's accelerated path to returning Americans to the lunar surface by **2028** as part of the **Artemis program**.

## Unveiling the Universe's Grand Design

Beyond our immediate cosmic neighborhood, scientists are making strides in mapping the universe itself. The **MeerKLASS Collaboration**, an international research project, reported its first measurement of the large-scale clustering of **cosmic hydrogen**. This marks a significant breakthrough in advancing a technique that brings scientists closer to mapping the universe's vast structures. Understanding how hydrogen, the most abundant element, is distributed across the cosmos is fundamental to comprehending the universe's evolution.

Further back in cosmic time, observations from the **James Webb Space Telescope** revealed an unexpected abundance of bright galaxies in the early universe. New research suggests that **light-propelled winds** from massive stars acted like a "cosmic leaf blower," clearing gas and dust from these young galaxies and allowing their light to shine brightly, helping to explain this puzzling observation. Meanwhile, the venerable **Hubble Space Telescope** continues to deliver stunning images and scientific insights. A photo released on November second showcases **NGC 4449**, a dwarf irregular galaxy, as researchers used Hubble to study thirty-seven nearby dwarf galaxies, which contain a few thousand to hundreds of millions of stars.

And speaking of planets, new research is challenging long-held assumptions about how rocky planets retain their atmospheres. For a long time, it was thought that giant impacts would strip away a young planet's atmosphere. However, it turns out that these impacts might actually help rocky planets retain their atmospheres by adding mass and gravitational pull, a fascinating twist in our understanding of planetary formation.

## Orbital Farewells and Earthly Glimpses

After twenty-two years of dedicated service, NASA's **Swift space telescope** made a fiery plunge back to Earth on Wednesday, November fourth, ending its mission to investigate the universe's biggest explosions. The telescope, which studied **gamma-ray bursts**, re-entered the atmosphere after a failed attempt three months prior by a private spacecraft to boost it to a higher orbit. Swift's re-entry was captured on video, appearing as a blazing streak of light.

Closer to home, Earth has gained a new temporary companion: a **mini-moon** designated **2026 QQ8**. This small space rock began orbiting Earth in April and is expected to remain with us through 2026. These transient mini-moons are fascinating natural laboratories for studying orbital mechanics.

In aeronautical news, NASA unveiled its newest experimental aircraft, the **X-70 Fractal**, on Wednesday at the Kennedy Space Center in Florida. This high-performance, highly adaptable research platform is designed to advance flight research, pushing the boundaries of aviation technology.

Finally, for those looking up tonight, the **Southern Taurid meteor shower** is peaking around November fourth and fifth. While it's a minor shower, it's known for producing bright **fireballs**. Look towards the constellation Taurus around midnight for the best chance to spot these slow-moving, luminous streaks. Also, keep an eye out for **Venus**, which will be a brilliant morning star throughout November. On the morning of November fifth, it will be visible with a waning crescent Moon and the star **Spica** in the east-southeast sky before sunrise.
