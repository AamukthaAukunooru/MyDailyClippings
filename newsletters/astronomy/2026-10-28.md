## A New Dwarf Planet Candidate Beyond Pluto

Our solar system might just be getting a little bigger, or at least, a little more crowded in its distant reaches. Scientists have announced the discovery of a new **dwarf planet candidate**, provisionally named **2026 ED138**, orbiting beyond Neptune and Pluto. This icy body was spotted earlier this month by a scientist using data from the **Rubin Observatory**, a groundbreaking facility designed to survey the entire visible sky.

The Rubin Observatory, with its immense 8.4-meter (27.6-foot) primary mirror, is uniquely suited for detecting faint, moving objects like this one. Its wide field of view and rapid survey capabilities allow astronomers to map the dynamic universe, revealing objects that have long eluded detection. The discovery of 2026 ED138 is a testament to the observatory's power and promises many more such findings as it continues its operations. While 2026 ED138 still needs to undergo further observations and official classification by the International Astronomical Union, its presence adds another intriguing piece to the puzzle of our solar system's outer reaches, hinting at a rich population of icy worlds waiting to be discovered in the Kuiper Belt and beyond. Understanding these distant objects helps us piece together the history of our solar system's formation and evolution.

## Our Solar System's Future: A Glimpse from a Dead Star

Imagine our Sun, billions of years from now, having exhausted its nuclear fuel and shed its outer layers, leaving behind a dense, cooling remnant known as a **white dwarf**. What happens to the planets that once orbited it? New observations from the European Southern Observatory's **Very Large Telescope (ESO's VLT)** offer a compelling glimpse into this cosmic future. Astronomers have discovered a rare **double debris disk** orbiting a dead star.

Unlike other similar systems, this particular white dwarf is surrounded by not one, but two distinct rings of rocky and icy debris. This configuration provides crucial clues about how planetary systems evolve after their central star dies. The inner disk, closer to the white dwarf, likely consists of material from asteroids and smaller bodies that were disrupted by the star's transformation. The outer disk, further out, could be remnants of larger, more distant objects, perhaps even former planets, that survived the star's violent death throes but are now slowly being pulled apart. This discovery, made by a team of astronomers using the VLT's powerful instruments, is invaluable for understanding the long-term fate of planetary systems, including our own. It suggests that even after a star's demise, the echoes of its planetary family can persist for eons, offering a profound look at the cyclical nature of cosmic existence.

## A Year on Mars, Without Leaving Earth

Humanity's journey to Mars took another significant step this week, as four dedicated volunteers completed a year-long **Mars simulation** mission. These "analog astronauts" have spent 378 days in isolation within a specialized habitat at NASA's Johnson Space Center, meticulously simulating the challenges and routines of a long-duration mission to the Red Planet.

The mission, part of NASA's **Crew Health and Performance Exploration Analog (CHAPEA)** program, is designed to gather critical data on the psychological and physiological effects of extended space travel. Living in a 1,700-square-foot (158-square-meter) habitat, the crew faced simulated equipment failures, communication delays, and resource limitations, all while conducting scientific research and maintaining their physical and mental well-being. This rigorous experiment provides invaluable insights into the human factors of deep-space exploration, helping NASA and its partners develop the strategies and technologies needed to keep future Mars crews safe and productive. The data collected from this year-long endeavor will directly inform mission planning for actual human expeditions to Mars, bringing us closer to the day when astronauts truly set foot on another planet.
