Greetings, fellow stargazers and cosmic adventurers! Today, we're celebrating a monumental achievement in planetary exploration, delving into groundbreaking discoveries that are reshaping our understanding of the universe, and looking ahead to the technologies that will enable humanity's future among the stars. From a long-awaited arrival at our solar system's innermost planet to a new map of the highest-energy light in the cosmos, the past few days have been a testament to human ingenuity and our insatiable curiosity about the universe.

## BepiColombo's Grand Arrival at Mercury

After an incredible eight-year journey spanning nine billion kilometers, the European-Japanese **BepiColombo** mission has successfully entered orbit around Mercury, the solar system's smallest and innermost planet. This marks a triumphant culmination of years of meticulous planning and complex orbital maneuvers. The mission, a collaboration between the European Space Agency (ESA) and the Japan Aerospace Exploration Agency (JAXA), consists of two scientific orbiters: ESA's Mercury Planetary Orbiter (MPO) and JAXA's Mercury Magnetospheric Orbiter (Mio). These twin spacecraft will now begin their detailed study of Mercury's mysterious surface, internal structure, magnetic field, and exosphere.

Mercury is an extreme world, with surface temperatures swinging from a scorching 430 degrees Celsius (800 degrees Fahrenheit) during the day to a frigid minus 180 degrees Celsius (minus 290 degrees Fahrenheit) at night. Its proximity to the Sun, just 58 million kilometers (36 million miles) on average, makes it a challenging target for spacecraft. BepiColombo's mission is crucial for understanding how rocky planets form and evolve, especially those close to their parent stars. The data collected will provide unprecedented insights into this enigmatic world, helping us piece together the story of our solar system's formation.

## Unveiling the Universe's Deepest Secrets

Astronomers are making strides in understanding the most energetic phenomena in the cosmos. NASA's **Fermi Gamma-ray Space Telescope** has completed a 14-year all-sky map, effectively bridging a long-standing "MeV gap" in astronomy. Gamma rays, the most energetic form of light, have been notoriously difficult to measure in the megaelectronvolt (MeV) energy range. This new map provides an unprecedented view of the universe in these elusive energies, revealing previously hidden details about pulsars, black holes, and other extreme cosmic events. This achievement by the Fermi team is akin to gaining a new sense, allowing us to perceive parts of the universe that were previously invisible.

Meanwhile, a radical new theory about the universe's age and composition is making waves. A study from the University of Ottawa suggests an alternative model where the universe is 26.7 billion years old, significantly older than the widely accepted 13.8 billion years. What's more, this theory proposes that the universe may not require **dark matter** or **dark energy** to explain its expansion and structure, two mysterious components that currently dominate cosmological models. While still a radical idea, this model has successfully passed several major cosmological tests, prompting scientists to re-examine our fundamental understanding of the cosmos. Such bold new perspectives are vital for pushing the boundaries of scientific inquiry and could lead to a profound shift in our cosmic worldview.

## Paving the Way for Future Exploration

Our ambitions for exploring the Moon and Mars are being fueled by innovative technological advancements and continued robotic reconnaissance. Researchers are developing a new method to extract **oxygen from lunar soil**, a critical step toward establishing sustainable human bases on the Moon. This technique, drawing parallels from computer chip manufacturing, could provide astronauts with a vital resource for breathing and rocket propellant, significantly reducing the cost and complexity of lunar missions.

On Mars, NASA's **Curiosity rover** continues its diligent work, recently capturing images of drifting twilight clouds in the Martian atmosphere. These post-sunset clouds, observed on the 5,061st Martian day of its mission, offer valuable insights into the Red Planet's atmospheric dynamics. Curiosity has also been busy collecting samples, with a collage of 49 drill holes showcasing its extensive geological survey of the Martian surface. These samples are crucial for understanding Mars's past habitability. Complementing Curiosity's efforts, the **Perseverance rover** has been examining low-viscosity lavas in Jezero Crater, providing clues about Mars's ancient volcanic activity and geological history. These ongoing missions are meticulously charting Mars's past, paving the way for future human missions.

As we look up at the night sky, remember that the **Leonid meteor shower** is still active until November 30th, even though its peak has passed. While you might not see the spectacular rates of its peak on November 17th and 18th, you can still catch occasional bright streaks of light across the sky. The best time to look is after midnight and before dawn, away from city lights, allowing your eyes to adjust to the darkness. No special equipment is needed, just a comfortable spot to lie back and watch the celestial show.
