Here are today's cosmic highlights, a blend of profound theoretical shifts and tangible steps toward our future in space. We're diving into a radical new theory that could redefine the universe's age and composition, alongside crucial discussions about the responsible development of lunar resources. Further afield, new discoveries are shedding light on Jupiter's ocean moon Europa and revealing bizarre stellar systems, while our Mars rovers continue their vital work, even amidst a few technical hiccups.

## Rethinking the Cosmos and Our Lunar Future

A groundbreaking new study from the University of Ottawa is challenging fundamental tenets of modern cosmology, proposing a universe that is significantly older and potentially free of the need for both **dark matter** and **dark energy**. This "radical theory," led by Professor Rajendra Gupta, suggests the universe could be 26.7 billion years old, nearly twice the age estimated by the standard cosmological model. This model, known as the **Lambda-CDM model**, posits a universe that is 13.8 billion years old and composed largely of mysterious dark matter and dark energy to explain observed phenomena like galactic rotation and accelerated expansion. Gupta's research, published in *Monthly Notices of the Royal Astronomical Society*, combines the "tired light" theory, where photons lose energy over vast cosmic distances, with the concept of evolving coupling constants, which govern how fundamental forces interact. This alternative framework has successfully passed several major cosmological tests, offering a compelling, albeit controversial, new perspective on the universe's grand narrative.

Closer to home, the moon is becoming a focal point for both scientific exploration and resource utilization, prompting scientists to call for a moratorium on industrial exploitation. Researchers are expressing concerns that rapid, unregulated development could lead to irreversible damage to the lunar environment, warning against turning our celestial neighbor into a "Wild West". This call for caution comes as new research highlights innovative ways to extract vital resources from the lunar surface. One promising development involves a novel method for extracting **oxygen from lunar soil**, a critical step toward supporting long-term human bases on the moon. This technique, which draws surprising parallels to computer chip manufacturing, could be key to making lunar outposts self-sufficient, reducing our reliance on costly resupply missions from Earth. Balancing the immense potential of lunar resources with the imperative to protect its pristine environment will be a defining challenge for space agencies and private companies in the coming decades.

## Unveiling Europa's Secrets and Stellar Oddities

Far beyond Earth, Jupiter's enigmatic moon **Europa** continues to captivate scientists with its hidden global ocean, believed to harbor conditions potentially suitable for life. New observations from the W. M. Keck Observatory on Maunakea, Hawaiʻi Island, have revealed the oceanic origin of Europa's **sodium cloud**. This discovery provides direct evidence that material from Europa's subsurface ocean is making its way into space, offering a tantalizing glimpse into the composition of this vast, sub-ice sea. Understanding the chemistry of Europa's ocean is crucial for assessing its habitability and guiding future missions, like NASA's Europa Clipper, which will conduct detailed investigations of this icy world.

Meanwhile, astronomers are peering into the intricate lives of stars, uncovering systems that challenge our current understanding of stellar evolution. One such discovery is a **triple star system** located just 28 light-years from Earth that, according to astronomers, "shouldn't exist". This unusual configuration defies conventional models of how stars form and evolve, particularly concerning the dynamics of **common envelopes**, where stars share a vast cloud of gas. Such systems provide invaluable natural laboratories for testing and refining our theories about stellar birth and death. In a related breakthrough, astronomers have witnessed for the first time a **stellar merger** within a common envelope. This rare observation provides direct evidence of a phenomenon where two stars, spiraling inwards within a shared gaseous envelope, eventually coalesce, offering crucial insights into the violent and transformative processes that shape binary star systems.

## Mars Missions: Glitches and Glimpses

Our robotic emissaries on Mars continue their tireless work, though not without their challenges. NASA's **Perseverance rover** has temporarily halted its science operations due to an issue with its antenna, while the venerable **Odyssey orbiter** is contending with a balky reaction wheel. NASA engineers are actively working to resolve these glitches, hoping to get both spacecraft back to full science capacity by the end of November. These incidents highlight the inherent difficulties of operating complex machinery millions of miles from Earth, where even minor technical issues can cause significant delays.

Despite these setbacks, other Martian explorers are still delivering fascinating data. NASA’s **Curiosity Mars rover**, now in its 5,061st Martian day, or **sol**, of operation, recently captured images of **drifting twilight clouds** on October 31, 2026. These post-sunset clouds offer clues about the Martian atmosphere and its dynamics. Curiosity has also been diligently collecting samples, with a collage of 49 drill holes captured as of November 2026, each representing a sample taken from rocks on the Martian surface. These precious samples are crucial for understanding Mars's geological history and its potential for past habitability.
