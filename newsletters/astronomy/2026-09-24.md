## Peering into the Cosmos: From Baby Worlds to Dark Secrets

Astronomers have unveiled the youngest known exoplanet to date, a "baby world" named Elias 2-24 b. This gas giant, about twice the mass of Jupiter, resides approximately 450 light-years away and was directly imaged, a remarkable feat given its youth. Direct imaging allows scientists to capture the actual light from these distant worlds, providing crucial insights into their formation and early evolution. This discovery offers a unique opportunity to witness planetary birth in action, helping us understand how gas giants, like those in our own solar system, coalesce from swirling disks of dust and gas around young stars.

Further enhancing our cosmic vision, NASA has selected PRIMA, the PRobe far-Infrared Mission for Astrophysics, as the first in a new class of missions. This space telescope will explore the universe's history and evolution by observing in the far-infrared spectrum. This wavelength range is critical for studying the cold dust and gas where stars and galaxies are born, often obscured by visible light. PRIMA promises to open a new window into the universe's earliest epochs, tracing the cosmic tapestry from its nascent stages to its present-day grandeur.

Meanwhile, the mysteries of dark matter, the invisible scaffolding that holds galaxies together, continue to tantalize. Astrophysicists from The University of Hong Kong and Beijing Normal University have developed a new, more realistic approach to test "ultralight," or "fuzzy," dark matter using wave simulations. This innovative method treats dark matter not as discrete particles, but as a wave, which could explain its distribution in galaxies. By simulating how these waves interact, scientists hope to gain a deeper understanding of this enigmatic substance that constitutes roughly 27% of the universe's mass but remains undetected by conventional means.

Our venerable workhorse, the Hubble Space Telescope, recently celebrated a monumental achievement, completing its 200,000th orbit around Earth on September 19. In addition to this milestone, Hubble is actively searching for a rare, gravitationally lensed supernova within the massive galaxy cluster MACS J0417. Gravitational lensing, predicted by Einstein, occurs when a massive object, like a galaxy cluster, bends and magnifies the light from a more distant object behind it, acting like a cosmic magnifying glass. Observing such a lensed supernova could provide unprecedented details about the star's explosion and the expansion rate of the universe.

## Our Cosmic Neighborhood: Unraveling Origins and Setting Sail

Closer to home, the analysis of samples from the asteroid Bennu has yielded a surprising revelation: its origin may involve Jupiter. Bennu, which orbits the Sun every 1.2 years and swings past Earth every six years, was the target of NASA's OSIRIS-REx mission, which successfully collected material from its surface. The unexpected connection to Jupiter suggests a more dynamic and complex early solar system than previously thought, where gravitational interactions with the gas giant could have significantly influenced the migration and composition of small bodies like Bennu. This insight into asteroid origins is vital for understanding the building blocks of our solar system and the delivery of water and organic molecules to early Earth.

The European Space Agency’s Jupiter Icy Moons Explorer, JUICE, is making its way to the outer solar system and will perform a crucial Earth swing-by on September 28. This gravity assist maneuver will use Earth's gravitational pull to accelerate the spacecraft and adjust its trajectory, slingshotting it towards its ultimate destination: Jupiter and its fascinating icy moons, Ganymede, Callisto, and Europa. These moons are prime targets in the search for extraterrestrial life, as they are believed to harbor vast subsurface oceans. The swing-by is a testament to the intricate orbital mechanics that make interplanetary travel possible, allowing us to send probes across billions of kilometers.

Finally, a look at our own Moon is providing clues to its distant past. Unlike Earth, the Moon no longer possesses a global magnetic field generated by a molten core. However, new findings from the far side of the Moon point to a past magnetic field. On Earth, our geodynamo, the movement of liquid iron in the outer core, generates a protective magnetic field. Evidence of a past lunar magnetic field suggests that the Moon's interior was once far more active, a crucial piece of the puzzle in understanding its thermal evolution and how it lost its internal dynamo, shaping its present-day barren landscape.
