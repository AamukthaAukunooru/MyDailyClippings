**Interviewer:** Welcome! Today, we're designing TikTok. As a senior engineer, I'd like you to walk me through how you'd approach building a system like this. You have about 45 minutes.

**Candidate:** Thank you. I'll follow my standard system design template, starting with requirements, moving through estimates, API, data model, high-level design, deep dives, and finally discussing bottlenecks and trade-offs. I'll make sure to name any common patterns I use.

## 1. Requirements

**Candidate:** First, let's clarify the functional and non-functional requirements. For functional requirements, I assume users should be able to:
1.  Upload short videos, say up to three minutes in length.
2.  View a personalized video feed, typically through an infinite scroll.
3.  Interact with videos by liking, commenting, and sharing.
4.  Follow and unfollow other users.
5.  Search for videos and users.
6.  Manage their own user profile.
Are there any other core functional requirements I should consider?

**Interviewer:** That covers the main ones. Let's assume those are the primary features.

**Candidate:** Great. Now for non-functional requirements, which are crucial for a system of TikTok's scale.
1.  **Scale:** How many users are we targeting? Billions of users globally? Millions of concurrent users?
2.  **Latency:** What are our targets for video playback, feed loading, and interaction latency?
3.  **Availability:** What level of uptime do we need? Four nines, five nines?
4.  **Consistency:** What are our consistency requirements for different types of data? For instance, user profiles versus like counts.

**Interviewer:** Good questions. Let's aim for:
1.  **Scale:** One billion daily active users, or DAU, with peaks of tens of millions concurrent users.
2.  **Latency:** Video playback should start within 200 milliseconds. Feed loading, under 500 milliseconds. Interactions like liking or commenting, under one second.
3.  **Availability:** We need high availability, targeting four nines, or 99.99%.
4.  **Consistency:** User-critical data, like follower counts, should be strongly consistent. For less critical, high-volume data like total likes on a video, eventual consistency is acceptable.

**Candidate:** Understood. One billion DAU, low latency, high availability, and a mix of consistency models. This will heavily influence our design choices.

## 2. Back-of-the-envelope estimates

**Candidate:** Let's do some quick back-of-the-envelope calculations to understand the scale of data and traffic.
*   **Video Uploads:** If one percent of our one billion DAU upload one video per day, that's 10 million new videos daily.
*   **Video Size:** Let's assume an average video is 30 seconds long, 1080p resolution. With modern compression, this could be around 5 to 10 megabytes. I'll use 10 megabytes for a conservative estimate.
*   **Storage:** 10 million videos per day times 10 megabytes per video equals 100 terabytes of new video data daily. Over five years, that's 100 terabytes per day times 365 days per year times 5 years, which is approximately 182.5 petabytes. This is a massive amount of storage, indicating the need for object storage.
*   **Video Views QPS (Queries Per Second):** If each of our one billion DAU watches, say, 100 videos a day, that's 100 billion video views daily. Dividing by the number of seconds in a day, 86,400, gives us roughly 1.1 million video views per second on average.
*   **Bandwidth:** At 1.1 million QPS, if each video stream consumes 10 megabytes, that's 11 terabytes per second of outbound bandwidth. This figure immediately tells me that a global Content Delivery Network, or CDN, is absolutely essential.
*   **Feed QPS:** If each user refreshes their feed, say, five times a day, that's 5 billion feed loads daily. This translates to about 58,000 QPS for feed generation.

**Interviewer:** Those numbers are indeed large. What's your immediate takeaway from these estimates?

**Candidate:** My immediate takeaways are:
1.  Massive storage for video files, requiring a highly scalable and cost-effective object storage solution.
2.  Extremely high read QPS and bandwidth for video streaming, making a global CDN non-negotiable.
3.  High QPS for feed generation, implying a need for efficient data retrieval and potentially pre-computation or caching.
4.  The system will be read-heavy, but video uploads and social interactions will also generate significant write loads.

## 3. API design

**Candidate:** Next, I'll outline the main API endpoints. I'll describe them in a RESTful style, focusing on clarity.

For **Video Management**:
*   `POST /videos`: To upload a new video. It would take the video file, user ID, description, and perhaps some tags. It returns a video ID.
*   `GET /videos/{id}`: To retrieve metadata for a specific video. Returns video ID, user ID, description, CDN URL for streaming, like count, comment count, and so on.
*   `GET /videos/{id}/stream`: To stream the actual video content. This would likely redirect to a CDN URL.

For **Feed Generation**:
*   `GET /feed`: To retrieve a personalized video feed for the authenticated user. It would take optional parameters like a `pagination_token` for infinite scrolling. It returns a list of video metadata.

For **User Interactions**:
*   `POST /videos/{id}/like`: To like a video. Takes user ID.
*   `POST /videos/{id}/comment`: To add a comment to a video. Takes user ID and comment text.
*   `POST /videos/{id}/share`: To share a video.
*   `POST /users/{id}/follow`: To follow another user. Takes the follower's user ID and the followed user's ID.

For **User Profiles and Search**:
*   `GET /users/{id}/profile`: To get a user's profile information.
*   `GET /search?query={term}&type={video|user}`: To search for videos or users.

**Interviewer:** Why separate `GET /videos/{id}` and `GET /videos/{id}/stream`?

**Candidate:** That's a good question. Separating them allows us to fetch video metadata quickly without incurring the overhead of a full video stream request. The metadata can be served from a fast database or cache, while the actual video stream is handled by the CDN, potentially with different authentication or authorization mechanisms. It also allows the client to decide when to start streaming after seeing the metadata.

## 4. Data model and storage choice

**Candidate:** Now for the data model and storage choices. Given the scale and mixed consistency requirements, I'd propose a combination of database technologies.

1.  **User Data:**
    *   **Schema:** `User` table with `user_id` (primary key), `username`, `email`, `password_hash`, `profile_picture_url`, `follower_count`, `following_count`, `created_at`.
    *   **Storage:** A sharded relational database like PostgreSQL or MySQL. This provides strong consistency for critical user data and supports complex queries for user management. We'd use database sharding to distribute the load as user count grows.

2.  **Video Metadata:**
    *   **Schema:** `Video` table with `video_id` (primary key), `user_id` (foreign key), `title`, `description`, `cdn_url` (for processed video), `thumbnail_url`, `upload_timestamp`, `likes_count`, `comments_count`, `shares_count`, `visibility_status`.
    *   **Storage:** Also a sharded relational database, potentially co-located with user data shards or on separate shards, depending on access patterns. Strong consistency is important for core video information.

3.  **Likes, Comments, Follows (Social Graph Data):**
    *   **Schema:**
        *   `Like` table: `like_id` (PK), `user_id` (FK), `video_id` (FK), `timestamp`.
        *   `Comment` table: `comment_id` (PK), `user_id` (FK), `video_id` (FK), `parent_comment_id` (for replies), `text`, `timestamp`.
        *   `Follow` table: `follow_id` (PK), `follower_user_id` (FK), `followed_user_id` (FK), `timestamp`.
    *   **Storage:** For these high-volume, potentially eventually consistent interactions, we could consider a NoSQL database like Cassandra or DynamoDB for high write throughput and horizontal scalability. However, for strong consistency on follower counts, we might keep `follower_count` and `following_count` in the User table and update them transactionally, or use a separate relational database for precise counts if eventual consistency is not acceptable. Given the requirement for strong consistency on follower counts, I'd lean towards using the sharded relational database for `Follow` and `User` tables, and potentially a NoSQL store for `Like` and `Comment` if the write volume overwhelms the relational database. For now, let's assume sharded relational databases for all metadata.

4.  **Raw Video Files:**
    *   **Storage:** Object Storage, like Amazon S3 or Google Cloud Storage. This offers extreme durability, scalability, and cost-effectiveness for binary large objects.

5.  **Caches:**
    *   **Storage:** Redis or Memcached for frequently accessed data like user profiles, hot video metadata, and segments of personalized feeds. This is a common pattern known as the **cache-aside pattern**, where the application checks the cache first, and if there's a miss, it fetches data from the database and populates the cache.

**Interviewer:** You mentioned sharding for relational databases. How would you shard the User and Video tables?

**Candidate:** For the `User` table, I'd typically shard by `user_id` using a consistent hashing scheme. This distributes users evenly across shards. For the `Video` table, sharding by `video_id` is an option, but sharding by `user_id` (the uploader) could also be beneficial for queries that fetch all videos by a specific user. However, if we shard by `user_id` for videos, a single popular user could create a hot shard. So, sharding by `video_id` (which is globally unique and sequential or random) is generally safer to ensure even distribution. We'd need a distributed ID generation service for `video_id`s, perhaps using a combination of timestamp and worker ID to ensure uniqueness and sortability.

## 5. High-level design

**Candidate:** Let's put these components together. I envision a microservices architecture, which allows for independent scaling and development of different functionalities.

1.  **Clients:** Mobile apps (iOS, Android) and web browsers.
2.  **Load Balancers:** Distribute incoming requests across our API Gateway instances.
3.  **API Gateway:** This acts as the entry point, handling authentication, rate limiting, SSL termination, and routing requests to the appropriate microservice.
4.  **Core Microservices:**
    *   **User Service:** Manages user profiles, authentication, and authorization. Interacts with the User DB.
    *   **Video Upload Service:** Handles initial video uploads and metadata storage.
    *   **Video Processing Service:** Asynchronously transcodes videos, generates thumbnails, and prepares them for streaming. This would be a set of worker nodes.
    *   **Video Service:** Manages video metadata, retrieves video details, and provides CDN URLs. Interacts with the Video DB.
    *   **Social Graph Service:** Manages follows, likes, and comments. Interacts with the respective tables in the DB.
    *   **Feed Service:** Generates personalized video feeds. This is a complex service that would interact with the Social Graph Service, Video Service, and a Recommendation Engine.
    *   **Recommendation Engine:** A separate service, likely machine learning-based, that suggests videos to users.
    *   **Search Service:** Indexes video and user data, provides search functionality.
    *   **Notification Service:** Handles push notifications for new followers, comments, etc.
5.  **Databases:** As discussed: Sharded Relational Databases (PostgreSQL/MySQL) for core metadata, Object Storage (S3) for raw video files.
6.  **Caches:** Distributed cache (Redis) for hot data.
7.  **Message Queues:** Kafka or RabbitMQ for asynchronous communication between services, especially for video processing and feed updates. This decouples services and handles spikes in load.
8.  **Content Delivery Network (CDN):** Crucial for low-latency video streaming globally.

Let's walk through two key flows: video upload and feed retrieval.

**Video Upload Flow:**
1.  The client sends a `POST /videos` request to the API Gateway.
2.  The API Gateway authenticates the user and routes the request to the **Video Upload Service**.
3.  The Video Upload Service generates a unique `video_id` (using a distributed ID generator), stores initial metadata in the Video DB, and then uploads the raw video file directly to Object Storage.
4.  Crucially, it then publishes a message to a Kafka topic, say `video_uploaded_events`, containing the `video_id` and storage location. This is an example of **asynchronous processing**.
5.  The **Video Processing Service** consumes messages from `video_uploaded_events`. It retrieves the raw video from Object Storage.
6.  It transcodes the video into various resolutions and formats (e.g., HLS, DASH) and generates thumbnails. This is resource-intensive and would be distributed across many worker nodes.
7.  The processed videos and thumbnails are then uploaded back to Object Storage, and their CDN URLs are updated in the Video DB via the Video Service.
8.  Finally, the Video Processing Service publishes a `video_processed_events` message to Kafka, which triggers other services like the Search Service for indexing and the Feed Service for potential feed updates.

**Personalized Feed Retrieval Flow:**
1.  The client sends a `GET /feed` request to the API Gateway.
2.  The API Gateway authenticates the user and routes the request to the **Feed Service**.
3.  The Feed Service first checks its cache (Redis) for a pre-generated segment of the user's feed. This is the **cache-aside pattern**. If found, it returns the video metadata immediately, achieving very low latency.
4.  If there's a cache miss, the Feed Service orchestrates the feed generation:
    *   It queries the **Social Graph Service** to get a list of users the current user follows.
    *   It queries the **Recommendation Engine** for personalized video suggestions based on the user's viewing history, likes, and other behavioral data.
    *   It might also query the **Video Service** for trending or popular videos.
5.  The Feed Service then aggregates these results, applies a ranking algorithm, deduplicates, and filters based on business logic.
6.  It stores this newly generated feed segment in the cache for subsequent requests.
7.  Finally, it returns a list of video metadata (IDs, titles, thumbnail URLs) to the client.
8.  The client then uses these video IDs to fetch the actual video streams from the CDN via `GET /videos/{id}/stream`.

**Interviewer:** That's a clear overview. The feed generation sounds complex. Let's deep dive into that.

## 6. Deep dives

**Candidate:** The personalized feed generation is indeed one of the hardest parts of TikTok. It's a blend of social graph, content recommendations, and real-time data.

**Deep Dive 1: Personalized Feed Generation and Ranking**

The Feed Service needs to provide a fresh, relevant, and engaging stream of videos. This is typically achieved through a hybrid approach:

*   **Source Data Aggregation:**
    *   **Followed Users (Social Graph):** Videos from users the current user follows. This is a **fan-out on write (push model)** for highly followed users, where new videos are pushed to followers' feed queues, or **fan-out on read (pull model)** for users with fewer followers, where the feed is generated on demand. TikTok likely uses a mixed approach, with a strong emphasis on pull for its "For You" page.
    *   **Recommendation Engine:** This is the core of TikTok's "For You" page. It uses sophisticated machine learning models. These models would analyze:
        *   **User behavior:** Videos watched, liked, commented on, shared, skipped.
        *   **Video features:** Audio, visual content, tags, description.
        *   **Social connections:** What friends watch.
        *   **Contextual information:** Device type, location, time of day.
        The Recommendation Engine would output a ranked list of video IDs.
    *   **Trending/Popular Videos:** Videos that are currently viral or gaining traction.

*   **Ranking and Filtering:**
    *   Once the Feed Service gathers candidates from these sources, it applies a multi-stage ranking algorithm. This algorithm considers factors like video freshness, user engagement history, predicted watch time, creator popularity, and diversity to ensure a varied feed.
    *   It also applies filters to remove duplicate content, already watched videos, or content violating policies.

*   **Caching and Pre-computation:**
    *   For the "For You" page, segments of the feed are likely pre-computed for users and stored in a fast key-value store like Redis or Cassandra. When a user requests their feed, a pre-computed chunk is served immediately. As the user scrolls, the next chunk is either already cached or generated on the fly. This is a form of **materialized view** for the feed.
    *   The Recommendation Engine itself might pre-compute recommendations periodically, or it might have a real-time component that adjusts recommendations based on immediate user interactions.

**Interviewer:** That's a good breakdown. What about the video streaming itself? How do you ensure low latency and handle global distribution?

**Candidate:**

**Deep Dive 2: Video Streaming and Global CDN**

Given the 1.1 million QPS and 11 terabytes per second bandwidth estimate, efficient video streaming is critical.

*   **Adaptive Bitrate Streaming (ABS):** We'd use protocols like HLS (HTTP Live Streaming) or DASH (Dynamic Adaptive Streaming over HTTP). The Video Processing Service would transcode each video into multiple quality levels (e.g., 360p, 720p, 1080p). The client player then dynamically switches between these quality levels based on the user's network conditions and device capabilities. This ensures a smooth playback experience even with fluctuating bandwidth.

*   **Global Content Delivery Network (CDN):** This is the backbone of video delivery.
    *   When a user requests a video, the client is redirected to the nearest CDN edge server.
    *   If the video is cached at that edge server, it's served directly, providing extremely low latency.
    *   If not, the CDN fetches the video from the origin (our Object Storage), caches it, and then serves it to the user.
    *   CDNs are highly optimized for media delivery, handling geographical distribution, load balancing, and network optimizations. We would likely use multiple CDN providers for redundancy and better global coverage. This is a **multi-CDN strategy**.

*   **Efficient Codecs:** Using modern video codecs like H.264 (AVC) and H.265 (HEVC) helps reduce file sizes without significant loss in quality, further reducing bandwidth requirements and improving streaming performance. The Video Processing Service would handle this.

**Interviewer:** You mentioned database sharding earlier. How would you handle hot shards or data growth for the relational databases?

**Candidate:**

**Deep Dive 3: Scalable Data Storage for Metadata**

For our sharded relational databases (PostgreSQL/MySQL), managing growth and hot spots is crucial.

*   **Sharding Strategy:** As discussed, sharding by `user_id` for user data and `video_id` for video metadata helps distribute data. We'd use a **consistent hashing** algorithm to map IDs to specific shards. This minimizes data movement when adding or removing shards.
*   **Replication:** Each shard would have a master-replica setup. Writes go to the master, and reads can be distributed across multiple replicas. This improves read scalability and provides high availability. If a master fails, a replica can be promoted.
*   **Hot Shard Mitigation:**
    *   If a shard becomes a "hot shard" due to a popular user or many videos mapping to it, we can employ techniques like **re-sharding** (migrating data from the hot shard to new shards) or **vertical partitioning** (splitting tables within a shard).
    *   For specific high-read data, like counts (likes, comments), we might denormalize them or use a separate, highly scalable key-value store (like Redis for counters) and update the relational database eventually. This is a **denormalization pattern** for read optimization.
*   **Connection Pooling:** At the application level, connection pooling ensures efficient reuse of database connections, reducing overhead.
*   **Database Proxies:** A database proxy layer can sit between the application services and the database shards, handling routing queries to the correct shard, connection management, and potentially even read-write splitting.

## 7. Bottlenecks, failure modes and trade-offs

**Candidate:** Let's discuss potential bottlenecks, failure modes, and the trade-offs we've made.

**Bottlenecks:**
1.  **Video Processing:** Transcoding is CPU and I/O intensive. This is mitigated by asynchronous processing with message queues and a large pool of distributed workers.
2.  **Feed Generation:** The Recommendation Engine and the aggregation logic can be computationally heavy. Caching, pre-computation, and incremental updates are key mitigations.
3.  **Database Writes:** High QPS for likes, comments, and follows can overwhelm relational databases. Sharding, eventual consistency for non-critical counts, and potentially offloading to NoSQL stores for raw event logging are solutions.
4.  **Network Bandwidth:** For video streaming, this is largely handled by the CDN, but origin bandwidth to the CDN must also be robust.

**Failure Modes:**
1.  **Single Point of Failure:** Addressed by using redundant components at every layer: multiple load balancers, API Gateway instances, microservice instances, database replicas, and multiple CDN providers.
2.  **Data Loss:** Object storage provides high durability. Regular backups for databases, and geo-replication for critical data, prevent data loss.
3.  **Service Overload/Cascading Failures:** Implementing **circuit breakers** and **retries** in inter-service communication prevents a failing service from taking down others. **Rate limiting** at the API Gateway protects backend services.
4.  **Database Failures:** Master-replica replication allows for failover. Sharding isolates failures to a subset of data.

**Trade-offs:**
1.  **Consistency vs. Availability/Performance:** We chose strong consistency for critical user data (e.g., follower counts) and eventual consistency for high-volume, less critical data (e.g., total video likes, which can be eventually updated from event streams). This balances data integrity with system performance and availability.
2.  **Latency vs. Cost:** Aggressive caching and a global CDN significantly reduce latency but come with higher infrastructure costs. We accept this trade-off for a good user experience.
3.  **Complexity vs. Scalability/Agility:** A microservices architecture is more complex to develop, deploy, and operate than a monolith. However, it provides independent scalability for services, allows for different technology choices per service, and enables faster development cycles for large teams. This is a necessary trade-off for a system of TikTok's scale.
4.  **Fan-out on Write vs. Fan-out on Read:** For feed generation, we discussed a hybrid approach. Fan-out on write (push) provides lower read latency but higher write load and storage. Fan-out on read (pull) saves storage but increases read latency. The optimal balance depends on the specific user interaction patterns and the desired freshness of the feed. TikTok's "For You" page leans heavily on pull-based recommendations.

**What changes at ten times the load?**
At ten times the load, we'd need:
*   Even more aggressive database sharding and potentially more distributed database systems (e.g., a NewSQL database like CockroachDB or TiDB, or a highly scaled NoSQL solution for social graph).
*   More sophisticated, potentially real-time, machine learning models for the Recommendation Engine, requiring larger compute clusters.
*   More CDN capacity and potentially even more distributed edge computing for video processing closer to the upload source.
*   Enhanced monitoring, auto-scaling, and chaos engineering practices to ensure resilience.
*   A deeper investment in infrastructure automation and reliability engineering.

## 8. Wrap-up

**Candidate:** To summarize, designing TikTok for one billion DAU requires a highly distributed, scalable, and resilient system. Our design leverages a microservices architecture, sharded relational databases for core metadata, object storage for video files, and a global CDN for efficient video streaming. Asynchronous processing with message queues handles intensive tasks like video transcoding. The personalized feed is generated by combining social graph data with a powerful recommendation engine, heavily relying on caching for low latency. We've made deliberate trade-offs between consistency, availability, and performance to meet the demanding non-functional requirements.

**Interviewer:** Thank you. That was a comprehensive design.
