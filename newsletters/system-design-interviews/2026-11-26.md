**Interviewer:** Welcome. Today, let's design a Distributed Job Scheduler. Imagine a system that can schedule and execute various types of jobs reliably across a fleet of machines. How would you approach this?

## Requirements

**Candidate:** Alright, a distributed job scheduler. This is a classic problem with many interesting trade-offs. As always, I'd like to start by clarifying the requirements, both functional and non-functional, to scope our discussion.

**Candidate:** Functionally, what kinds of jobs do we need to support? Are we talking about simple one-off tasks, recurring jobs like cron jobs, or more complex workflows? And what about job payloads? Can they be arbitrary data?

**Interviewer:** Good questions. Let's assume we need to support one-off jobs, recurring jobs with cron-like schedules, and potentially jobs that run at a specific future time. Job payloads can be arbitrary, up to a few megabytes, and the system should simply deliver them to a worker for execution. The system also needs to track job status, allow cancellation, and support retries on failure.

**Candidate:** Understood. Now, for the non-functional requirements. What kind of scale are we aiming for? How many jobs per day, and what's the expected concurrency?

**Interviewer:** We're looking at a high-scale system. Let's say ten million jobs per day, with peak concurrency reaching tens of thousands of active jobs at any given moment.

**Candidate:** Ten million jobs per day, tens of thousands concurrent. That's significant. What are the latency expectations? For scheduling a job, and for a job to actually start execution once its scheduled time arrives?

**Interviewer:** Scheduling a job should be fast, ideally within tens of milliseconds. For execution, once a job is due, it should ideally start within a few seconds, let's say five seconds, but the job execution itself can take anywhere from seconds to hours.

**Candidate:** And what about availability and consistency? How critical is it that the system is always up and that job status is immediately consistent across all queries?

**Interviewer:** High availability is crucial for both scheduling and execution. We can tolerate eventual consistency for job status updates, but job definitions and schedules need to be strongly consistent when they are created or modified. We cannot lose job definitions.

**Candidate:** Perfect. So, to summarize: we need to schedule one-off, recurring, and time-based jobs, execute them reliably, track status, support retries, and allow cancellation. We're targeting ten million jobs per day, tens of thousands concurrent, with low latency for scheduling and prompt execution. High availability is key, and we'll aim for strong consistency for job definitions and eventual consistency for status.

## Back-of-the-envelope estimates

**Candidate:** Let's do some quick back-of-the-envelope estimates to understand the scale better. Ten million jobs per day.

**Candidate:** For requests per second, that's ten million divided by eighty-six thousand four hundred seconds in a day, which is roughly one hundred and fifteen requests per second on average. However, traffic usually isn't uniform. If we assume a peak-to-average ratio of ten, we might see around one thousand one hundred and fifty requests per second for scheduling new jobs.

**Candidate:** Now, storage. If each job definition, including its payload and metadata, averages one kilobyte, then ten million jobs per day would require ten gigabytes of storage per day. Over a year, that's about three point six terabytes. This isn't massive for a single database, but it tells us we'll need a robust, scalable storage solution.

**Candidate:** For job status updates, workers will be sending updates frequently. If we have tens of thousands of concurrent jobs, and each updates its status every minute, that's tens of thousands of updates per minute, or hundreds per second. These are typically small writes.

**Candidate:** Bandwidth will primarily be driven by job payloads being sent to workers and status updates. If payloads are up to a few megabytes, we need to consider how they are transferred. For ten thousand concurrent jobs, if each has a one-megabyte payload, that's ten gigabytes of data potentially being transferred to workers at any given moment, though not all at once. This suggests we might want to store larger payloads separately.

## API design

**Candidate:** Based on these requirements, let's sketch out a basic API. We'll need endpoints for managing jobs.

**Candidate:**
*   `POST /jobs`: This would be our primary endpoint for scheduling a new job. It would take a JSON payload including `jobDefinition` (e.g., a command, a script, a URL to call), `schedule` (e.g., `once` with a `runAt` timestamp, or `cron` string), `retries` configuration, and potentially an `idempotencyKey`. It would return a `jobId` and the initial `status`.
*   `GET /jobs/{jobId}`: To retrieve the current status and details of a specific job.
*   `GET /jobs?status=...&type=...`: To list jobs, perhaps with filtering by status, type, or creation time.
*   `PUT /jobs/{jobId}/cancel`: To cancel a job that hasn't started or is in progress.
*   `PUT /jobs/{jobId}/pause`: To pause a recurring job.
*   `PUT /jobs/{jobId}/resume`: To resume a paused recurring job.

**Candidate:** Internally, workers will need APIs to fetch tasks and report status.
*   `POST /worker/register`: For workers to register themselves with the system.
*   `POST /worker/heartbeat`: For workers to periodically report their health.
*   `POST /jobs/{jobId}/status`: For workers to update the status of a job they are executing (e.g., `RUNNING`, `SUCCEEDED`, `FAILED`). This would include execution details and potentially results.

## Data model and storage choice

**Candidate:** For the data model, we'll need to store job definitions, their schedules, current status, execution history, and worker information.

**Candidate:** A `Job` table would contain:
*   `job_id` (Primary Key, UUID)
*   `name` (string)
*   `job_definition` (JSON, containing the actual task payload or a reference to it)
*   `schedule_type` (enum: `ONCE`, `CRON`, `AT`)
*   `schedule_details` (JSON, e.g., `run_at` timestamp, `cron_string`)
*   `status` (enum: `PENDING`, `DISPATCHED`, `RUNNING`, `SUCCEEDED`, `FAILED`, `CANCELLED`)
*   `created_at`, `updated_at` (timestamps)
*   `last_dispatched_at`, `next_dispatch_at` (timestamps, for recurring jobs)
*   `retry_count`, `max_retries` (integers)
*   `worker_id` (foreign key, if assigned to a specific worker)
*   `idempotency_key` (string, for reliable retries)

**Candidate:** An `ExecutionHistory` table could log each attempt:
*   `execution_id` (PK)
*   `job_id` (FK)
*   `worker_id` (FK)
*   `start_time`, `end_time`
*   `status` (`RUNNING`, `SUCCEEDED`, `FAILED`)
*   `error_message`, `output`

**Candidate:** For storage choice, given the need for strong consistency for job definitions, complex queries (like "find all jobs due in the next five minutes"), and transactional updates (e.g., changing job status), a **relational database** like PostgreSQL or MySQL would be an excellent fit. It provides ACID properties, robust indexing, and supports complex joins for analytics or monitoring.

**Candidate:** For the potentially larger job payloads (up to a few megabytes), we could store them directly in a JSONB column in PostgreSQL if they are within reasonable limits, or for very large payloads, store them in an object storage service like Amazon S3 and store only the reference (URL) in the database. This is a common **blob storage pattern**.

**Interviewer:** Why not a NoSQL database, like Cassandra or MongoDB, which might scale better for ten million jobs a day?

**Candidate:** That's a great question. While NoSQL databases offer horizontal scalability, they often come with trade-offs in consistency and query flexibility. For our core job metadata, we need to efficiently query by time, status, and potentially other attributes to find jobs due for dispatch. Relational databases excel here with secondary indexes and transactional integrity. For example, finding `jobs WHERE status = 'PENDING' AND next_dispatch_at <= NOW()` is a very efficient query in a well-indexed relational database. If we foresee the need to scale beyond what a sharded relational database can handle, we could consider a hybrid approach or a NoSQL database with strong secondary index capabilities, but PostgreSQL is a solid starting point that simplifies many consistency challenges.

## High-level design

**Candidate:** Let's outline the main components and how they interact.

**Candidate:**
1.  **API Gateway/Load Balancer**: This is the entry point for all client requests, distributing traffic to our services.
2.  **Scheduler Service**: This service handles the `POST /jobs` and `PUT /jobs` requests. It validates job definitions, persists them to the Job Store, and then publishes a "Job Created" event to a Message Queue. This service is stateless and can be scaled horizontally.
3.  **Job Store**: Our PostgreSQL database, storing all job definitions, schedules, and status.
4.  **Dispatcher Service**: This is the "brain" of our scheduler. It continuously queries the Job Store for jobs that are `PENDING` and whose `next_dispatch_at` is in the past or very near future. Once it identifies a job to run, it marks the job as `DISPATCHED` in the Job Store and publishes the job payload to a Message Queue for workers to pick up. This service needs to be highly available and resilient. We'll likely use a **leader election pattern** here, like ZooKeeper or etcd, to ensure only one dispatcher is actively publishing jobs to avoid duplicates, or we'll shard the job data and have multiple dispatchers, each responsible for a subset.
5.  **Message Queue**: A robust, distributed message queue like Apache Kafka or RabbitMQ. It acts as a buffer between the Dispatcher and the Workers, ensuring reliable, at-least-once delivery of job tasks. It also decouples the services, allowing them to scale independently.
6.  **Worker Service**: A fleet of worker instances that poll the Message Queue for new job tasks. When a worker receives a task, it executes the job (e.g., runs a script, makes an HTTP call). During and after execution, it updates the job's status in the Job Store via the Status Update Service. Workers are stateless and can be scaled out based on load.
7.  **Status Update Service**: A lightweight service that receives status updates from workers and persists them to the Job Store. This can also handle retries based on the job's configuration.
8.  **Monitoring and Alerting**: Essential for any distributed system, tracking job failures, system health, and performance metrics.

**Candidate:** Let's trace a job's lifecycle:
1.  A client calls `POST /jobs` on the API Gateway.
2.  The request goes to the Scheduler Service.
3.  The Scheduler Service validates the input, stores the job definition in the Job Store (PostgreSQL), and returns `jobId` to the client. The job's status is `PENDING`.
4.  The Dispatcher Service, continuously polling the Job Store, finds this job because its `next_dispatch_at` is now (for one-off jobs) or matches its cron schedule.
5.  The Dispatcher atomically updates the job's status to `DISPATCHED` in the Job Store and publishes the job payload to the Message Queue. This atomic update is crucial to prevent multiple dispatchers from picking up the same job, a form of **distributed lock**.
6.  A Worker Service instance consumes the job task from the Message Queue.
7.  The Worker executes the job. It periodically sends status updates (e.g., `RUNNING`) to the Status Update Service, which updates the Job Store.
8.  Upon completion, the Worker sends a final status (`SUCCEEDED` or `FAILED`) to the Status Update Service. If failed, the Status Update Service might trigger a retry by updating `next_dispatch_at` and `retry_count` in the Job Store, making it visible to the Dispatcher again.

## Deep dives

**Interviewer:** That's a solid high-level overview. Let's deep dive into a couple of critical areas. First, how do you ensure reliable job execution, specifically handling worker failures or message queue issues, to achieve at-least-once delivery?

**Candidate:** Ensuring reliable, at-least-once job execution is paramount.
1.  **Message Queue Guarantees**: Our Message Queue (Kafka or RabbitMQ) is configured for **at-least-once delivery**. This means a message is guaranteed to be delivered to a consumer at least once, even if the consumer or broker crashes. The worker must explicitly acknowledge receipt and successful processing of a message. If a worker crashes before acknowledging, the message will be redelivered to another worker.
2.  **Idempotency**: Since messages can be delivered multiple times, jobs must be **idempotent**. This means executing the same job multiple times should have the same effect as executing it once. For example, if a job is to increment a counter, it should use a transaction or a conditional update to ensure it increments only once per unique job execution ID. We can pass an `idempotencyKey` with the job payload to help workers achieve this.
3.  **Job Status and Timeouts**: When a worker picks up a job, it should immediately update the job's status in the Job Store to `RUNNING` and record the `worker_id`. It can also set an expected `completion_timeout`.
4.  **Reaper/Monitor Service**: We'd have a separate "Reaper" or "Monitor" component, possibly integrated into the Dispatcher or as a standalone service. This service periodically scans the Job Store for jobs that are in `RUNNING` status but have exceeded their `completion_timeout` or whose assigned `worker_id` is no longer active (based on worker heartbeats). If such a job is found, the Reaper marks it as `FAILED` and potentially triggers a retry by updating its `next_dispatch_at` and incrementing `retry_count`, making it available for the Dispatcher to re-queue. This handles scenarios where a worker crashes mid-execution.

**Interviewer:** Okay, that covers worker failures. What about the Dispatcher? It's critical for finding and pushing jobs. How do you make that highly available and prevent duplicate dispatches if multiple Dispatchers are running?

**Candidate:** The Dispatcher is indeed a critical component. For high availability and to prevent duplicate dispatches, we employ a **leader election pattern**.
1.  **Leader Election**: We'd use a distributed coordination service like Apache ZooKeeper or etcd. Multiple Dispatcher instances would compete to become the leader. Only the elected leader is responsible for querying the database and dispatching jobs. If the leader fails, ZooKeeper/etcd detects this, and a new leader is elected from the remaining instances. This ensures a single, active dispatcher at any given time.
2.  **Atomic Dispatch**: When the leader Dispatcher finds a job to dispatch, it performs an atomic update in the Job Store: `UPDATE jobs SET status = 'DISPATCHED', last_dispatched_at = NOW() WHERE job_id = ? AND status = 'PENDING'`. The `WHERE status = 'PENDING'` clause is crucial. If another process (e.g., a rogue dispatcher or a race condition) tried to dispatch the same job, this update would fail because the status wouldn't be `PENDING` anymore. This is a form of **optimistic locking** or a **compare-and-swap** operation at the database level.
3.  **Sharding for Scale**: For ten million jobs a day, even a single leader Dispatcher might struggle to query the entire `Job` table efficiently. We can shard the `Job` table, perhaps by `job_id` hash or `tenant_id`. Then, we can have multiple Dispatcher groups, with each group responsible for a specific shard, using leader election within each group. This allows parallel dispatching across shards, significantly increasing throughput.

## Bottlenecks, failure modes and trade-offs

**Candidate:** Let's discuss potential bottlenecks, failure modes, and the trade-offs we've made.

**Candidate:**
*   **Bottlenecks**:
    *   **Job Store (Database)**: The database could become a bottleneck, especially for the Dispatcher's continuous polling queries and the high volume of status updates. We've mitigated this with efficient indexing, atomic updates, and considering sharding. Read replicas can offload `GET` requests, but writes remain a challenge.
    *   **Message Queue**: Throughput limits of the message queue could be a bottleneck if job dispatch rates or worker consumption rates are imbalanced. Scaling Kafka partitions or RabbitMQ queues would address this.
    *   **Worker Capacity**: If job execution is slow or resource-intensive, workers could become a bottleneck. Auto-scaling worker groups based on queue depth or CPU utilization is key.
*   **Failure Modes**:
    *   **Dispatcher Failure**: Handled by leader election. A new leader takes over, ensuring dispatching resumes.
    *   **Worker Failure**: Handled by message queue retries, job idempotency, and the Reaper service.
    *   **Message Queue Failure**: If the message queue goes down, new jobs cannot be dispatched, and workers cannot receive tasks. A highly available message queue setup (e.g., Kafka clusters, RabbitMQ clusters) is essential. Jobs already in the database are safe.
    *   **Database Failure**: This is the most critical. If the Job Store fails, the entire system is severely impacted. We need robust database high availability solutions, like primary-replica setups with automatic failover, and regular backups.
    *   **Network Partitions**: Services might lose connectivity. Our at-least-once delivery and idempotency help here, as messages might be retried once connectivity is restored.

**Candidate:** **Trade-offs**:
*   **Consistency vs. Availability**: We chose strong consistency for job definitions and schedules (using a relational DB and atomic updates) and eventual consistency for job status. This is a practical trade-off, as immediate consistency for status isn't always required and allows for higher throughput.
*   **Complexity vs. Reliability**: Introducing components like a message queue, leader election, and a reaper service adds complexity to the system. However, this complexity is necessary to achieve the high reliability and availability requirements of a distributed job scheduler.
*   **Latency vs. Throughput**: The Dispatcher's polling interval balances how quickly jobs are picked up against the load on the database. A shorter interval means lower dispatch latency but higher database load.

**Candidate:** **What changes at ten times the scale?**
*   **Database Sharding**: This becomes critical. We'd likely shard the `Job` table by `job_id` (using a hash) or `tenant_id` if jobs are tied to specific tenants. Each shard would have its own Dispatcher group.
*   **Message Queue Scaling**: More partitions in Kafka, more brokers, or more RabbitMQ queues and exchanges.
*   **Dedicated Services**: The Status Update Service might need to be a larger, more dedicated service to handle the increased write volume.
*   **Advanced Dispatching**: Instead of simple polling, the Dispatcher might use database change data capture (CDC) or a more sophisticated event-driven approach to detect new or due jobs, reducing direct database load.
*   **More Workers**: Auto-scaling worker groups would need to be very responsive to handle spikes in job volume.

## Wrap-up

**Candidate:** To summarize, we've designed a distributed job scheduler capable of handling ten million jobs daily with high availability. The core components include a Scheduler Service for job submission, a PostgreSQL Job Store for strong consistency of job definitions, a highly available Dispatcher Service using leader election to find and queue jobs, a Message Queue for reliable task delivery, and a fleet of idempotent Worker Services for execution. We've addressed reliability through at-least-once delivery, idempotency, and a Reaper service for worker failures, and scalability through sharding and horizontal scaling of stateless components.

**Interviewer:** Excellent. Thank you.

Three to five key terms and patterns to remember from this problem:
1.  **Idempotency**: Essential for reliable job execution, especially with at-least-once delivery guarantees.
2.  **Leader Election**: A common pattern for ensuring a single active coordinator in a distributed system, crucial for the Dispatcher here.
3.  **At-Least-Once Delivery**: A message queue guarantee that requires careful handling with idempotency on the consumer side.
4.  **Distributed Locks / Atomic Updates**: Using the database or a coordination service to ensure only one process acts on a resource at a time.
5.  **Sharding**: A fundamental technique for scaling databases and services horizontally.

One common mistake candidates make when designing a job scheduler is not adequately addressing the "due job discovery" problem at scale. Simply querying `WHERE scheduled_time <= NOW()` on a massive table can become a severe bottleneck. The candidate here touched upon this by suggesting sharding and efficient indexing, which is crucial. Another common mistake is overlooking the need for idempotency, which is critical when dealing with retries and at-least-once delivery guarantees.
