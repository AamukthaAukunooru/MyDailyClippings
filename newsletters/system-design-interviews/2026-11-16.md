**Interviewer:** Good morning. Today, let's design Instagram. Imagine you're building the core services for a new photo and video sharing platform. How would you approach this?

**Candidate:** Good morning. That's a classic problem, and I'm happy to tackle it. I'll start by clarifying the **1. Requirements**, then move to **2. Back-of-the-envelope estimates**, **3. API design**, **4. Data model and storage choice**, **5. High-level design**, **6. Deep dives** into the hardest parts, discuss **7. Bottlenecks, failure modes and trade-offs**, and finally, **8. Wrap-up** with a summary.

## Requirements

**Candidate:** To begin, I need to understand the core functional and non-functional requirements. Functionally, I assume users should be able to:
*   Upload photos and short videos.
*   View their personalized feed of posts from people they follow.
*   Follow and unfollow other users.
*   Like and comment on posts.
*   View user profiles and their posts.
*   Search for users and hashtags.

**Interviewer:** Those are good assumptions. Let's focus on photos for now, and we can discuss videos as an extension. For non-functional requirements, let's aim for:
*   **Scale:** We expect 100 million daily active users, or DAU.
*   **Writes:** About 10 million new posts, including photos and short videos, per day.
*   **Reads:** On average, a user views 200 feed items per day.
*   **Media size:** Average photo size is 500 kilobytes.
*   **Latency:** Feed generation should be under 200 milliseconds. Photo upload should be under 500 milliseconds.
*   **Availability:** We need high availability, say 99.99 percent.
*   **Consistency:** Eventual consistency is acceptable for the feed, but user profiles and post metadata should be strongly consistent.

**Candidate:** Excellent, those numbers give us a solid foundation.

## Back-of-the-envelope estimates

**Candidate:** Now, let's do some back-of-the-envelope estimates to understand the scale of traffic and storage.

First, **requests per second**.
*   **Photo uploads:** 10 million posts per day divided by 86,400 seconds in a day gives us approximately 115 writes per second on average. We'd likely see peaks 2 to 5 times higher, so let's plan for around 500 writes per second for uploads.
*   **Feed reads:** This will be much higher. 100 million DAU, each viewing 200 feed items per day. That's 20 billion feed item views per day. Dividing by 86,400 seconds, we get roughly 230,000 feed item views per second. This is a critical number for our read path.
*   **Other actions (likes, comments, follows):** Let's assume on average each DAU performs 5 such actions per day. That's 500 million actions per day, which is about 5,800 requests per second.
So, we're looking at peak read traffic in the hundreds of thousands of requests per second, and write traffic in the low thousands.

Next, **storage**.
*   **Media storage:** 10 million photos per day, each 500 kilobytes. That's 5 terabytes of new data per day. Over a year, that's 5 terabytes times 365 days, which is about 1.8 petabytes per year. This will quickly grow into many petabytes.
*   **Metadata storage:** For each post, we might store a few kilobytes of metadata (user ID, caption, timestamp, location, etc.). 10 million posts per day times 1 kilobyte per post is about 10 gigabytes of metadata per day. Over a year, that's 3.6 terabytes. This is much smaller than media storage.
So, we need a solution for petabyte-scale object storage and terabyte-scale structured data storage.

Finally, **bandwidth**.
*   **Upload bandwidth:** 5 terabytes per day is about 58 megabytes per second average upload speed.
*   **Download bandwidth:** This is the big one. 20 billion feed item views per day, each 500 kilobytes. That's 10 petabytes of data downloaded per day. Divided by 86,400 seconds, that's about 115 gigabytes per second. This is a massive amount of data, which immediately tells me we'll need a robust Content Delivery Network, or CDN, to offload traffic from our origin servers.

**Interviewer:** Good numbers. The CDN will definitely be crucial.

## API design

**Candidate:** For the **3. API design**, I'd define a RESTful API. Here are some core endpoints:

*   `POST /api/v1/posts`: To upload a new photo or video. It would take parameters like `user_id`, `caption`, and the media file itself, or perhaps a URL to the uploaded media. It would return a `post_id` and confirmation.
*   `GET /api/v1/feed`: To retrieve a user's personalized feed. It would take `user_id` (implicitly from authentication token) and optional parameters like `last_post_id` for pagination. It would return a list of post objects, each containing `post_id`, `user_id`, `media_url`, `caption`, `likes_count`, `comments_count`, etc.
*   `GET /api/v1/users/{user_id}/posts`: To view a specific user's posts.
*   `POST /api/v1/posts/{post_id}/like`: To like a post.
*   `DELETE /api/v1/posts/{post_id}/like`: To unlike a post.
*   `POST /api/v1/posts/{post_id}/comment`: To add a comment. It would take `user_id`, `text`.
*   `POST /api/v1/users/{user_id}/follow`: To follow another user.
*   `DELETE /api/v1/users/{user_id}/follow`: To unfollow a user.

I'd ensure all requests are authenticated, likely using OAuth tokens, and include versioning in the API path.

## Data model and storage choice

**Candidate:** Moving on to **4. Data model and storage choice**. We have two main types of data: media files and structured metadata.

For **media files**, given the petabyte scale and the need for high availability and durability, I'd choose an **Object Storage Service**, like AWS S3 or Google Cloud Storage. These services are highly scalable, durable, and cost-effective for large binary blobs. They also integrate well with CDNs.

For **structured metadata**, we need to store users, posts, follows, likes, and comments.
*   **Users table:** `user_id` (primary key), `username`, `email`, `password_hash`, `profile_picture_url`, `bio`, `created_at`.
*   **Posts table:** `post_id` (primary key), `user_id` (foreign key to Users), `media_url`, `caption`, `location`, `created_at`, `likes_count`, `comments_count`.
*   **Follows table:** `follower_id` (foreign key to Users), `followee_id` (foreign key to Users), `created_at`. This would be a composite primary key of `(follower_id, followee_id)`.
*   **Likes table:** `post_id` (foreign key to Posts), `user_id` (foreign key to Users), `created_at`. Composite primary key `(post_id, user_id)`.
*   **Comments table:** `comment_id` (primary key), `post_id` (foreign key to Posts), `user_id` (foreign key to Users), `text`, `created_at`.

For these relational-like structures, a **sharded Relational Database Management System (RDBMS)** like PostgreSQL or MySQL would be a good choice. They offer strong consistency, transactional support, and mature ecosystems. We'd shard these tables, likely by `user_id` for user-centric data, to distribute the load.

For the **user feed**, which is highly read-intensive and can tolerate eventual consistency, I'd consider a **NoSQL key-value store** or a wide-column store. A good option here would be Apache Cassandra or AWS DynamoDB. Each user would have a "feed inbox" which is essentially a list of `post_id`s, sorted by time. This allows for very fast retrieval of a user's feed.

We'll also need a **caching layer** like Redis for frequently accessed data, such as user profiles, popular posts, and parts of the user feed to meet the 200-millisecond latency requirement. This is the **cache-aside pattern**, where the application checks the cache first, and if data isn't found, it fetches from the database and populates the cache.

**Interviewer:** You mentioned sharding. How would you shard the main relational database?

**Candidate:** For sharding, I'd primarily shard by `user_id`. This means all data related to a specific user, like their posts, likes they've made, and comments they've written, would ideally reside on the same shard. This improves locality for user-specific queries. The `users` table would be sharded by `user_id`. The `posts` table could also be sharded by the `user_id` of the post creator. For tables like `likes` and `comments`, we could also shard by the `user_id` of the liker/commenter, or use a distributed transaction coordinator if cross-shard joins are strictly necessary, though we'd try to avoid them. For the feed store, it's naturally sharded by `user_id` as each user has their own feed.

## High-level design

**Candidate:** Let's sketch out the **5. High-level design**.

At the front, we'd have **Clients** (mobile apps, web browsers) communicating with our backend.
Requests would first hit a **Load Balancer** to distribute traffic, followed by an **API Gateway**. The API Gateway handles authentication, rate limiting, and routing requests to the appropriate microservices.

Our core services would be:
1.  **User Service:** Manages user profiles, authentication, and follow relationships.
2.  **Post Service:** Handles post metadata (captions, counts, etc.).
3.  **Media Service:** Manages media uploads and processing.
4.  **Feed Service:** Generates and serves personalized user feeds.
5.  **Interaction Service:** Handles likes and comments.

These services would communicate with each other, primarily through internal APIs, and asynchronously via **Message Queues** like Kafka or RabbitMQ for events that don't require immediate synchronous processing.

The **data layer** would consist of:
*   **Sharded RDBMS (PostgreSQL):** For User, Post, Follow, Like, Comment metadata.
*   **Object Storage (S3):** For storing raw media files.
*   **Distributed Cache (Redis):** For hot data and frequently accessed feed items.
*   **NoSQL Feed Store (Cassandra/DynamoDB):** For storing personalized user feeds.

Finally, a **Content Delivery Network (CDN)** would sit in front of the Object Storage to serve media files globally, significantly reducing latency and bandwidth load on our origin servers.

Let's trace two critical flows:
1.  **Photo Upload:**
    *   The client sends a `POST /api/v1/posts` request to the API Gateway.
    *   The API Gateway authenticates and routes it to the **Post Service**.
    *   The Post Service generates a `post_id` and potentially requests a **presigned URL** from the **Media Service**. This is an **idempotency key** pattern, where the client gets a unique URL to upload the file directly to object storage, preventing duplicate uploads if the client retries.
    *   The client then uploads the actual photo file directly to the Object Storage using the presigned URL.
    *   Once the upload is complete, the client notifies the Post Service, which then updates the Post metadata in the RDBMS.
    *   The Post Service then publishes a "Post Created" event to the Message Queue.
    *   A **Fan-out Service** (listening to the Message Queue) picks up this event and updates the feeds of all the followers.

2.  **Feed Retrieval:**
    *   The client sends a `GET /api/v1/feed` request.
    *   The API Gateway routes it to the **Feed Service**.
    *   The Feed Service first checks the **Distributed Cache (Redis)** for the user's feed.
    *   If not found or stale, it queries the **NoSQL Feed Store (Cassandra)** for the `post_id`s in the user's feed.
    *   It then fetches the metadata for these `post_id`s from the Post Service (which might hit its own cache or the RDBMS).
    *   Finally, it returns the list of enriched post objects to the client. The media URLs returned would point to the CDN.

**Interviewer:** That's a clear overview. The feed generation is often the trickiest part. Let's do a deep dive there.

## Deep dives

**Candidate:** For **6. Deep dives**, let's focus on **Feed Generation** and **Media Handling**.

**Deep Dive 1: Feed Generation Strategy**
The core challenge is how to efficiently populate and retrieve a user's feed, especially with 100 million DAU and 230,000 feed item views per second. There are two main patterns:
*   **Fan-out on Write (Push Model):** When a user posts, the new post is immediately pushed to the feed inboxes of all their followers. This makes feed retrieval very fast, as the feed is pre-computed. This is great for active users and high read throughput.
*   **Fan-out on Read (Pull Model):** When a user requests their feed, the system fetches posts from all the users they follow, sorts them by time, and returns them. This is simpler to implement initially and more efficient for users who follow many inactive users, as no work is done until the feed is requested. However, it can be slow for users following many people or if the feed needs complex ranking.

Given our latency requirement of 200 milliseconds for feed generation and high read volume, I'd lean towards a **Hybrid Approach**.
*   For most users, we'd use **Fan-out on Write**. When a post is created, the Post Service publishes an event to a Message Queue. A **Fan-out Service** consumes this event and writes the `post_id` to the feed inboxes of all followers in our NoSQL Feed Store (Cassandra). This ensures fast feed retrieval.
*   **Super-followers:** What happens if a user has millions of followers, like a celebrity? Pushing to millions of inboxes for every post becomes a write bottleneck. For these "super-followers," we'd use **Fan-out on Read**. When a follower of a super-follower requests their feed, the Feed Service would explicitly fetch recent posts from the super-follower and merge them with the pre-computed feed from other followed users. This is a common optimization to manage the write load for highly followed accounts.

The Feed Store (Cassandra) would be sharded by `user_id`, with each user's feed stored as a list of `post_id`s. We'd also use Redis as a cache in front of Cassandra for the most recently accessed feeds.

**Interviewer:** Good point on super-followers. How about media handling, especially with videos in mind?

**Candidate:** **Deep Dive 2: Media Handling and Processing**
Media files are large and require specific handling.
*   **Upload:** As discussed, clients would ideally upload directly to Object Storage using presigned URLs. This offloads our backend services from handling large binary streams.
*   **Asynchronous Processing:** Once a media file is uploaded, an event (e.g., S3 event notification) is triggered and sent to a Message Queue. Worker services consume these messages.
    *   **Image/Video Processing Workers:** These workers would perform tasks like:
        *   **Transcoding:** For videos, converting them into various formats and resolutions (e.g., 360p, 720p, 1080p) suitable for different devices and network conditions. For photos, generating thumbnails and different sizes. This is a **multi-resolution pattern**.
        *   **Watermarking/Metadata Extraction:** Adding a watermark or extracting EXIF data.
        *   **Content Moderation:** Running machine learning models to detect inappropriate content.
    *   The processed versions would also be stored in Object Storage, with their URLs updated in the Post metadata.
*   **Content Delivery Network (CDN):** All media URLs served to clients would point to the CDN. The CDN caches content geographically closer to users, reducing latency and our origin bandwidth. We'd configure appropriate caching headers (e.g., `Cache-Control: max-age`) to maximize CDN hit rates.

**Interviewer:** What happens if a Cassandra node storing a user's feed dies?

**Candidate:** That's a critical failure mode. With Cassandra, we'd configure a high **replication factor**, typically 3 or more, meaning each piece of data is stored on multiple nodes. If one node dies, the data is still available on its replicas. Cassandra also uses **eventual consistency** and **gossip protocol** for node discovery and state synchronization. When a node comes back online, it will catch up on any missed writes from its replicas. For reads, we'd configure a consistency level like `QUORUM`, ensuring that a majority of replicas respond before a read is considered successful, further mitigating data loss or unavailability during node failures.

## Bottlenecks, failure modes and trade-offs

**Candidate:** Let's discuss **7. Bottlenecks, failure modes and trade-offs**.

**Bottlenecks:**
*   **Feed Fan-out for Super-followers:** As discussed, this is a major write bottleneck for the Fan-out Service and Feed Store. Our hybrid approach mitigates this.
*   **Database Hotspots:** If a particular shard or database instance receives disproportionately more traffic, it can become a bottleneck. Sharding by `user_id` helps, but highly active users can still create hotspots. We might need to further split shards or use specialized caching for such users.
*   **Network Bandwidth:** While the CDN handles most media delivery, our origin servers still need to serve metadata and handle uploads. Proper network provisioning and traffic shaping are essential.

**Failure Modes:**
*   **Service Failures:** Any microservice can fail. We'd use **load balancing** with health checks, **auto-scaling groups** to replace unhealthy instances, and **circuit breakers** to prevent cascading failures between services.
*   **Database Failures:**
    *   **RDBMS:** Use **leader-follower replication**. If the leader fails, a follower can be promoted. For writes, we'd use synchronous replication for strong consistency or asynchronous for higher write throughput with slight consistency lag.
    *   **NoSQL (Cassandra):** As mentioned, high replication factor and `QUORUM` consistency for reads/writes ensure availability.
*   **Message Queue Failure:** If Kafka or RabbitMQ goes down, events might be lost or delayed. We'd use a clustered setup for high availability and ensure consumers are **idempotent** so reprocessing messages doesn't cause issues.
*   **CDN Outages:** While rare, a CDN outage would severely impact media delivery. We could have a fallback to direct object storage access, though with higher latency.

**Trade-offs:**
*   **Consistency vs. Availability:** We chose strong consistency for metadata (user profiles, posts) and eventual consistency for feeds. This is a classic **CAP theorem** trade-off, prioritizing availability and performance for the feed.
*   **Push vs. Pull for Feed:** Our hybrid approach balances the high read performance of push with the resource efficiency of pull for super-followers.
*   **Storage Cost vs. Latency/Processing:** Storing multiple media resolutions increases storage cost but reduces real-time processing and improves user experience by delivering optimized content.

**What changes at ten times the scale?**
At ten times the scale (1 billion DAU, 100 million posts/day):
*   **More Aggressive Sharding:** We'd need many more shards for all our databases.
*   **Advanced Caching:** More layers of caching, potentially using multiple Redis clusters, and more sophisticated cache invalidation strategies.
*   **Feed Ranking:** Simple chronological feeds won't suffice. We'd need to integrate machine learning models into the Feed Service to rank posts based on user engagement, relevance, and other factors. This would add complexity to feed generation.
*   **Global Distribution:** We'd likely need to operate in multiple geographical regions, requiring solutions for cross-region data replication and traffic routing.
*   **More Specialized Databases:** We might consider dedicated search engines (Elasticsearch) for hashtag and user search, and potentially a Graph Database for more complex social graph queries beyond simple follows.
*   **Observability:** Robust monitoring, logging, and tracing become even more critical to identify and resolve issues quickly in such a distributed system.

## Wrap-up

**Candidate:** To summarize, our Instagram design focuses on scalability, low latency, and high availability. We've proposed a microservices architecture with a clear separation of concerns. Key components include a CDN for media delivery, object storage for petabyte-scale media, sharded relational databases for metadata, and a NoSQL store with a hybrid fan-out model for efficient feed generation. Asynchronous processing via message queues handles media transcoding and feed updates. We've considered trade-offs between consistency and availability, and discussed how to handle failures and scale to ten times the current load, particularly for super-followers and feed ranking.

**Interviewer:** Thank you, that was a comprehensive design.
