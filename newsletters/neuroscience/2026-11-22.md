Happy Sunday, and welcome to My Daily Clippings. Today, we're looking at some intriguing developments in how we understand both artificial and biological intelligence, alongside a concerning incident affecting a member of the neuroscience community. Researchers are challenging the notion that AI models truly mimic the human brain, even those designed to predict brain activity. We also have news of a Princeton neuroscientist's detention, sparking concern across the scientific world. Finally, a new study highlights a surprisingly simple habit that may significantly reduce the risk of Alzheimer's disease.

## AI's Divergent Path from the Human Brain

A recent discovery is prompting scientists to re-evaluate how closely artificial intelligence models actually resemble the human brain. For years, a common assumption has been that as AI becomes more sophisticated, especially in areas like predicting brain activity, it might be operating on principles similar to our own neural networks. However, new research suggests this may not be the case. Scientists found that even AI models that are highly effective at predicting brain responses do not necessarily achieve this through mechanisms analogous to biological cognition. This finding challenges a fundamental premise in AI research and neuroscience, suggesting that while AI can replicate outcomes, its underlying "thought" processes might be fundamentally different. The study, published yesterday, emphasizes the need for a deeper understanding of AI's internal workings rather than just its external performance, particularly when drawing parallels to human intelligence. This work could reshape how we design future AI systems and how we interpret their capabilities in relation to biological brains.

## Princeton Neuroscientist Detained by ICE

The neuroscience community is expressing "shock and pain" following the detention of Eva Yi Xie, a third-year graduate student at Princeton University, by U.S. Immigration and Customs Enforcement, or ICE. Xie, who is part of Carlos Brody's lab at Princeton, was detained yesterday at an airport in Washington, D.C., while returning from the annual Society for Neuroscience meeting. The incident has raised significant concerns about the treatment of international scientists and the potential chilling effect on academic exchange and research collaboration. Carlos Brody's lab at Princeton focuses on the neural circuits underlying decision-making and working memory, areas where international collaboration and diverse perspectives are crucial. The detention of a researcher traveling from a major scientific conference underscores the vulnerabilities faced by non-citizen scientists and has prompted calls for greater support and protection for the international academic community.

## Reading for a Healthier Brain

Good news for bibliophiles: a simple habit, reading for pleasure, has been linked to a significantly lower risk of Alzheimer's disease. New research highlights that engaging in regular reading may reduce the risk of developing Alzheimer's by as much as thirty-three percent. This finding adds to a growing body of evidence suggesting that cognitive engagement plays a vital role in brain health and resilience against neurodegenerative conditions. Researchers emphasize that reading for pleasure is not just a leisure activity, but a powerful form of mental exercise that can contribute to better memory, reduced stress, and improved overall mental health. While the study did not specify the exact mechanisms, it is believed that the cognitive stimulation involved in reading helps build **cognitive reserve**, making the brain more resilient to the pathological changes associated with Alzheimer's. This finding, reported yesterday, reinforces the importance of lifelong learning and mentally stimulating activities in maintaining brain health as we age.
