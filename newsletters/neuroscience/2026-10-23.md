Here are today's clippings, focusing on some intriguing new findings in brain science, from surprising avian intelligence to a closer look at popular supplements and evolving research tools. We'll delve into why some brains are faster, why others stay smooth, and how our understanding of conditions like autism is becoming more refined.

## Avian Brains in the Fast Lane and Primate Brain Development

New research highlights the remarkable efficiency of avian brains, specifically in **crows**. A study published yesterday reveals that these birds can accurately complete complex decision-making tasks twice as fast as primates, despite significant differences in their brain structures. This finding challenges some long-held assumptions about the neural architecture required for advanced cognitive functions. While the specific researchers and institutions were not detailed in the initial report, this work underscores the diverse evolutionary paths to intelligence.

Meanwhile, another study sheds light on the development of brain **wrinkles**, or gyri and sulci, by examining the common marmoset. Scientists have discovered a surprising secret behind why common marmosets have unusually small, smooth brains, despite their early brain development mirroring that of other primates. The key lies in subtle changes to **brain-building cells** that slow down neuron production. This research, while focused on marmosets, offers insights into the fundamental cellular mechanisms that govern cortical folding, a process crucial for the complex functions of the human brain. The study suggests that even minor alterations in cell proliferation rates during development can lead to significant differences in brain morphology.

## Re-evaluating Fish Oil and Refining Autism Definitions

For years, millions have taken **fish oil supplements** hoping to boost brain health, memory, and protect against neurodegenerative diseases like Alzheimer's. However, a surprising two-year clinical trial has cast doubt on these widely held beliefs. The study found that while fish oil supplements successfully delivered **omega-3 fatty acids** to the brain, they failed to improve memory or protect against brain shrinkage associated with Alzheimer's disease. This significant human clinical trial, involving a substantial cohort over two years, suggests that for healthy adults and those at risk of cognitive decline, fish oil may not offer the cognitive benefits many expect. Researchers suspect that the timing or specific formulation might be factors, or that the benefits are simply not as broad as previously hypothesized.

In other news, our understanding and definition of **profound autism** are evolving. A recent study by the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, or CDC, indicates a decline in the prevalence of profound autism due to an updated, more precise definition. This new definition incorporates **adaptive scores**, which measure an individual's ability to perform daily living skills, alongside traditional diagnostic criteria. By including these functional assessments, the CDC aims to provide a more nuanced and accurate picture of autism spectrum disorder, particularly for those with the most significant support needs. This change in definition has important implications for public health tracking, resource allocation, and clinical interventions.

Finally, the field of **human brain organoids** continues to mature, though with recognized limitations. Touted by the U.S. government as a promising alternative to animal models for studying the brain, these three-dimensional cell cultures have seen significant improvements since their debut in two thousand thirteen. Scientists are making strides in overcoming key limitations such as **immaturity** and **consistency** in their development. While organoids offer unprecedented opportunities to study human brain development and disease in a dish, researchers are actively working to enhance their complexity and reproducibility to fully realize their potential as a robust research tool.
