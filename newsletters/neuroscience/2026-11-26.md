Today's neuroscience clippings bring us exciting developments across diagnostics, potential therapeutics, and early disease markers. Researchers at Stanford have leveraged artificial intelligence to uncover distinct brain patterns underlying ADHD and anxiety, moving beyond traditional diagnostic labels. We also have news of a promising new nasal treatment that has shown remarkable success in restoring memory in aging mice and combating Alzheimer's-like symptoms. Finally, new findings suggest that depression in older adults might serve as an important early indicator for Alzheimer's disease, linked to the accumulation of tau protein.

## Uncovering Hidden Brain Types for ADHD and Anxiety

A significant new study from Stanford Medicine and the University of Iowa has identified three distinct **brain biotypes** in children with attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder, or ADHD, and anxiety, or both conditions. This human study, published in *Nature Mental Health* on October 16, utilized an **AI-powered analysis** of functional MRI, or fMRI, brain scans from over six thousand one hundred children, aged nine to ten, participating in the Adolescent Brain Cognitive Development, or ABCD, study. The findings were further validated in an additional two hundred and twenty-four children from the Healthy Brain Network study.

The research team, which included Yu Zhang, PhD, assistant professor of psychiatry and behavioral sciences at Stanford Medicine, and Yong Jiao, PhD, a postdoctoral scholar at the University of Iowa, found that these biotypes do not neatly align with traditional symptom-based diagnoses. The AI model examined **functional connectivity**, revealing two major dimensions of brain dysfunction: **cognitive-behavioral dysfunction** and **emotion-attention dysfunction**. The three identified biotypes are termed externalizing-disinhibited, internalizing-distressed, and mixed. Importantly, these distinct brain patterns were detectable in scans years before a clinical diagnosis was made, suggesting a potential for earlier detection and more personalized interventions for these common childhood mental health conditions.

## Nasal Drops Offer Hope for Memory and Alzheimer's in Mice

In a promising development for potential Alzheimer's therapeutics, researchers at the University of Sydney have developed experimental nose drops that significantly improved learning and memory in aging mice. This animal study, led by Professor Greg Neely at the Charles Perkins Centre, demonstrated that the treatment restored the cognitive performance of eighteen-month-old mice to levels comparable to much younger, three-month-old animals.

The innovative treatment employs **mRNA technology**, similar to that used in COVID-19 vaccines. It works by delivering **messenger RNA**, or mRNA, packaged in **lipid nanoparticles**, which instructs cells lining the nasal passages to produce **brain-derived neurotrophic factor**, or **BDNF**. BDNF is a naturally occurring protein vital for neuron survival, plasticity, and the formation of memories, and its levels are known to decrease with age and in Alzheimer's disease. Beyond restoring youthful memory in aged mice, the nasal drops also prevented severe cognitive decline in mice genetically engineered to develop Alzheimer's-like disease. These mice, known as 5xFAD models, typically exhibit memory problems around four months of age, but were protected when treatment began at two months, prior to symptom onset. Remarkably, this therapeutic effect was achieved without reducing the **amyloid plaques** that are a hallmark of Alzheimer's, suggesting a novel mechanism for combating cognitive decline. The findings, published in *Advanced Science*, suggest this non-invasive delivery system could bypass the **blood-brain barrier** and potentially complement existing Alzheimer's therapies.

## Depression as an Early Warning Sign for Alzheimer's

New research indicates that depression in older adults may be more than just a mental health concern, potentially serving as an early indicator of underlying Alzheimer's disease pathology. A human study published in *JNeurosci*, led by Teodora Markova of Brandeis University and her colleagues, found a direct link between increasing levels of **tau protein** and worsening depressive symptoms in older adults who still maintained normal memory and thinking abilities.

The researchers analyzed data from older men and women participating in the **Alzheimer's Disease Neuroimaging Initiative**. They observed that the faster tau protein, a hallmark of Alzheimer's that forms disruptive tangles in the brain, accumulated, the more rapidly depressive symptoms increased. This relationship was particularly evident in individuals who had not yet developed mild cognitive impairment or Alzheimer's disease, suggesting that mood changes could emerge before noticeable cognitive decline. While the study does not establish a direct causal link, it points to a significant biological connection, highlighting the importance of monitoring depressive trajectories in aging populations as a potential early risk assessment for neurodegenerative disease.
