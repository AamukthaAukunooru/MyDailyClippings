Today, we're diving into some significant developments across neuroscience, from large-scale brain mapping initiatives to new insights into animal cognition and computational approaches to neurological disorders. We'll also touch on a timely discussion about how we define "model organisms" and the expansive potential of network science to reshape our understanding of biology and health.

## Mapping the Primate Brain with the BRAIN Initiative

A major stride in neuroscience infrastructure comes from the **BRAIN Initiative**, which has entered a new phase focused on creating a comprehensive "parts list" for the **basal ganglia**. This ambitious project aims to develop a standardized cell naming system and robust infrastructure for data collection and analysis, ultimately setting the stage for a whole-brain primate atlas. The effort, highlighted in The Transmitter, underscores a collaborative push to systematically categorize the diverse cell types within this critical brain region. The basal ganglia are involved in a wide array of functions, including motor control, learning, and reward processing, and a detailed cellular map is expected to provide unprecedented clarity into their complex circuitry. This foundational work is crucial for understanding both healthy brain function and the mechanisms underlying neurological disorders that affect these circuits.

## Swift Decisions and Splicing Insights

New research from the University of Tübingen reveals that **crows** possess astonishingly fast decision-making capabilities. Scientists discovered that these intelligent birds can make visual decisions twice as quickly as humans and monkeys. The study highlights the remarkable efficiency of the avian brain, suggesting that crows process visual information and arrive at a choice with exceptional speed. While the specific neural mechanisms underlying this rapid processing are still being explored, this finding from an animal study adds to our understanding of comparative cognition and the diverse strategies brains employ for efficient decision-making.

On the computational front, a new **machine-learning model** has been developed to identify **autism-linked genetic variants** that disrupt **splicing**. Published in The Transmitter, this model offers a powerful tool for investigating genetic variations in regions of the genome that have historically been challenging to interpret. Splicing is a crucial process where non-coding regions, or introns, are removed from RNA transcripts, and errors can lead to dysfunctional proteins. By pinpointing variants that scramble this process, the model provides a new avenue for understanding the genetic underpinnings of autism spectrum disorder. This computational approach, which analyzes human genetic data, promises to accelerate the discovery of novel genetic risk factors and shed light on their functional consequences.

## Defining Models and Unpacking Networks

The scientific community is currently engaged in a thought-provoking discussion regarding the appropriate use of the term "**model organism**." An article in The Transmitter argues that this term should be reserved exclusively for biological systems, cautioning against its application to artificial neural networks. The concern is that referring to AI as "model organisms" could inadvertently distort funding priorities and diminish the explanatory power derived from the shared evolutionary history across biological organisms. This debate highlights the importance of precise terminology in science and its potential impact on research direction and resource allocation.

Meanwhile, the expansive field of **network science** continues to offer a unifying framework for understanding complex systems, from the internet to the intricate networks within our own bodies. In Quanta Magazine, **Albert-László Barabási** elaborates on how the same organizing principles govern these diverse systems. He suggests that by mapping the networks inside our bodies, we could revolutionize how we approach disease treatment and even how we think about nutrition. This perspective, while not a new finding, emphasizes a powerful interdisciplinary approach that could unlock deeper insights into biological function and dysfunction, including the complex neural networks that define our brains.
