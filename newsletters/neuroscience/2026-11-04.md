## Fear May Blur, Not Sharpen, Our Perception of Danger

A new discovery challenges long-held assumptions about how the brain processes fear, suggesting that this fundamental emotion may actually blur the distinction between dangerous and safe environments. This "better safe than sorry" mechanism, observed in mice, could offer fresh insights into conditions like anxiety and post-traumatic stress disorder, or PTSD.

Researchers at the Douglas Research Centre at McGill University and the University of Toronto, Scarborough, led by senior author Mark Brandon, an Associate Professor in McGill's Department of Psychiatry, conducted experiments using augmented reality and real-time brain imaging in mice. They trained the animals to associate one environment with danger and another with safety. Contrary to the expectation that fear would lead to clearer neural separation of these experiences, the scientists found that the brain activity patterns for threatening and harmless environments began to overlap.

This blurring of memories, published in *Nature Neuroscience*, suggests that fear might make the brain less precise in distinguishing between threat and safety, potentially allowing for faster, albeit sometimes overgeneralized, responses to perceived danger. Professor Brandon noted that these findings could reshape how scientists think about anxiety and PTSD, where individuals often experience intense fear in situations that are objectively safe. Understanding this underlying mechanism could pave the way for novel therapeutic approaches that aim to recalibrate the brain's fear response.

## Promising Alzheimer's Drug Repairs DNA Damage, Reduces Inflammation

In a significant development for Alzheimer's research, scientists have identified an experimental drug that not only repairs DNA damage but also reduces brain inflammation, two key features of the neurodegenerative disease. What makes this discovery particularly noteworthy is that the drug, known as **KCL-286**, has already completed initial Phase 1 human safety and tolerability trials for its original purpose: treating spinal cord injuries.

The research, conducted by scientists at King's College London, suggests that KCL-286 could offer a faster path toward new Alzheimer's therapies by targeting multiple disease-related pathways simultaneously, rather than focusing solely on the amyloid or tau proteins that have been the primary targets of many past treatments. Professor Jonathan Corcoran, Professor of Neuroscience at the Institute of Psychiatry, Psychology, and Neuroscience at King's College London, highlighted that the drug's prior human safety clearance could dramatically cut down the traditional multi-year timeline required for new drug development.

In mouse models of Alzheimer's disease, KCL-286 produced encouraging results, influencing several cellular pathways involved in the disease. This multi-pronged approach, addressing DNA damage and inflammation, represents a promising new strategy for tackling Alzheimer's, especially given that these biological changes can begin long before the disease reaches its advanced stages. While its effectiveness against Alzheimer's in humans still needs to be established through further clinical testing, the initial safety data offers a strong foundation for future research.

## Excess Weight Linked to Faster Brain Aging

A major 24-year study involving more than eight thousand two hundred adults suggests that carrying excess body weight may accelerate brain aging, leading to a faster decline in crucial cognitive skills. This large-scale human study, conducted by researchers at the University of Georgia, highlights the broader impact of obesity on long-term brain health.

The study, which tracked over eight thousand two hundred adults aged fifty and older for more than two decades, found a consistent relationship between higher body mass index, or BMI, and a more rapid rate of cognitive decline. Specifically, participants with higher BMIs experienced faster deterioration in memory, overall cognitive performance, and executive functioning. Executive functions are vital for everyday activities, encompassing abilities like concentrating, planning, organizing tasks, and regulating emotions. The connection between excess weight and accelerated brain aging was particularly pronounced in individuals over sixty-five years old.

While the study, published on November second, two thousand twenty-six, does not establish a direct causal link, it strongly suggests that body weight is a significant factor in maintaining cognitive health as we age. Each one-point increase in BMI was associated with a faster rate of cognitive decline. These findings underscore the importance of weight management not just for physical health, but also for preserving cognitive function and potentially mitigating the risk of age-related cognitive impairments.
