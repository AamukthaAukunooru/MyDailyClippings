Hello, and welcome to "My Daily Clippings." Today, November fifteenth, two thousand twenty-six, we're diving into a flurry of new findings that promise to reshape our understanding of brain health and disease. From novel approaches to detecting Alzheimer's years before symptoms emerge, to unprecedented efforts in mapping the brain's cellular landscape, and even a fresh look at environmental factors in Parkinson's, the neuroscience community is buzzing with significant developments. Many of these insights are being shared at the ongoing Neuroscience two thousand twenty-six conference in Washington, D.C., highlighting the collaborative and rapid pace of discovery.

## New Windows into Early Alzheimer's Detection

The quest for earlier and more accurate Alzheimer's disease diagnosis has seen significant strides this week, with two independent research efforts offering promising new biomarkers. Researchers at Stanford Medicine have identified hidden patterns of **brain activity** that can predict an individual's risk of Alzheimer's years before memory problems become apparent. This novel approach, published November fourth in *Science Advances*, not only flags vulnerability but also reveals which brains are remarkably resistant to aging, potentially identifying "super agers." The team developed a scoring system by comparing brain activity in adults aged seventy-five and older, including those with Alzheimer's and "super agers" whose memories remained sharp. This system was then applied to cognitively healthy adults between fifty-five and seventy, revealing a strong correlation between brain patterns and future cognitive health over follow-up periods extending up to twelve years. People whose brain patterns more closely resembled those of Alzheimer's patients were more likely to experience cognitive decline, accumulate amyloid-beta plaques, and receive an Alzheimer's diagnosis.

Adding to this, Johns Hopkins University researchers have pinpointed two specific brain regions that show significant signs of Alzheimer's disease even before the hippocampus, the area traditionally associated with memory loss, is affected. Presented at Neuroscience two thousand twenty-six, their work, recently published in *NeuroImage Reports*, suggests that the **entorhinal cortex** and **amygdala** begin to shrink earlier in the disease progression. This finding, based on the analysis of hundreds of brain scans from individuals at various stages of Alzheimer's, could provide crucial new biomarkers for diagnosis and intervention at a stage when treatments might be most effective. Biomedical engineer and senior author Michael Miller noted that these changes manifest as much as a decade before clinical symptoms appear, emphasizing the potential for early intervention.

## Charting the Brain's Cellular Universe

In a monumental undertaking, a global consortium of over fifty institutions and hundreds of scientists has unveiled the most detailed cellular atlas of the **basal ganglia**, a critical brain region involved in movement, learning, and decision-making. This landmark collection of twenty-six studies, published November twelfth in the *Cell Press* family of journals, is part of the National Institutes of Health's **BRAIN Initiative Cell Atlas Network**, or BICAN. The atlas catalogues the activities, identities, and locations of millions of individual cells within the basal ganglia across human, rhesus macaque, cynomolgus macaque, and marmoset brains, profiling twenty-one million human cells alone. This unprecedented level of detail provides a foundational resource for understanding brain function and disease, with all data openly available to the scientific community. John Ngai, director of the NIH BRAIN Initiative, highlighted that this granularity opens vast opportunities to understand basic brain function and the cellular underpinnings of neurological, neuropsychiatric, and substance use disorders.

Complementing this, researchers at the Broad Institute of MIT and Harvard, and Harvard Medical School, have charted previously hidden architecture and patterns of cellular makeup in the **striatum**, a key component of the basal ganglia. Their findings, reported November thirteenth, reveal six new molecular zones within the striatum and show that the proportions of certain neurons and glial cells decline with advancing age. By performing single-nucleus RNA sequencing on four million cells from the striatum and prefrontal cortex of nearly two hundred adult postmortem brains, the team discovered that while cellular balance is consistent within an individual, it varies significantly between people, particularly for non-neuronal cells like astrocytes and microglia. This work promises to enable more precise studies of brain disorders and contribute to a comprehensive cellular atlas of the entire human brain.

## New Evidence Links Herbicide to Parkinson's Risk

Preliminary new research is adding to the growing body of evidence suggesting a link between exposure to the herbicide **paraquat** and an increased risk of Parkinson's disease. Scientists affiliated with the University of Rochester and the University of Georgia conducted a study in laboratory mice, exposing them to air containing paraquat particles for sixty days. The findings, available as a preprint since November third and reported November twelfth, showed neurotoxic brain effects in the mice, including a reduction in **dopamine neurons** and impaired motor function.

Parkinson's disease is characterized by the degeneration of dopamine-producing neurons in the substantia nigra. Deborah Cory-Slechta, a neurotoxicologist at the University of Rochester Medical School and co-author of the study, emphasized that these findings, particularly the inhalation exposure route, bolster an already robust database of evidence. She noted that the impacts were greater in male mice than in female mice, a distinction also observed in human Parkinson's disease diagnoses, where men are affected at a higher rate than women. While this work is a preprint and has not yet undergone final peer review, it raises significant concerns regarding the contribution of paraquat exposure to the rising incidence of Parkinson's disease.
