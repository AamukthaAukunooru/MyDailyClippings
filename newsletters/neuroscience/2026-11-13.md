## Mapping the Basal Ganglia with the BRAIN Initiative

A significant step towards a comprehensive understanding of the primate brain has been announced, as the **BRAIN Initiative** enters a new phase focused on creating a detailed "parts list" for the **basal ganglia**. This ambitious project, reported by The Transmitter, aims to establish a standardized cell naming system and robust infrastructure for data collection and analysis, laying the groundwork for a whole-brain primate atlas. The basal ganglia, a group of subcortical nuclei, are crucial for motor control, learning, and reward processing, and their dysfunction is implicated in disorders like Parkinson's and Huntington's disease.

This effort is part of the larger **BRAIN Initiative Cell Census Network (BICCN)**, which seeks to characterize the full diversity of cell types in the mammalian brain. By developing a common language and methodology for identifying and classifying cells within the basal ganglia, researchers can more effectively compare findings across different labs and studies. This standardized approach is critical for building a truly comprehensive and interoperable dataset, moving beyond fragmented knowledge to a unified understanding of brain circuitry. The ultimate goal is to create a detailed atlas that maps every cell type, its location, and its connections, providing an unprecedented resource for neuroscientists worldwide. This work primarily involves **animal studies**, specifically focusing on non-human primates, to create a foundational map that can inform our understanding of the human brain.

## AI Uncovers Autism's Splicing Secrets

In a promising development for understanding the genetic underpinnings of **autism spectrum disorder (ASD)**, a new machine-learning model has demonstrated its ability to identify autism-linked genetic variants that disrupt **gene splicing**. The Transmitter reports that this computational tool offers a novel way to investigate genetic variations in regions of the genome whose functions have historically been challenging to interpret. Many genetic studies of autism have focused on protein-coding regions, but a significant portion of the genome, including non-coding regions, plays a crucial role in regulating gene expression.

Gene splicing is a vital process where non-coding introns are removed from messenger RNA (mRNA) precursors, and coding exons are joined together to form mature mRNA. Errors in this process can lead to altered or non-functional proteins, potentially contributing to neurodevelopmental conditions like autism. The new machine-learning model is designed to predict the impact of genetic variants on splicing, even in areas previously considered "dark matter" of the genome. This **computational study** provides a powerful new lens through which to examine the complex genetic architecture of autism, potentially revealing new therapeutic targets or diagnostic markers. By pinpointing specific splicing aberrations, researchers can gain a deeper understanding of how genetic changes translate into biological dysfunction in the brain, paving the way for more precise interventions.

## Network Science: A New Lens for Health

Quanta Magazine recently featured an insightful discussion with **Albert-László Barabási**, a pioneer in **network science**, on how this field could fundamentally transform our approach to health and nutrition. Barabási explains that the same organizing principles that govern complex systems like the internet also apply to biological systems, from the intricate web of interactions within a cell to the broader physiological networks of the human body. By mapping these internal networks, we could gain unprecedented insights into disease mechanisms and personalize treatments.

Barabási's work emphasizes that diseases are often not isolated malfunctions but rather disturbances within a complex network of interacting molecules, cells, and organs. For example, understanding the **interactome**, the complete set of molecular interactions in a cell, can reveal how a single genetic mutation might ripple through the system, leading to disease. Similarly, in nutrition, a network-based approach could move beyond simplistic "good" or "bad" food classifications to understand how different dietary components interact within an individual's unique metabolic network. This perspective suggests a shift from a reductionist view of biology to a holistic, systems-level understanding. While the immediate findings are conceptual and theoretical, the implications for **human health** and **drug discovery** are profound, promising a future where treatments are tailored not just to a disease, but to an individual's specific biological network architecture. This approach, often leveraging large datasets from **human studies** and **cell studies**, aims to identify critical nodes and pathways that can be targeted for therapeutic benefit.
