## Optogenetics: A Nobel-Winning Revolution

The scientific community recently celebrated a monumental achievement with the awarding of the 2026 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine to Karl Deisseroth of Stanford University and the Howard Hughes Medical Institute, Peter Hegemann of Humboldt University of Berlin, and Georg Nagel of the University of Würzburg. Their groundbreaking work on **optogenetics** has been recognized for its profound impact on neuroscience. This revolutionary technique allows scientists to control the activity of nerve cells using light, fundamentally transforming how we study the brain.

The journey began with Hegemann and Nagel's discovery of **channelrhodopsin**, a light-sensitive protein found in single-celled green algae. They found that introducing this protein into various cells made them light-sensitive, capable of generating electrical signals when exposed to blue light. Building on this, Karl Deisseroth then ingeniously transformed channelrhodopsin into a light-controlled switch for nerve cells. In 2005, he demonstrated this by introducing the gene for channelrhodopsin into rat nerve cells, triggering electrical signals with blue light. Just two years later, he successfully applied this light-controlled switch in the brains of living mice, enabling researchers to precisely activate or silence specific neural circuits.

This precision has opened unprecedented opportunities for mapping brain circuits involved in movement, memory, emotions, and behavior. Beyond fundamental research, optogenetics is now being explored for potential clinical applications, including attempts to restore vision in individuals with severe visual impairment and to develop treatments for neurological disorders such as Parkinson's disease and epilepsy. As Per Svenningsson, Chair of the Nobel Committee for Physiology or Medicine, noted, optogenetics offers "opportunities for mapping the brain in a way that we could once only dream of".

## New Insights into Alzheimer's Risk and Prevention

In the realm of neurodegenerative diseases, recent findings offer both new diagnostic potential and intriguing dietary considerations for cognitive health.

A large international study, led by scientists at the Mass General Brigham Neuroscience Institute, has revealed that a simple blood test could help predict an individual's risk of Alzheimer's-related cognitive decline years before symptoms appear. Published in *JAMA* on October thirtieth, two thousand twenty-six, the research focused on **phosphorylated tau 217 (p-tau217)**, a blood biomarker strongly associated with Alzheimer's disease. By combining data from six observational studies and clinical trials conducted across three continents, researchers found a significant correlation between higher p-tau217 levels and the likelihood of future cognitive problems. Among older adults with very high levels of this biomarker, there was an estimated thirty-eight percent chance of developing cognitive impairment within five years, with the risk rising to an estimated seventy-eight percent over ten years, though the longer-term data was based on fewer participants. Senior and corresponding author Reisa Sperling, a neurologist with the Mass General Brigham Neuroscience Institute, highlighted that while disease-modifying treatments for asymptomatic high-risk individuals are not yet available, these findings pave the way for earlier intervention strategies aimed at preventing Alzheimer's before memory issues begin.

Adding to the conversation around cognitive health, a cross-sectional epidemiological and targeted lipidomics study of one thousand nine hundred seventeen community-dwelling older adults suggests a link between dietary **lecithin** intake and a reduced risk of **mild cognitive impairment (MCI)**. Reported on October thirtieth, two thousand twenty-six, the study found that higher dietary intake of lecithin, a primary source of **phosphatidylcholine**, was significantly associated with better cognitive performance on the Montreal Cognitive Assessment, or MoCA, and a lower risk of MCI. Lipidomic profiling further revealed that older adults with MCI had lower total phosphatidylcholine levels and an unfavorable ratio of saturated to polyunsaturated fatty acids. Interestingly, the cognitive protection associated with lecithin was more pronounced in individuals with lower educational attainment, suggesting that increasing dietary lecithin, found in foods like eggs, soybeans, and sunflower seeds, could be a cost-effective public health strategy to address cognitive health disparities.

## Promising Phase Three Results for Relapsing Multiple Sclerosis

In a significant development for the treatment of **multiple sclerosis (MS)**, new Phase 3 clinical trial results for the oral **BTK inhibitor remibrutinib** were reported today, November first, two thousand twenty-six. The trials demonstrated that remibrutinib significantly reduced the **annualized relapse rate** in patients with **relapsing multiple sclerosis (RMS)**. Specifically, the drug showed a thirty-five point two percent and thirty-one point two percent reduction compared to teriflunomide, a commonly used MS medication.

Importantly, the trials reported no liver safety signal or cases of Hy's Law, addressing a key safety concern often associated with some other BTK inhibitors. Heinz Wiendl, a Professor of Neurology and Chair of the Department of Neurology and Neurophysiology at the Medical Center, University of Freiburg, commented that the REMODEL trial results showed remibrutinib delivered a significant reduction in relapse activity and a clinically meaningful effect on confirmed disability progression. These positive Phase 3 outcomes suggest that remibrutinib could become a valuable new oral treatment option for individuals living with RMS, with the company planning to seek regulatory approval globally.
