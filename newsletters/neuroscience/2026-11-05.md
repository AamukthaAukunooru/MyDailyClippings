Happy Thursday, brain enthusiasts. Today's clippings bring us a fascinating mix of evolutionary insights, crucial findings for child development, and a promising new lead in the fight against Alzheimer's disease. We'll explore how ancient fish might explain our brain's curious cross-wiring, learn about a worm that's challenging our understanding of brain evolution, delve into how diet and specific cognitive skills impact children's developing minds, and finally, look at a drug originally for spinal cord injuries that shows potential against Alzheimer's.

## Rewriting the Story of Brain Evolution

Two recent findings are prompting us to reconsider fundamental aspects of brain organization and evolution. First, scientists may have finally explained the long-standing mystery of why our brains are **cross-wired**, meaning each hemisphere connects to the opposite side of the body. Researchers propose that this unique arrangement, which is conserved across vertebrates, might have originated more than 500 million years ago in the visual systems of ancient fish. This hypothesis suggests that the initial crossover was a mechanism to integrate visual information from both eyes, a foundational step that then influenced the development of motor and sensory pathways.

Adding another layer to our understanding of early brain development, a 2026 preprint highlights the **three-banded panther worm** as a creature with a brain structure unlike what was previously expected. This animal's unique brain organization suggests that brains could have evolved **centralization**, the gathering of neurons into a central processing unit, *before* **regionalization**, the specialization of different brain areas for specific functions. This finding challenges the conventional view of brain evolution, hinting at a more diverse and complex evolutionary path for nervous systems than previously thought.

## Diet, Cognition, and Children's Developing Brains

New research offers important insights into factors influencing children's brain development, from dietary habits to the specific skills that predict academic success. A study from the University of Southern California (USC) investigated the impact of **ultra-processed foods** on children's brains. Researchers studied 46 children between the ages of seven and ten, finding that those who consumed more ultra-processed foods showed a troubling brain response. Specifically, these foods may influence developing brains in ways that encourage weight gain, highlighting a potential neural mechanism behind the link between diet and childhood obesity. This human study underscores the importance of nutrition for cognitive health during critical developmental windows.

Separately, a massive new study has shed light on the most significant predictors of reading success in children, challenging some long-held assumptions. The research revealed that **intelligence** plays a surprisingly limited role in a child's ability to read well, and **visual processing** showed no meaningful connection at all. Instead, the strongest predictors for reading success were found to be **sound recognition** and **language comprehension**. This large-scale human study suggests that interventions focused on phonological awareness and understanding spoken language may be more effective in fostering reading skills than those targeting general intelligence or visual perception.

## A Promising New Avenue for Alzheimer's Treatment

In a significant development for neurodegenerative disease research, scientists have identified a new drug that shows promise in tackling several key features of **Alzheimer's disease**. This drug, which was originally developed for spinal cord injuries, has been found to repair **DNA damage** and reduce **brain inflammation**, both of which are critical pathological hallmarks of Alzheimer's. The discovery is particularly encouraging because the drug has already undergone initial safety testing in humans for its original purpose. This pre-existing safety data could significantly accelerate its development for Alzheimer's, offering a potential new therapeutic strategy to address the complex and multifaceted nature of the disease.
