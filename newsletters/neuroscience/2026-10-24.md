## A New Horizon for PTSD Treatment

In a significant development for mental healthcare, a novel treatment for **post-traumatic stress disorder**, or **PTSD**, has received FDA clearance. The **MeRT (Magnetic EEG-guided Resonance Therapy) System**, developed by California-based Wave Neuroscience in collaboration with researchers at the Texas A&M Health Institute of Biosciences and Technology, is the first personalized, biomarker-guided neuromodulation technology to be cleared for PTSD.

This innovative approach moves beyond traditional one-size-fits-all treatments by using **personalized brain mapping**. It involves analyzing a patient's unique brain activity patterns via **electroencephalography (EEG)**, then guiding targeted **transcranial magnetic stimulation (TMS)** to normalize abnormal brain activity.

Early clinical trial findings for the MeRT System have been remarkably encouraging. The double-blinded, randomized, controlled multisite trial, which included 158 adult patients, reported a 52 percent reduction in PTSD symptoms. Even more notably, 68 percent of participants achieved full remission from their symptoms. This represents a substantial improvement over previous medication outcomes, which have shown remission rates of no more than 10.9 percent in some reviews. Erik Won, DO, chief medical officer of Wave Neuroscience, described these results as a "quantum change" in what can be offered to patients. This personalized approach holds promise for millions of individuals who have not found sufficient relief from existing PTSD therapies.

## Unveiling the Brain's Hidden Messaging System

Scientists at the Allen Institute have made a fascinating discovery about a tiny, deep-seated brain region previously thought to be a simple broadcaster of chemical signals. New research in mice reveals that the **locus coeruleus (LC)**, located within the brainstem, operates with a far more sophisticated communication system than previously understood.

Historically, the LC, which produces the chemical messenger **norepinephrine (NE)**, was believed to act like a loudspeaker, sending essentially the same signal across wide areas of the brain to regulate functions like attention, learning, and stress responses. However, this new study, published in *Nature*, challenges that notion. Using advanced brain imaging, recordings of neural activity, genetic techniques, and behavioral experiments in mice, the researchers found that the LC communicates through several distinct pathways.

Instead of a general broadcast, the LC delivers targeted messages to specific destinations. Different groups of neurons within the LC respond to different experiences, with neurons in the upper, or dorsal, portion primarily connecting to the cerebral cortex and forebrain, while cells in the lower, or ventral, portion send signals mainly to the brainstem and spinal cord. This more organized network of communication suggests that the LC plays a precise role in modulating brain function. The researchers also noted intriguing similarities between the norepinephrine system and the brain's **dopamine** signaling system, hinting that these major chemical messengers may collaborate to support complex learning processes.

## Fish Oil Supplements: No Brain Benefit in Two-Year Trial

For years, millions have turned to fish oil supplements, hoping to boost brain health and ward off cognitive decline. However, a comprehensive two-year clinical trial has delivered a surprising blow to this popular belief, finding no significant benefit for memory or protection against Alzheimer's-related brain shrinkage.

The study, conducted by researchers at Keck Medicine of USC, including lead investigator Hussein Naji Yassine, MD, director of the USC Center for Personalized Brain Health, involved 365 older adults between the ages of 55 and 80. All participants rarely consumed fish, a primary dietary source of **omega-3 fatty acids**, and were considered at elevated risk for Alzheimer's disease, with nearly half carrying the **APOE4 gene**, a strong genetic risk factor.

Participants were randomly assigned to receive either daily fish oil supplements containing 2,000 milligrams of **docosahexaenoic acid (DHA)**, a key omega-3, or a placebo. The researchers confirmed that the supplements successfully delivered omega-3s to the brain, observing an average 17 percent increase in DHA levels in cerebrospinal fluid after six months. Despite this, the study found no measurable improvements in memory or other cognitive abilities compared to the placebo group. Furthermore, brain imaging revealed that the supplements did not prevent shrinkage of the **hippocampus**, a brain region crucial for memory and often used as a marker of brain aging and Alzheimer's risk.

Published in *eBioMedicine*, these findings suggest that while omega-3s are vital for brain cell connections, obtaining them through supplements may not provide the protective benefits many people expect. Researchers now suspect that the benefits associated with omega-3s might stem from a healthy overall diet rather than isolated supplements.
