Greetings, fellow brain enthusiasts, and welcome to your Monday edition of "My Daily Clippings." This past weekend, the neuroscience world converged in Washington, D.C., for the annual Society for Neuroscience meeting, Neuroscience 2026, bringing with it a flurry of exciting new data and discussions. From unprecedented maps of the human brain to fresh insights into Alzheimer's disease and even a new federal initiative, it's been a busy few days for understanding our most complex organ.

## Unveiling the Brain's Control Center: The Basal Ganglia Atlas

A monumental achievement from the National Institutes of Health's **BRAIN Initiative Cell Atlas Network**, or BICAN, has delivered the most comprehensive map yet of the human **basal ganglia**. This deep brain region is a critical hub for functions like movement, learning, and emotion, and its dysfunction is implicated in disorders such as Parkinson's disease, Huntington's disease, Tourette syndrome, and addiction. Researchers, including John Ngai, Ph.D., director of the NIH BRAIN Initiative, described this landmark work in a collection of twenty-eight papers published on November twelfth in Cell Press journals.

The atlas details the activities, identities, and locations of millions of individual cells within the basal ganglia across different developmental stages. Scientists employed single-cell sequencing on donated postmortem human brain tissue, combining this with advanced imaging techniques to link cellular characteristics to three-dimensional coordinates. This human-centric data is further enriched by parallel atlases from non-human primate, marmoset, and mouse brains, providing invaluable resources for preclinical models.

One key finding from this extensive human study is the discovery of six new molecular zones within the **striatum**, a major component of the basal ganglia. The research also revealed that the proportions of certain types of neurons and glial cells in the striatum decline with advancing age, and that the boundaries of these molecular zones can blur over time. Researchers Margarita Behrens and Joseph Ecker from the Salk Institute were among the global collaborators who contributed to this detailed mapping, which promises to accelerate the study of brain disorders and pave the way for targeted therapies.

## New Clues to Alzheimer's: Earlier Detection and Systemic Links

The ongoing Neuroscience 2026 conference has been a hotbed for Alzheimer's disease research, with several presentations highlighting promising avenues for earlier detection and a deeper understanding of its systemic connections.

Johns Hopkins University scientists have identified potential early warning signs of Alzheimer's that may appear up to a decade before memory loss becomes noticeable. Through the examination of hundreds of human brain scans, researchers, including Michael I. Miller and Yi Xie, found that two regions involved in memory and emotion, the **entorhinal cortex** and the **amygdala**, begin to shrink earlier than the **hippocampus**, the area typically monitored for Alzheimer's. These early changes are linked to the buildup of abnormal **tau protein**, suggesting new biomarkers for identifying individuals at risk.

In a complementary finding, Stanford University researchers have uncovered hidden patterns of brain activity that can predict Alzheimer's risk years before symptoms emerge. This approach, published on November fourth in *Science Advances*, also identified individuals whose brains exhibit exceptional resistance to cognitive decline, dubbed "super agers." The study involved cognitively healthy adults between fifty-five and seventy years old, as well as those aged seventy-five and older with and without Alzheimer's. These subtle brain activity patterns proved more effective at predicting future cognitive decline than established indicators like genetic risk factors or **amyloid-beta** and tau biomarkers.

Further research presented at Neuroscience 2026 emphasized the intricate connections between physical health and brain health in Alzheimer's. Andrei Vakhtin from the University of New Mexico reported that Long COVID patients exhibited "leaky gut" symptoms correlated with higher amyloid levels in their blood, suggesting a role for the **gut microbiome** in Alzheimer's risk. Meanwhile, Bart Rypma of The University of Texas at Dallas presented findings that even healthy older adults had less efficient brain waste-clearance systems, which were associated with decreased cognitive function. Another intriguing finding from Elaine Bearer, also from the University of New Mexico, revealed plastic-like particles in or around blood vessels in the deep brain tissue of patients who died from Alzheimer's, potentially contributing to hemorrhage or inflammation.

## Advancing Neuroscience: FDA Initiative and Psychedelic Insights

Beyond individual research findings, the landscape of neuroscience is also being shaped by new policy initiatives and explorations into novel therapeutic mechanisms.

On October twenty-second, the Food and Drug Administration, or FDA, launched the **Neuroscience Advancement and Innovation Nexus**, known as NAIN. This agency-wide initiative aims to accelerate the development of medical products for diseases and conditions affecting the brain and nervous system. NAIN's inaugural focus is the **Alzheimer's Prevention Accelerator**, which seeks to advance the science, tools, and regulatory approaches needed to develop treatments that prevent or delay the onset or progression of cognitive decline due to Alzheimer's disease. This initiative underscores a concerted effort to overcome the unique challenges in neuroscience product development by leveraging scientific advances in biomarkers and digital health technologies.

In a fascinating development from the University of California, Santa Cruz, researchers have shed light on how **psilocybin**, the active compound in "magic mushrooms," reorganizes connections between nerve cells in the brain. Published on October fifteenth in *Nature Neuroscience*, this animal study, conducted in mice, revealed that psilocybin rapidly encourages brain cells to form new connections and helps these connections mature. Intriguingly, the team was able to separate the cellular changes responsible for this brain rewiring from the behavioral responses typically associated with psychedelic effects. This discovery could provide a foundation for developing new medications that enhance the brain's ability to adapt and reorganize itself, a property known as **neuroplasticity**, potentially offering new approaches for conditions like depression and post-traumatic stress disorder, without inducing hallucinations.
