## The Developing Brain's Quality Control System

A landmark study published in *Nature* has shed new light on a fundamental process in early brain development: how the fetal brain eliminates millions of neurons. Researchers from Harvard Medical School and Boston Children’s Hospital have discovered that the developing brain employs a sophisticated **competitive system** to weed out genetically flawed neurons. This process explains why the prenatal brain initially overproduces a vast number of neurons, only to eliminate a significant portion of them before birth.

The study, which involved animal models, suggests that neurons with genetic defects are at a disadvantage in this competition, leading to their removal. This **neuronal pruning** is crucial for ensuring the formation of healthy and efficient neural circuits. Understanding this intrinsic quality control mechanism could have profound implications for our comprehension of neurodevelopmental disorders, where such pruning processes might go awry. The findings highlight the intricate biological safeguards in place during the earliest stages of brain formation, ensuring that only the most robust and functional neurons are integrated into the developing neural networks.

## Re-evaluating CTE Prevalence in NFL Veterans

New research has provided a more accurate picture of the prevalence of **chronic traumatic encephalopathy**, or **CTE**, among deceased professional football players. A study published yesterday addressed previous limitations related to **brain donation selection bias**, which often skewed earlier estimates. By correcting for this bias, the researchers now estimate that a staggering 74.5 percent of all NFL players who died between 2008 and 2023 had evidence of CTE.

This significant finding underscores the widespread impact of repetitive head trauma in professional football. Previous studies, often relying on brains donated specifically due to suspected neurological issues, likely overestimated the prevalence. However, this new analysis, by accounting for the characteristics of the broader population of deceased NFL players, paints a stark and concerning picture of the disease's reach. The study involved post-mortem examination of brain tissue from deceased NFL veterans, providing critical pathological evidence. This revised estimate serves as a crucial piece of data for ongoing discussions about player safety, long-term health, and the future of contact sports.

## Autistic Brains Process Faces Differently, and Earlier

A major new study has challenged previous assumptions about how autistic children's brains process faces, suggesting these differences may emerge earlier than scientists once thought. Published yesterday, the research reveals that the **autistic brain** processes faces in a distinct manner, with implications for understanding social cognition in autism.

The study found that **brain signals** associated with recognizing faces differ in autistic children compared to neurotypical children. This suggests that the neural pathways involved in face perception are organized or function differently from an early age. While the specific sample size was not detailed in the initial report, the designation as a "major new study" implies a robust investigation. These findings could lead to earlier identification of atypical social processing and potentially inform interventions aimed at supporting social development in autistic individuals. By pinpointing these differences in brain signals, researchers are gaining a more nuanced understanding of the neurological underpinnings of social perception in autism.
