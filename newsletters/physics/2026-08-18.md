## The Proton's Hidden Glue and Matter's Stability

For decades, the proton, a fundamental building block of atomic nuclei, has been understood to carry its **baryon number**, a conserved quantum property, primarily through its three constituent quarks. However, new results from the **STAR detector** at the **Relativistic Heavy Ion Collider (RHIC)** are challenging this long-held view, suggesting that **gluons**, the "glue-like" particles binding quarks together, play a central and surprising role in this conservation.

Physicists at RHIC, including researchers from Rice University like Nicole Lewis, found evidence that baryon number may be carried by a **Y-shaped "gluon junction"** that connects the proton's three main quarks. This discovery emerged from experiments where high-energy particle collisions at RHIC allowed scientists to track baryon number independently of electric charge. The data indicated that the gluon junction can be stopped more easily than the quarks themselves, with its energy transforming into new baryons that spray out perpendicularly to the beamline, while the quarks continue forward. This observation provides compelling support for the existence of this gluon junction. Zhangbu Xu, a professor at Kent State University with a joint appointment at Brookhaven Lab, noted that these findings strongly support the idea that baryon number is more favorably carried and transported by gluons in this special configuration, rather than solely by the three valence quarks. This profound insight could necessitate a rewrite of physics textbooks and deepen our understanding of why matter, and indeed the entire universe, remains stable.

## Reshaping Our Understanding of Matter

Beyond the proton, physicists are also making waves in our understanding of how matter can organize itself, from predicting new quantum states to fundamentally redefining the behavior of fluids. Researchers at **Monash University** in Australia have predicted a novel form of **quantum matter** that challenges decades of thought on ultracold particle behavior. Lead author Sam Foster, a PhD candidate at Monash's School of Physics and Astronomy, and his collaborators, including those from Heidelberg University in Germany, propose that under specific conditions, mixtures of **bosons** and **fermions**, two fundamentally different types of quantum particles, can form stable, self-bound **"quantum droplets."** This is remarkable because scientists previously believed such exotic droplets were unlikely in strongly interacting Bose-Fermi systems. The stability of these droplets arises from a delicate balance where an attractive force between particles is precisely counteracted by the pressure generated by the fermions, preventing collapse. This theoretical roadmap opens new avenues for experimental exploration and could be crucial for future quantum technologies, from ultra-precise sensors to quantum computing.

Meanwhile, the classical understanding of **fluid dynamics** is undergoing a significant update, with physicists redefining fluids from the ground up. For over a century, the same theoretical framework has largely governed our comprehension of fluid behavior. Now, with modern insights, a new approach is unifying three distinct theories of fluid dynamics: the microscopic, mesoscopic, and macroscopic perspectives. This breakthrough, which mathematically justifies theories like the **Navier-Stokes equations**, strengthens our confidence in these foundational principles and provides a more cohesive understanding of how fluids move, from individual particles to large-scale flows.

Adding to the exotic properties of matter, a new graphene device has successfully measured **fractional electric charges**, a phenomenon where electrons, typically considered indivisible, appear to split into fractions of their fundamental unit of charge. Researchers at the École Polytechnique Fédérale de Lausanne (EPFL) in Switzerland, in collaboration with institutions in Japan and the UK, utilized a novel **antidot** within a graphene sheet to isolate the **fractional quantum Hall effect**. This allowed for direct and straightforward conductance measurements of quasiparticle tunneling, revealing fractional charges such as e/3 and 2e/3. This practical method to study these strange quasiparticles is a significant step towards understanding exotic quantum properties and could extend to other **van der Waals materials**.

## Cutting-Edge Optics for Vision and Computation

In the realm of applied physics, advancements in optics are promising to enhance our ability to see through obstacles and dramatically improve data processing. A new **time-gating technique** has demonstrated the ability to "see through" challenging mediums like deep tissue and dense fog. This method leverages **near-infrared light**, which scatters less than visible light in such environments, to produce clear images where traditional methods would yield only blur. The technique works by selectively capturing light that has traveled the shortest path through the scattering medium, effectively filtering out scattered light that would degrade image quality. Such a breakthrough has profound implications, from assisting doctors in detecting cancer and other abnormalities within the human body to guiding autonomous vehicles safely through adverse weather conditions.

Complementing these imaging advancements, Caltech scientists have achieved a significant breakthrough in **integrated photonics**, creating **ultra-low-loss optical pathways on silicon chips** that rival the efficiency of fiber optics. This innovation dramatically outperforms existing technology, especially at visible wavelengths, where previous silicon-based platforms struggled with significant signal loss. Led by Professor Kerry Vahala, along with postdoctoral scholar Hao-Jing Chen and graduate student Kellan Colburn, the Caltech team developed a method using lithographically fabricated **germano-silicate waveguides** on standard silicon wafers. A thermal reflow process smooths these waveguides to near-atomic roughness, drastically reducing scattering loss. This breakthrough could unlock a new generation of more powerful lasers, miniature atomic sensors and clocks, and advanced quantum systems, while also leading to more energy-efficient data centers.
