## Superconductivity's Shifting Landscape

Two recent findings are significantly recalibrating our understanding of **superconductors**, materials that conduct electricity with zero resistance. For four decades, a particular class of these materials, crucial for technological applications, has been treated as uniformly structured. However, chemists at the University of Warwick have now demonstrated that these materials are, in fact, a complex mosaic of different crystal structures. Using advanced 3D imaging techniques, the team, including researchers like Dr. Ross Springell, revealed that this inhomogeneity is far more prevalent than previously assumed. This discovery, published in *Nature Communications*, forces a re-evaluation of fundamental models of superconductivity and could lead to new approaches for designing more efficient superconducting devices.

Complementing this, physicists at Cornell University have uncovered a critical factor in controlling **high-temperature superconductivity** in iron selenide (FeSe), a unique material of intense research interest. Contrary to the long-held belief that varying the electron count was the primary tuning knob, the Cornell team, led by Professor J.C. Séamus Davis, found that minimizing **disorder** within the material is the key. Their work, detailed in *Science* magazine, shows that reducing structural imperfections dramatically enhances superconductivity. This paradigm shift suggests that future efforts to engineer higher-temperature superconductors might focus less on chemical doping and more on pristine material synthesis.

## Unveiling Quantum Jumps and Decoding Decoherence

The quantum world continues to surprise and challenge our classical intuitions. A team at Stanford University has achieved a remarkable feat: the first direct observation of **quantum jumps** of sound in a mechanical resonator. This completes a scientific quest initiated over a century ago, demonstrating that even macroscopic mechanical systems can exhibit discrete energy transitions, much like electrons in an atom. The researchers, led by Professor Amir Safavi-Naeini, used a tiny silicon resonator cooled to near absolute zero to detect individual phonons, the quantum particles of sound, jumping between energy levels. This experimental confirmation, published in *Nature*, provides a new platform for exploring fundamental quantum mechanics and could have implications for quantum sensing and information processing.

Meanwhile, an underground experiment has provided crucial evidence regarding **quantum decoherence**, the perplexing process by which quantum systems lose their fragile quantum properties and transition to the classical world. Researchers, including those from the Vienna University of Technology, conducted experiments deep beneath the Gran Sasso mountain in Italy, in a facility designed to shield against environmental noise. By carefully observing the behavior of tiny silica nanoparticles, they were able to rule out a specific class of theoretical models that proposed gravity as the primary driver of quantum decoherence. This finding, published in *Physical Review Letters*, narrows down the possibilities for what "kills Schrödinger's cat" and brings us closer to understanding the boundary between the quantum and classical realms.

## Forging the Quantum Internet

The vision of a **quantum internet**, a network capable of transmitting quantum information securely and efficiently, continues to drive groundbreaking research. Two recent developments highlight significant strides in building the essential components for such a network.

Physicists at The City College of New York have demonstrated a novel method to convert **microwave signals** into **light** using magnetic waves within a layered semiconductor. This conversion is a critical challenge for quantum networks, as quantum processors often operate at microwave frequencies, while long-distance communication relies on optical fibers. The team, led by Professor Vinod M. Menon, utilized **2D magnets** to create a materials platform that enables this efficient and low-noise transduction, opening a new avenue for bridging different quantum technologies. Their work, published in *Nature Physics*, represents a crucial step towards integrating superconducting quantum circuits with fiber-optic networks.

Further advancing the quantum network architecture, researchers have developed a new **quantum memory device** that promises to synchronize faraway nodes in a network. For a truly global quantum internet, distant quantum computers must be able to coordinate their operations precisely in time. This new device, described in *APS Physics Magazine*, addresses this challenge by providing a robust and long-lived quantum memory capable of storing and releasing quantum information on demand, thereby enabling the necessary temporal coordination. This development, led by researchers at the California Institute of Technology and the Jet Propulsion Laboratory, is a key ingredient for establishing reliable and scalable quantum communication across vast distances.
