My Daily Clippings

Today, physics news brings us a fascinating array of advancements, from probing the existence of parallel universes to manipulating light and matter at the quantum scale, and even reshaping metals with "hot" electrons. These discoveries push the boundaries of our understanding and pave the way for future technologies in quantum computing, materials science, and fundamental particle physics.

## Unlocking Quantum Control and Computation

In a significant step forward for quantum optics and potential quantum computing applications, researchers from Skoltech, in collaboration with the N.D. Zelinsky Institute of Organic Chemistry and Westlake University, have experimentally demonstrated a continuous transition between **strong and weak coupling regimes** in a polariton microcavity. Polaritons, quasiparticles that are a hybrid of light and matter, are crucial for developing novel nanoscale lasers with low activation thresholds and for advancing quantum technologies. The team achieved this by fabricating microcavities with variable thickness, allowing them to observe the smooth shift from a strong coupling regime, where **polariton condensates** form, to a weak coupling regime, where the structure functions as a conventional laser. This work, published in *Nanophotonics*, could lead to ultrafast optical transistors and coherent light sources operating at room temperature.

Meanwhile, the practical realization of quantum computers takes another stride with new research focusing on speeding up **qubit readout** while reducing hardware complexity. Qubits, the fundamental units of quantum information, can exist in multiple states simultaneously, a phenomenon known as **superposition**. However, extracting information from these delicate quantum states, a process called measurement or readout, is often slow and prone to errors, which can range from 1-10% in state-of-the-art superconducting quantum processors. Researchers at Quantum Motion and University College London (UCL) have introduced a new technique called **radiofrequency electron cascade** for spin qubits in silicon metal-oxide-semiconductor devices. This method significantly amplifies the signal by using a third quantum dot coupled to a charge reservoir, demonstrating a minimum integration time of 8 microseconds, a substantial improvement over previous dispersive readout results in similar platforms. This advancement is directly compatible with standard industrial silicon manufacturing, offering a compact, fast, and scalable solution essential for building utility-scale quantum computers.

## Peering Beyond Our Universe and Into Molecular Chirality

A long-standing theoretical postulate in physics, the existence of a **mirror world** whose particles interact only feebly with our own, has been put to a rigorous test. This hypothetical mirror universe, with its own mirror electrons, protons, and neutrons, is also considered a candidate for **dark matter**. Researchers at the Paul Scherrer Institute (PSI) in Switzerland, in collaboration with ETH Zurich and Jagiellonian University in Kraków, have examined approximately 25 billion ultracold neutrons, achieving an unsurpassed level of precision in their search for evidence of neutrons oscillating into their mirror versions. The results, published in *Phys.org*, rule out, with a very high degree of certainty, the disappearance of neutrons into this mirror world. While previous experiments at the Institut Laue-Langevin in France had rekindled speculation about mirror neutrons, this new PSI experiment significantly constrains the theoretical parameter space, guiding theoretical physicists to explore new avenues in their quest to understand dark matter and fundamental symmetries.

In a different realm of fundamental interaction, scientists have developed a novel technique using **twisted laser light** to distinguish between mirror-image molecules, known as **enantiomers** or **chiral molecules**. Many molecules, particularly in biological systems and pharmaceuticals, exist in these two forms that are non-superimposable mirror images of each other, and their handedness can dramatically affect their properties and functions. Traditional methods for detecting chirality often rely on subtle differences in light absorption or electron emission, requiring complex equipment and precise alignment. Scientists from the Tata Institute of Fundamental Research, Indian Institute of Technology Mumbai, and Indian Institute of Technology Hyderabad engineered laser beams that not only spin but also twist as they propagate. When these structured light beams strike a chiral molecule, the interaction changes depending on how the light's "twist" matches the molecule's handedness, producing different charged fragments. Analyzing these fragments with a time-of-flight mass spectrometer allows for a faster, simpler, and more sensitive identification of molecular handedness, which could revolutionize analysis in chemistry and drug development.

## Ultrafast Insights into Material Transformations

Our understanding of how materials behave under extreme conditions has been advanced by new research from The University of Manchester, which reveals how **'hot electrons'** can trigger rapid structural changes in metals within billionths of a second. When metals are exposed to powerful laser pulses, their electrons can heat up almost instantaneously to extreme temperatures, while the atomic lattice remains relatively cool. This study, published in *Physical Review Materials*, demonstrates that this intense electronic excitation alone can cause metals to switch between different crystal structures, such as hexagonal, face-centered cubic, and body-centered cubic, in a fraction of a picosecond. Led by Dr. Sam Azadi, the research highlights that this behavior is driven not by traditional heat flow through the atomic lattice, but by **electronic entropy**, a measure of how electron populations spread across energy states at high temperatures. This discovery offers new insights into ultrafast materials behavior and could open doors for designing materials with tailored properties for applications ranging from ultrafast electronics to novel catalysts.
