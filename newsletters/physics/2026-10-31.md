Good morning, physics enthusiasts! Today's "My Daily Clippings" brings a fascinating array of breakthroughs, from the discovery of entirely new forms of magnetism to profound challenges to our understanding of the cosmos. We'll delve into materials science pushing the boundaries of quantum computing, explore how quantum information might become more robust, and even question the very existence of dark energy, all while witnessing a fundamental rule of laser physics being rewritten.

## Unveiling New States of Matter

For decades, our understanding of magnetism in materials without external influence was largely confined to two categories: **ferromagnetism**, like the magnets on your fridge, and **antiferromagnetism**, where atomic magnetic moments align in opposite directions, cancelling each other out. But a team led by the U.S. Department of Energy's SLAC National Accelerator Laboratory has now provided experimental proof for a third, distinct class: **altermagnetism**. Published in *Science*, this discovery, confirmed in **manganese telluride** (MnTe), reveals a unique geometric twist in the electronic structure of certain materials, leading to a magnetic order that is neither purely ferromagnetic nor antiferromagnetic. This confirmation, coming just four years after the initial postulate, opens up entirely new avenues for spintronics and data storage technologies, potentially offering faster and more energy-efficient devices.

Meanwhile, the quest for **topological superconductors**, materials that could revolutionize **quantum computing** by combining two distinct "flavors" of superconductivity, has seen a significant advance. Researchers at Cornell University, led by Associate Professor of Physics Brad Ramshaw and doctoral student Sahas Kamat, have demonstrated that **uranium ditelluride**, or UTe₂, can exhibit both superconducting states simultaneously under extreme pressure. This rare behavior, published in *Nature Physics*, involved applying pressure equivalent to about eighteen thousand pounds focused on five millimeters, akin to nine cars balanced on a pinky finger. This makes UTe₂ a leading candidate for hosting the exotic properties required for robust quantum bits, or **qubits**.

## Advancing Quantum Information and Cosmology

The fragility of quantum information remains a major hurdle for **quantum networks**. However, a promising development comes from the study of **quantum skyrmions**, which have been shown to outlast entanglement. Engineers at the University of Pennsylvania, led by Professor Liang Feng and postdoctoral fellow Haoqi Zhao, demonstrated this resilience by imprinting a skyrmion pattern onto entangled photons using a silicon nitride photonic chip. Even when enough disruption was introduced to sever the photons' entanglement, the topological property of the skyrmion remained intact. This resilience hints at more robust quantum networks, potentially connecting quantum computers into more powerful systems and enabling new forms of secure communication.

Complementing these advances in quantum materials and information resilience, a new device promises to improve the readout of **superconducting qubits**. Researchers at the National Institute of Standards and Technology, NIST, and the University of Colorado Boulder, including Akash V. Dixit, have developed a system that uses **millimeter-wave radiation** to accurately read the states of these delicate quantum bits without disturbing them. This is a critical step, as preserving the quantum state during measurement is paramount for reliable quantum computation, and their method achieved over ninety-nine percent accuracy without needing a quantum-limited amplifier.

Shifting our gaze to the cosmic scale, mathematicians have put forth a provocative argument challenging the very existence of **dark energy**. A team from the University of California, Davis, including distinguished professor emeritus of mathematics Blake Temple, published calculations in *Proceedings of the Royal Society A* suggesting a fundamental flaw in the standard model of the universe. Their work implies that **Einstein's equations** of general relativity might explain the universe's accelerating expansion without needing this mysterious component, arguing that instabilities inherent in the **Einstein-Euler equations** could naturally lead to cosmic acceleration. This potential paradigm shift, if confirmed, would rewrite our understanding of cosmic evolution.

Adding to our evolving cosmic picture, a wave of **three-dimensional mapping** is challenging the long-held view of the **interstellar medium** as a static backdrop. Instead, this new mapping reveals the interstellar medium's dynamic nature, with complex structures and movements that significantly influence galactic processes. Research by Jonathan Shelest, an undergraduate at the Technion in Haifa, Israel, and his colleagues, utilizing data from the Gaia telescope, has created detailed maps showing that much of the interstellar medium is unstable, a finding that contradicts leading theoretical models. This shift in perspective, reported in *Quanta Magazine*, promises a more nuanced understanding of how stars form and galaxies evolve.

## Rewriting the Rules of Laser Physics

Finally, a truly surprising result comes from laser physics, where scientists have reportedly broken a textbook rule by generating **X-rays** with much higher energies than conventional theory predicts. This unexpected discovery, published in *Nature Photonics*, comes from a collaboration between TU Wien, the Vienna University of Technology, and the University of California San Diego. Researchers, including Professor Tenio Popmintchev and Dimitar Popmintchev, found that when powerful ultraviolet lasers were directed at **helium atoms**, two electrons working together could overcome a previously accepted energy cutoff. This breakthrough, where two quantum-mechanically correlated electrons release their energy simultaneously, reveals new mechanisms for energy transfer in laser-matter interactions and opens new possibilities for quantum research.
