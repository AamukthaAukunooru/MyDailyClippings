## Unlocking Quantum Secrets at the Smallest Scales

In a truly astonishing feat, researchers at **Delft University of Technology** in the Netherlands have achieved direct control over the **magnetic core** of a single atom using radio waves. This accomplishment challenges long-held beliefs among physicists who considered such direct manipulation of a nuclear spin impossible due to its inherent shielding. The team, including lead researcher **Sander Otte** and main author **Hester Vennema**, utilized a converted **scanning tunneling microscope** and refined a technique called **double magnetic resonance** to independently address the magnetism of both the atom's nucleus and its orbiting electron. Otte himself expressed surprise, stating, "According to our simple understanding, this should not really happen". This unexpected ability to flip the nuclear spin, even when largely disentangled from the electron, opens up significant new avenues for secure **quantum information storage**, leveraging the nuclear spin's superior protection from external interference, despite its weaker magnetism.

Meanwhile, in a development poised to impact future electronics, scientists at **Kyushu University** in Japan have discovered that an ultrathin layer of **iridium manganese (IrMn)** can significantly enhance two crucial **spin-related signals**. This improvement occurs within a structure composed of a magnetic insulator and a platinum film. The research, conducted by a spintronics group at Kyushu University, focuses on understanding and manipulating **spin currents**, the flow of angular momentum rather than charge. This fundamental understanding and material enhancement could pave the way for **ultralow-power electronics**, a critical step towards more energy-efficient computing and devices.

## New Eyes and Minds for Physics

Our ability to observe and understand the universe at its most fundamental levels is constantly evolving, thanks to ingenious new tools. Physicists from the Universities of **Regensburg**, **Marburg**, and **Forschungszentrum Jülich** in Germany, along with the University of Graz, have developed a novel **slow-motion electron camera**. This groundbreaking instrument is part of the "Orbital Cinema" project, a collaborative effort backed by an **European Research Council Synergy Grant** worth eleven point three million euros. The camera has allowed them to reveal how intense electric fields drive electrons in **graphene**, causing them to execute **quantum jumps** across energy gaps in a seemingly paradoxical manner. This "orbital cinematography" aims to record the rapid movements of electrons in molecules in ultra-fast slow motion, promising revolutionary insights into the inner workings of quantum leaps and charge transfer processes, and potentially offering new ways to control chemical reactions with electric fields and light. Key researchers involved in this ambitious project include **Professor Stefan Tautz** from Forschungszentrum Jülich, **Professor Ulrich Höfer** from Philipps-Universität Marburg, and **Professor Rupert Huber** from the University of Regensburg.

In another stride for scientific instrumentation, researchers at **Aarhus University** in Denmark have developed an **AI-powered Nuclear Magnetic Resonance (NMR) spectrometer** that can learn for itself. NMR spectroscopy is a powerful technique capable of revealing molecular structure down to the atomic level, but its effectiveness can be limited by weak signals. By employing artificial intelligence, this new spectrometer can learn and optimize the best **pulse sequence** to overcome these limitations, enhancing its ability to probe the intricate details of molecules. This integration of AI into complex scientific instruments represents a significant leap, allowing for more efficient and effective data acquisition and analysis in fields ranging from materials science to molecular biology.

## Revolutionizing Computing with Light

The quest for faster and more efficient computing continues unabated, and a recent breakthrough from **UC San Diego** in the United States offers a dramatic leap forward for data storage. Scientists there have developed a novel method that could make computer memory **one thousand times faster** than current technologies. Led by **Professor Abdoulaye Ndao** and with **Muhammad Waleed Khalid** as the first author, the team has found a surprising new way to store digital information using light instead of traditional magnets. Their technique, known as **optical switching**, involves precisely shaping and shrinking an **ultrafast laser beam** to manipulate the magnetic states within a material. This innovation overcomes previous limitations, demonstrating successful optical switching in a structure with nine alternating layers of platinum and cobalt, without requiring specific light polarization. Published in *Nature Communications*, this development promises to pave the way for smaller, faster, and more energy-efficient memory devices, potentially leading to consumer electronics with significantly faster boot times and longer battery life.
