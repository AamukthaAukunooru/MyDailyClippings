## Clogging: A Universal Phase Transition Confirmed

The frustration of a clogged drain or a jammed printer is a common experience, but the underlying physics of how particles get stuck and halt flow has long presented a challenge for experimental verification. Now, researchers at the Institute of Science Tokyo have provided the first experimental proof that **clogging** behaves as a **nonequilibrium phase transition**. This finding confirms theoretical predictions and offers a deeper understanding of a phenomenon observed across diverse systems, from granular materials to biological transport networks.

The team, led by former graduate student Takahide Minemura and Professor Emeritus Satoshi Okuma in the Department of Physics, investigated the motion of **superconducting vortices** in **amorphous molybdenum-germanium thin films**. These vortices, which are quantized whirlpools of circulating current, were driven by direct electrical currents. In these disordered films, intrinsic material defects act as **pinning sites**, trapping individual vortices. When a vortex becomes immobilized, it creates a microscopic bottleneck, obstructing the passage of subsequent vortices and leading to a complete cessation of flow, or clogging.

Published in *Communications Physics* on September twenty-fifth, two thousand twenty-six, their study demonstrated that this transition from continuous flow to complete obstruction shares critical dynamics with **vortex depinning**. Crucially, they showed that clogging is characterized by **diverging relaxation times** near a critical threshold, a hallmark of a nonequilibrium phase transition. This experimental validation, which required a modified two-step measurement protocol, significantly advances our fundamental understanding of how such widespread transport phenomena operate.

## Steering Quantum Information with Magnetic Waves

In the quest for next-generation computing, particularly in the realm of quantum technologies, scientists are constantly exploring new ways to manipulate information. A significant step forward comes from the U.S. Department of Energy's (DOE) Ames National Laboratory, where researchers have discovered a novel method to control the **handedness**, or **chirality**, of **magnons**. Magnons are tiny magnetic waves that can carry information through materials without the movement of electrical charge, offering a potentially more efficient alternative to conventional electronic technologies.

Ames Lab Scientist Rob McQueeney and his team made this discovery while studying magnetic excitations in **Kagome metals**, a class of quantum materials known for their unusual magnetic behavior. They focused on **ferrimagnets**, materials that naturally host both right-handed and left-handed magnons. The breakthrough lies in their ability to switch a magnon's handedness by simply changing the material's magnetic state with an external magnetic field.

The researchers observed that in certain ferrimagnets, such as **ErMn₆Sn₆**, the normally distinct right- and left-handed magnon states can **mix** under specific symmetry conditions. This mixing, seen as "avoided crossings" in their measurements, allows for the manipulation of magnon handedness with relatively little energy. In contrast, in a similar material, **TbMn₆Sn₆**, magnons with opposite handedness crossed without interacting. This ability to easily control and combine different magnon chiralities, published in *Physical Review Letters* in July two thousand twenty-six, opens up exciting new possibilities for developing **magnon-based quantum information and quantum computing technologies**.

## In-Sensor Computing: Merging Detection and Processing

The ever-increasing volume of data generated by sensors demands more efficient processing, ideally closer to the source. Addressing this challenge, a collaborative research effort led by institutions including the Changchun Institute of Optics, Fine Mechanics and Physics of the Chinese Academy of Sciences and Peking University, has developed an innovative **aluminum gallium nitride (AlGaN) device** that integrates **light detection, memory, and basic processing** capabilities. This advancement, reported in *Nature Materials*, represents a significant step towards **in-sensor computing**.

The device leverages the unique properties of **Group III nitride semiconductors**, which are well-established in industrial manufacturing. Specifically, it exploits **polarization interfaces** within an AlGaN heterostructure. These interfaces create built-in electric fields that effectively separate photogenerated electrons and holes, reducing their recombination and enabling high light sensitivity. By employing asymmetric nickel and titanium contacts, the researchers can control the movement of these separated charge carriers. Under a forward bias, holes are retained at one interface, allowing the device to store incident light information. Conversely, under zero or reverse bias, the stored holes are released, transforming the same component into a high-speed detector.

This ingenious control scheme allows the device to operate in multiple modes without any physical structural changes. It can detect light at the impressive level of **nanowatts per square centimeter** and maintain stable operation at temperatures up to **three hundred degrees Celsius**. With a device-based model achieving over ninety percent accuracy, this technology promises to reduce the need for transferring raw data between separate detectors, memory units, and processors, thereby enhancing the efficiency and feasibility of future in-sensor computing architectures.
