## Unveiling a New Quantum Space-Time Limit for Electrons

In a groundbreaking discovery that could revolutionize electronics, an international collaboration, including researchers from the **Regensburg Center for Ultrafast Nanoscopy (RUN)**, has identified a surprising new **quantum limit** that restricts how precisely electrons can be tracked in both space and time simultaneously. This finding, reported on November seventh, challenges previous assumptions and extends our understanding of fundamental quantum mechanics.

While Werner Heisenberg's famous **uncertainty principle** dictates that a particle's position and momentum cannot be known with arbitrary precision, it did not impose the same restriction on position and time. Now, experimental evidence demonstrates that an electron's location and its behavior over time are indeed fundamentally linked in a way that prevents unlimited precision.

The team, led by Professors Jascha Repp, Rupert Huber, Franz Giessibl, and Klaus Richter, utilized a sophisticated new laser system to observe electron behavior on incredibly short **attosecond** timescales. By precisely timing laser pulses, they drove electrons from an extremely sharp metal tip towards a silver surface, a distance of only a few atomic diameters. This allowed them to directly control and study the quantum motion of individual electrons.

The implications of this newly observed **space-time limit** are profound. The ability to precisely control **electron wave packets** could pave the way for manipulating chemical reactions with unprecedented accuracy and developing electronics hundreds of thousands of times faster than current technology. The researchers noted that concentrating a single electron within an exceptionally small region of space and time can generate local peak current densities as high as one trillion amperes per square centimeter, opening new avenues for ultrafast quantum technologies.

## A Crucial Step Towards Room-Temperature Superconductivity

The long-sought dream of **room-temperature superconductivity** has moved a significant step closer, thanks to new research demonstrating that a key prerequisite for this phenomenon, the pairing of electrons, can occur at ambient temperatures. Scientists from Utrecht University, Leiden University, and Ludwig Maximilian University of Munich published their findings on November seventh, shedding light on the complex mechanisms governing high-temperature superconductors.

Superconductivity, the ability of certain materials to conduct electricity with zero resistance, relies on two fundamental conditions: electrons binding together into **Cooper pairs**, and these pairs moving in a synchronized, collective motion known as **phase coherence**. In conventional superconductors, these two processes happen concurrently at extremely low temperatures. However, in **cuprate high-temperature superconductors**, the new experimental findings reveal a crucial decoupling.

First authors Maialen Ortego Larrazabal and Jiasen Niu, using custom-built measuring equipment, were able to differentiate between currents carried by individual electrons and those carried by electron pairs. Their work showed that electrons in cuprates can form **Cooper pairs** at temperatures well above the point where superconductivity actually sets in, with the binding interaction strong enough to maintain these pairs even at room temperature.

This means that while electron pairing is achievable at room temperature, **phase coherence** remains the primary bottleneck preventing resistance-free electrical conduction under ambient conditions. This breakthrough provides invaluable insight into the fundamental physics of these materials and offers a clear direction for future research aimed at unlocking the full potential of room-temperature superconductivity for energy-efficient technologies.

## Magnetic Fields Could Push White Dwarfs Beyond the Chandrasekhar Limit

In a fascinating development that challenges a cornerstone of stellar astrophysics, new computer simulations suggest that some **dead stars**, known as **white dwarfs**, could grow far more massive than previously thought possible. This discovery, announced on November sixth by researchers from the Department of Physics at the **Indian Institute of Science (IISc)** and their collaborators, could reshape our understanding of stellar evolution and the universe's most powerful explosions.

For decades, the **Chandrasekhar limit**, approximately one point four times the mass of our Sun, has stood as the theoretical upper mass boundary for a stable white dwarf. Beyond this limit, the immense gravitational pressure was believed to cause the star to collapse further, often leading to a catastrophic **Type Ia supernova**.

However, these new simulations introduce a powerful new factor: internal **magnetic fields**. The research indicates that even a relatively weak magnetic field can become increasingly influential as a white dwarf accumulates matter and compresses. This compression strengthens the internal magnetic field, which in turn provides additional pressure that helps the star resist the crushing force of gravity.

The simulations showed that a magnetized white dwarf could theoretically reach approximately two point four times the mass of the Sun, substantially exceeding the conventional Chandrasekhar limit. This finding offers a compelling explanation for the existence of unusually bright **Type Ia supernovae** that astronomers have observed, which previously suggested progenitor white dwarfs with masses as high as two point eight times that of the Sun. This work opens up exciting new avenues for exploring the extreme physics of stellar remnants and the cosmic events they trigger.
