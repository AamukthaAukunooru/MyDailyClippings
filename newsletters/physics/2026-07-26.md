## Altermagnetism Takes Center Stage, Reshaping Our View of Magnetism

In a truly significant development for condensed matter physics, the **Europhysics Prize** of the European Physical Society (EPS) Condensed Matter Division for 2026 has been awarded for the discovery of **altermagnetism**. This recognition firmly establishes altermagnetism as a third fundamental class of magnetic order, sitting alongside the long-understood ferromagnetism and antiferromagnetism.

While ferromagnets, like the ones on your fridge, have parallel spins leading to a net magnetic moment, and antiferromagnets have anti-parallel spins that cancel out, altermagnets present a unique twist. They exhibit a staggered spin arrangement similar to antiferromagnets, meaning they have no net magnetic moment, but their electronic bands are spin-polarized. This combination gives them properties distinct from both their predecessors, opening up new avenues for scientific exploration and potential technological applications. The discovery promises to influence fields from spintronics to data storage, as researchers can now explore materials with previously unrecognized magnetic behaviors. This kind of fundamental reclassification of a physical phenomenon is rare and speaks volumes about the depth of the discovery.

## Quantum Computing: Breakthroughs and Barriers

The world of quantum technology continues its rapid evolution, with both promising new developments and critical challenges emerging. On the positive side, researchers are pushing the boundaries of qubit design and stability. A team led by Professor Hosung Seo from SKKU, in collaboration with the University of Wisconsin, Madison, and the University of Sydney, has announced the creation of the world's first **zinc oxide spin qubit**. This novel qubit platform leverages the unique properties of zinc oxide, a semiconductor material, and could represent a significant step towards more scalable and robust quantum devices. The ability to create stable spin qubits in new material systems is crucial for overcoming current limitations in quantum computer architecture.

Further bolstering the foundational elements of quantum computing and precision measurement, researchers from the University of Amsterdam and the University of New South Wales have made strides with **ytterbium ions**. They've confirmed that these metallic ions can enter certain long-lived, nearly stable quantum states. This finding, which answers a question that has persisted for decades, is particularly exciting because such long-lived states are ideal for maintaining quantum coherence, a critical requirement for both highly accurate **atomic clocks** and error-resistant **quantum computing** operations.

However, as quantum systems grow in complexity, new challenges inevitably arise. One such hurdle highlighted recently is the **Quantum Zeno effect**. Researchers are finding that as the number of qubits in a system scales up, this effect could potentially "freeze" computations. The Quantum Zeno effect, in essence, describes how frequent measurements can prevent a quantum system from evolving. In the context of quantum computing, this means that the necessary constant monitoring and error correction in large qubit arrays could inadvertently halt the very computations they are designed to facilitate. Understanding and mitigating this effect will be crucial for realizing the full potential of large-scale quantum computers.

## Echoes of the Big Bang and Stellar Alchemy

Our quest to understand the universe's origins and the fundamental processes that govern it continues to yield fascinating results. At the **Large Hadron Collider (LHC)**, all four main experiments, including ATLAS, CMS, ALICE, and LHCb, have found new indications that collisions between oxygen and neon nuclei can create the extreme state of matter that characterized the universe in its very first microseconds after the **Big Bang**. This state, known as **quark-gluon plasma**, is a superheated, superdense "soup" of elementary particles, and observing its formation in smaller systems like oxygen-neon collisions provides invaluable data for understanding the early universe's evolution.

Meanwhile, a scientific team led by the **Facility for Rare Isotope Beams (FRIB)** has shed new light on how elements are forged in the hearts of stars. They've identified the origin of a mysterious excess of low-energy gamma rays emitted by the nucleus of **zinc-70**. Their findings reveal that this excess is caused by **magnetic clues inside atomic nuclei**, specifically a phenomenon known as **nuclear magnetism**. This discovery helps to refine our models of nuclear structure and stellar nucleosynthesis, providing a clearer picture of the intricate processes that create the elements essential for life itself.
