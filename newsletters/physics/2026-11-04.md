## Quantum Materials: Room-Temperature Supersolids and Laser-Drawn Superconductivity

In a significant leap for quantum materials science, an international collaboration has announced the observation of a **supersolid** quantum state at **room temperature**. Researchers from the Faculty of Physics at the University of Warsaw, the Military University of Technology in Poland, IBM Research Zurich, and the French National Centre for Scientific Research (CNRS) achieved this groundbreaking feat by studying **exciton-polaritons**—hybrid quasiparticles of light and matter—within an optical microcavity. Unlike previous supersolids, which typically demand temperatures near absolute zero, this new state exists under ambient conditions, opening exciting avenues for practical quantum technologies. Supersolids are fascinating because they exhibit both the rigid, ordered structure of a crystal and the frictionless flow characteristic of a superfluid, a seemingly contradictory combination. The team's findings, published in *Science Advances*, demonstrate that these short-lived polaritons can form a **Bose-Einstein condensate** and behave as a supersolid, pushing the boundaries of what's possible in quantum engineering.

Adding to the excitement in quantum materials, another team has developed an innovative technique to "draw" superconducting properties directly into materials at the nanoscale. Researchers from ICMAB-CSIC, the Politecnico di Milano, and Université de Liège have adapted a **direct laser writing technique** that allows them to precisely control the behavior of a superconductor, tuning it from a fully superconducting state to an insulating one with extraordinary precision. This method is particularly impactful for **high-temperature superconductors (HTSC)**, which are ceramic materials notoriously difficult to pattern at very small scales. By varying the laser power and exposure time, the scientists can locally modify the material's oxygen levels, which are key to its superconducting properties. This ability to program superconducting and insulating regions could revolutionize the design of components for **quantum technologies**, advanced superconducting electronics, and next-generation memory devices. Irene Biancardi of Politecnico di Milano was the first author of the study, with Anna Palau of ICMAB-CSIC and professors Edoardo Albisetti and Daniela Petti of Politecnico di Milano serving as corresponding authors.

## LHC's Glimpse into the Zeptoverse and Early Universe

CERN's Large Hadron Collider (LHC) continues to be a powerhouse of discovery, with two significant announcements this week. Physicists working with the upgraded **LHCb experiment** have found the first evidence of an exceptionally rare particle decay, a quest spanning forty-two years. They observed a **beauty particle**, specifically a B0 meson, decaying into two **muons** (a process known as Bd to mu mu). This particular decay is predicted to occur only about once in every ten billion beauty particles, making its detection a monumental achievement. This finding, with a statistical significance of 3.2 sigma, brings scientists closer to exploring the **"Zeptoverse"**, an unimaginably tiny realm roughly a billionth of a trillionth of a meter across. Within this subatomic world, new particles and forces, potentially linked to the mysteries of **dark matter** or the universe's matter-antimatter imbalance, might be hiding. Dr. Harry Cliff from the University of Cambridge's Cavendish Laboratory led the analysis team, with Professor Tim Gershon from the University of Warwick leading the LHCb experiment.

In a separate but equally profound discovery, scientists at the LHC have uncovered compelling evidence that even relatively small atomic collisions can recreate the extraordinary state of matter that filled the universe moments after the Big Bang. This primordial substance, known as **quark-gluon plasma**, forms at temperatures exceeding one hundred thousand times that of the Sun's core, causing ordinary protons and neutrons to break down into their fundamental constituents: **quarks and gluons**. What makes this finding particularly surprising is that it emerged from collisions of **oxygen and neon nuclei**, challenging the long-held assumption that only much heavier nuclei, like lead, could generate the extreme conditions necessary for quark-gluon plasma formation. All four major LHC collaborations—ALICE, ATLAS, CMS, and LHCb—have reported signs of this primordial matter, offering physicists a novel way to investigate the universe's earliest moments.

## Cosmic Messengers: High-Energy Neutrinos from IceCube

Far beneath the Antarctic ice, the **IceCube Neutrino Observatory** continues its vital work, detecting incredibly powerful **neutrinos**, often dubbed "ghost particles," that stream to Earth from distant cosmic sources. These elusive particles are of immense interest because they travel vast cosmic distances—billions of light-years—without being deflected by magnetic fields or absorbed by matter, carrying pristine information about the most extreme environments in the universe. Some of the neutrinos detected by IceCube possess energies roughly ten billion times greater than those originating from our own Sun. The detection of these **high-energy neutrinos** is crucial for unraveling their mysterious origins, understanding violent cosmic events like supernovae and active galactic nuclei, and shedding light on some of the universe's deepest secrets. This year, the profound impact of this research was recognized with the 2026 Nobel Prize in Physics awarded to Francis Halzen for his decisive contributions to the IceCube Neutrino Observatory and the discovery of high-energy neutrinos of astrophysical origin. IceCube identifies these particles by observing faint flashes of blue light, known as Cherenkov radiation, produced when a neutrino interacts with the ice.
