This week has brought forth some truly captivating developments across the physics landscape, from the microscopic realm of quantum acoustics to the cosmic expanse of our own galaxy. We're seeing groundbreaking progress in directly observing fundamental quantum phenomena, uncovering exotic astrophysical objects surprisingly close to home, and leveraging the burgeoning power of quantum computers to probe the very origins of matter. These discoveries not only push the boundaries of our understanding but also hint at transformative technological applications on the horizon.

## Sound Makes Quantum Leaps

In a remarkable feat of experimental precision, researchers at Stanford University have for the first time directly observed **quantum jumps of sound** in real time. Published on September 22, this breakthrough marks a significant milestone in quantum physics, demonstrating that even sound, typically perceived as a continuous wave, obeys the discrete energy transitions characteristic of the quantum world.

The team, led by Stanford physicist Amir Safavi-Naeini, managed to record individual **phonons**, the quantum equivalent of sound particles, abruptly vanishing from one energy state and reappearing in another. While quantum jumps have been observed in trapped ions in 1986 and photons in 2007, sound had remained a more elusive target. Safavi-Naeini's group achieved this by utilizing a meticulously crafted mechanical resonator, built with chip fabrication techniques, allowing them to precisely control and monitor the vibrational energy at the quantum scale.

This direct observation of sound behaving quantum mechanically is not merely an academic curiosity. It opens up new avenues for developing **quantum technologies** with sound, potentially leading to advancements in quantum computing, error correction, and highly sensitive biological sensors. The ability to manipulate phonons in this manner is a prerequisite for many operations required by future quantum devices, underscoring the profound implications of this fundamental discovery.

## The Milky Way's First Microblazar

Astronomers have made an electrifying discovery right in our cosmic backyard: the first-ever **microblazar** with a jet pointed directly at Earth within our own Milky Way galaxy. Announced on September 27, this intriguing black hole system, named **IRAS 18293-0941**, is located just 12,000 light-years away.

Blazars are typically supermassive black holes at the centers of distant galaxies that shoot out powerful jets of plasma directly toward Earth, making them appear exceptionally bright. The existence of smaller, stellar-mass black holes, dubbed microblazars, exhibiting similar directed jets has long been theorized but never confirmed in our galaxy until now.

A team of astronomers from Spain, the Netherlands, and Argentina, with lead author Josep Martí from the University of Jaén, identified IRAS 18293-0941 as a binary system. It consists of a black hole orbiting a companion star every 11.38 days, siphoning off material that is then expelled in two powerful jets rushing outwards at three-quarters the speed of light. Crucially, one of these jets is aimed squarely at us. The other, less visible jet, was detected by its collision with a nearby molecular cloud, causing it to light up. This groundbreaking finding, accepted for publication in *Astronomy & Astrophysics* and pre-published on arXiv on September 1, provides an unprecedented opportunity to study these high-energy phenomena up close and test theories of black hole accretion and jet formation.

## Quantum Computers Recreate Early Universe Physics

In a significant stride for quantum simulation, researchers have successfully used a quantum computer to recreate **"string breaking" dynamics**, a fundamental process linked to how matter formed in the extreme conditions of the early universe. This work, led by the Duke Quantum Center (DQC) and published on September 23 in *Nature Physics*, demonstrates the growing capability of trapped-ion quantum computers to tackle profound questions in fundamental physics.

The experiment utilized a **13-ion quantum simulator** to observe the phenomenon where two connected fundamental building blocks of matter, like quarks, are pulled apart. As the separation increases, so much energy accumulates that new particles effectively "pop into existence" when the connection breaks, akin to the formation of particle-antiparticle pairs. Christopher Monroe, the Gilhuly Family Presidential Distinguished Professor of Electrical and Computer Engineering and Physics at Duke, who led the research, highlighted that quantum computer simulations offer "the best platform to investigate complex questions like matter formation, short of having witnessed the Big Bang itself".

This international collaboration, involving institutions such as the University of Maryland, Oxford University, and California Institute of Technology, represents a marked development in the quantum science field. It opens new avenues for physicists to understand the dynamics of fundamental forces and matter evolution, potentially offering insights into the universe's most ancient mysteries.
