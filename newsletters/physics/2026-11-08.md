This week in physics, we've seen remarkable strides at the frontiers of quantum mechanics, from chilling atoms to unprecedented lows in space to simulating fundamental quantum phenomena with trapped ions. Researchers are pushing the boundaries of what's possible in quantum simulation, offering new avenues to explore the universe's most elusive properties and even challenging our very understanding of reality. Meanwhile, the long-sought dream of room-temperature superconductivity has received a crucial new insight, potentially guiding us closer to a technological revolution.

## Quantum Extremes and New Systems

The pursuit of absolute zero continues to yield astonishing results, with Chinese scientists recently setting a new world record for the coldest temperature ever achieved by humans. Aboard the **Tiangong space station**, a team cooled a cloud of **rubidium atoms** to an astounding 9.7 picokelvin, which is less than ten trillionths of a degree above absolute zero. This achievement, published in *Science*, shatters the previous record of 38 picokelvin set by a German team in 2021, and is roughly four times colder. The experiment, conducted in the ultra-cold atom physics rack within the station's Mengtian laboratory module, allowed for the observation of the atom cloud for seven seconds. This extended observation time, significantly longer than what's possible on Earth due to gravity, is crucial for studying quantum phenomena like **Bose-Einstein condensates** and exploring potential applications in ultra-precise space sensors, climate change tracking, and even testing Einstein's theory of gravity. Researchers from Peking University, the Shanghai Institute of Optics and Fine Mechanics, and the Technology and Engineering Center for Space Utilization under the Chinese Academy of Sciences were involved in this groundbreaking work.

In another significant development for quantum many-body physics, an international team of researchers from Germany, Thailand, India, China, and the United States has, for the first time, created a **three-component Hubbard system** with tunable interactions in the laboratory. Using a **quantum gas microscope**, the team was able to visualize individual atoms, providing an unprecedented level of control and observation. This work, also published in *Science*, is a crucial step for future quantum simulations in nuclear physics and materials science. The standard Hubbard model typically considers two spin states, but introducing a third component significantly enhances the system's versatility, allowing for the investigation of richer quantum mechanical phases. This approach is particularly promising for advancing our understanding of **high-temperature superconductivity**. Professor Peter Schauss, leading research at the University of Hamburg and the Cluster of Excellence "CUI: Advanced Imaging of Matter," highlighted the observation of a **Mott state**, where strong interactions suppress double occupation of sites, as particularly important.

## Simulating the Unseen and Challenging Reality

Quantum simulations are proving to be powerful tools for probing fundamental physics. In a new *Nature Physics* study, physicists at Oxford University and the Instituto de Física Teórica in Madrid demonstrated the **Aharonov-Bohm effect** in a quantum simulation of a **lattice gauge theory** using a **hybrid trapped-ion quantum computer**. The Aharonov-Bohm effect, predicted in 1959 by Yakir Aharonov and David Bohm, describes how a charged particle can be influenced by a magnetic field even if it never directly enters the region where the field is present. This experiment used **qubits** to represent gauge fields and the vibrational modes of the same trapped ions as **quantum oscillators** to embody matter. By encoding magnetic flux in an entangled state of two qubits, the researchers observed destructive interference that suppressed the tunneling of a simulated matter particle, effectively freezing its motion. This work represents a significant step towards using quantum simulators to tackle complex problems in high-energy physics that are intractable for classical computers.

On a more conceptual, yet equally profound, front, recent work has continued to challenge Albert Einstein's classical notion of reality. *Quanta Magazine* reported on how a strange quantum theorem has further "killed" Einstein's reality. Einstein, along with Boris Podolsky and Nathan Rosen, proposed a famous thought experiment in 1935 to argue for a "real and local" world, where objects possess intrinsic properties independent of observation. However, John Stewart Bell's theorem in 1964 provided an experimental test that showed the world could not be both real and local, a finding confirmed by experiments in the 1980s. More recently, the **PBR theorem**, named after Matthew Pusey, Jonathan Barrett, and Terry Rudolph, delivered what many theorists consider the final blow to Einstein's classical reality. This theorem, experimentally confirmed multiple times, suggests that the quantum state is either objectively real or that, in some sense, nothing is real, including the quantum state itself. This means the properties of a quantum object might be inextricably linked with the act of observing it, a concept Einstein found deeply unsettling.

## The Elusive Quest for Room-Temperature Superconductivity

The dream of **room-temperature superconductivity**, which promises technologies like levitating trains and ultra-efficient energy storage, has received a crucial new insight. New research led by scientists from Utrecht University, Leiden University, and Ludwig Maximilian University of Munich has clarified one of the key requirements for achieving this technological marvel. Their findings, published in *Science*, reveal that in **high-temperature superconductors**, the formation of **electron pairs**, known as **Cooper pairs**, can occur at temperatures well above the point where superconductivity actually sets in, even at room temperature. This suggests that the "glue" holding these pairs together is strong enough at ambient temperatures.

However, the bottleneck for achieving room-temperature superconductivity appears to be the **phase coherence**, which is the synchronous movement of these electron pairs. In conventional, low-temperature superconductors, electron pairing and phase coherence happen simultaneously. But in high-temperature superconductors, the pairs form earlier, indicating that their collective, synchronized motion is the missing ingredient. This research, enabled by newly developed measuring equipment that can distinguish between currents carried by individual electrons and those carried by pairs, provides a new direction for the search for practical room-temperature superconductors. As research leader Ingmar Swart of Utrecht University noted, demonstrating that electron pairing can occur at room temperature is "really good news," as it confirms one of the two essential conditions can be met. The focus now shifts to understanding and enhancing the phase coherence of these Cooper pairs.
