## Peering into the Quantum Vacuum: CERN's Latest Glimpse of the Ultra-Rare

Particle physicists at **CERN** have announced the first evidence of an extraordinarily rare particle decay, a process so elusive it occurs only about once in every ten billion **beauty particles**. This breakthrough, observed by the upgraded **LHCb experiment** at CERN's **Large Hadron Collider**, marks a significant milestone in a search that has spanned more than forty years. The elusive process, known as **Bd to mu mu**, involves a particle containing a beauty quark transforming into two muons, which are heavier relatives of electrons.

The evidence for this decay has reached a statistical significance of 3.2 sigma, a strong indicator, though physicists will require additional data before formally declaring a discovery. This long-awaited result is crucial for testing the **Standard Model** of particle physics, our leading framework for describing fundamental particles and their interactions. Even small discrepancies between theoretical predictions and observed decay rates could provide valuable clues, potentially pointing towards the existence of unknown particles or forces of nature. Researchers from the **University of Manchester** played important roles in this international effort, contributing to the advanced silicon pixel detector, the **Vertex Locator**, which is positioned just five millimeters from the LHC beams and precisely measures particle paths.

## Cosmic Enigmas: From Silent Black Holes to Accelerated Pulsar Hunts

Our understanding of the universe's most enigmatic objects, **black holes** and **pulsars**, has also seen significant advances. New research led by **Caltech** suggests that many black holes may be born not from spectacular supernova explosions, but from massive stars quietly collapsing without a bang. By analyzing gravitational wave observations from **LIGO**, **Virgo**, and **KAGRA** detector networks, researchers identified surprising patterns in two hundred fifty-seven black hole mergers. These patterns closely match theoretical predictions for black holes formed from stars that never went supernova, offering intriguing evidence for a distinct population of these cosmic behemoths. This finding provides another piece of evidence that massive stars can follow different paths at the ends of their lives, potentially revealing how a substantial fraction of the universe's black holes came into existence.

In another astronomical leap, an international team of scientists has discovered a new **gamma-ray pulsar**, designated **PSR J1622-7204**, using a method approximately a million times faster than previous techniques. The team, comprising researchers from the **Max Planck Institute for Gravitational Physics (Albert Einstein Institute)** in Hannover, Germany, the **Weizmann Institute of Science** in Rehovot, Israel, and **Chalmers University** in Gothenburg, Sweden, adapted algorithms from **cryptography** to process fifteen years of data from **NASA's Fermi Large Area Telescope**. This innovative approach allowed them to pinpoint the pulsar, which spins two hundred sixty-one times per second, in just ten hours of computing time on a single standard CPU core. This dramatic acceleration in detection capability opens new avenues for discovering previously undetectable pulsars, especially those in binary systems, which have long been considered almost impossible to find.

## The Interstellar Speed Bump: Relativistic Drag on Solar Sails

Finally, for those dreaming of interstellar voyages, new research has uncovered a surprising obstacle for future **solar sails** traveling at extreme speeds. Scientists **Chao Shen** and **Jiaze Li** of the **Harbin Institute of Technology** have found that once a spacecraft approaches seventy-five percent of the speed of light, the very light propelling it could begin to slow it down.

Solar sails work by harnessing the momentum of light, using vast, lightweight reflective surfaces to gradually build speed in the vacuum of space. However, at relativistic velocities, a phenomenon known as **relativistic light aberration** comes into play. From the perspective of the rapidly moving spacecraft, the diffusely scattered light, which normally contributes to forward thrust, becomes increasingly concentrated in the spacecraft's forward direction. Because emitting light carries momentum, this forward concentration of scattered light creates a corresponding recoil force in the opposite direction, effectively producing a drag that works against the sail's propulsion. This unexpected source of resistance will be a critical factor for engineers and physicists to consider as humanity plans its ambitious missions to reach distant stars.
