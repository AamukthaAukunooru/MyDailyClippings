## The Ghost of the Early Universe

Astronomers may have stumbled upon a relic from the universe's infancy: a mysterious object named **Phoebe**, which could be a **primordial black hole** formed just moments after the **Big Bang**. This intriguing entity was detected through the subtle bending of light, a phenomenon known as **gravitational lensing**, when it briefly magnified a distant star. What makes Phoebe particularly fascinating is its estimated mass, which is only about three times that of our Moon. Such a low mass for a black hole is highly unusual for those formed from collapsing stars, leading scientists to hypothesize its primordial origin. If confirmed, Phoebe would represent a direct observational link to the universe's earliest moments, offering invaluable insights into the conditions that prevailed before the formation of the first stars and galaxies. Furthermore, these lunar-mass primordial black holes are also considered prime candidates for a component of **dark matter**, the elusive substance thought to make up about 27 percent of the universe's mass. The discovery, reported by *ScienceDaily Physics* on November twenty-sixth, could therefore open new avenues in the ongoing hunt for dark matter.

## A Quantum Leap with 123 Qubits

In the rapidly advancing field of quantum computing, researchers have announced a significant milestone: the successful entanglement of one hundred and twenty-three **qubits** in a new quantum network. This breakthrough, detailed in a study published in *Physical Review Letters*, brings us closer to realizing practical quantum computers. Unlike classical bits that store information as either a zero or a one, qubits can exist in a superposition of both states simultaneously, allowing for exponentially greater computational power. In this innovative network, the researchers have managed to store information in the precise **positions of atoms**, a novel approach that enhances stability and scalability. The ability to entangle such a large number of qubits coherently is a crucial step towards building fault-tolerant quantum machines capable of tackling problems currently intractable for even the most powerful supercomputers. This development, highlighted by *Phys.org Physics* on November twenty-fifth, signals that the "quantum leap" in computing is indeed taking shape, with real-world applications and scientific breakthroughs now anticipated within years, not decades.

## Trapping Antimatter to Unravel Cosmic Asymmetry

Physicists have achieved a remarkable feat in the quest to understand one of the universe's most profound mysteries: why there is so much more matter than antimatter. Researchers have successfully trapped a **positron**, the antimatter counterpart of an electron, and cooled it to temperatures approaching **absolute zero**. This delicate operation involved suspending the positron in a **wall-free trap**, ensuring it remained safely isolated from ordinary matter, which would annihilate it upon contact. The ability to precisely control and study antimatter at such extreme conditions is critical for experiments aiming to compare the fundamental properties of matter and antimatter with unprecedented accuracy. According to *ScienceDaily Physics* on November twenty-fifth, this breakthrough could provide vital clues in explaining the cosmic imbalance that allowed our universe, and everything in it, to exist. If matter and antimatter were created in equal amounts during the Big Bang, as current theories suggest, then there must be some subtle difference in their behavior that led to the observed dominance of matter. Trapping and studying these elusive particles is a crucial step in uncovering that fundamental asymmetry.
