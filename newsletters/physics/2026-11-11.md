Greetings, physics enthusiasts, and welcome to another edition of My Daily Clippings!

This week has delivered a fascinating array of breakthroughs, pushing the boundaries of our understanding from the subatomic realm to the intricate dance of quantum materials. We're seeing long-sought-after particle decays finally yielding their secrets, novel states of matter emerging at surprisingly accessible temperatures, and ingenious new techniques sharpening our view of the molecular world. It's a testament to the relentless curiosity and innovation driving physics forward.

## Unveiling the Universe's Rarest Secrets

A four-decade quest at **CERN** has culminated in the observation of an extraordinarily rare particle decay, a finding that could offer a crucial window into physics beyond the **Standard Model**. The **LHCb collaboration** announced evidence for the decay of a **Bd meson**, a particle composed of a beauty quark and a down antiquark, into two **muons**. This process is predicted to occur only once in approximately ten billion decays, making it one of the rarest transformations ever observed.

The search, spanning more than forty years, finally yielded a statistical significance of 3.2 standard deviations, a strong indicator of the decay's existence. While the measurements align with the predictions of the Standard Model, the extreme rarity of this decay makes it a "golden channel" for probing the influence of hypothetical new particles that might be too heavy to be directly produced in the Large Hadron Collider. Researchers like **Marco Santimaria** from the Frascati National Laboratory of INFN, who co-led the analysis team, emphasized the extreme precision required to distinguish the muon signal from overwhelming background noise. The success was significantly aided by an upgraded LHCb detector, including the UK-built **Vertex Locator**, which enhanced data collection rates and particle distinction capabilities.

## Quantum Leaps in Materials and Light

In a remarkable development for quantum materials, scientists have observed a bizarre new **quantum state of matter** that behaves simultaneously like a solid and a liquid, and, even more surprisingly, at **room temperature**. This exotic state, known as a **supersolid**, possesses the orderly structure of a crystal while exhibiting the frictionless flow characteristic of a **superfluid**. An international team, including researchers from the **University of Warsaw**, the **Military University of Technology** in Poland, **IBM Research Zurich**, and the **French National Centre for Scientific Research (CNRS)**, achieved this breakthrough by creating hybrid particles of light and matter within a liquid-crystal microcavity. This innovative approach bypasses the need for the extreme cooling typically required for such quantum phenomena, with the microcavities operating at temperatures approaching sixty degrees Celsius, or one hundred forty degrees Fahrenheit. This achievement, detailed in *Science Advances*, overturns a major practical limitation for exotic quantum experiments and paves the way for new light-based computing and sensing technologies.

Meanwhile, at the **U.S. Department of Energy's Oak Ridge National Laboratory (ORNL)**, researchers and their collaborators have used a combination of lasers and neutrons to observe a **long-lived quantum state** in a magnetic material, defying established equilibrium rules. This laser-driven magnetic state exhibited a violation of "**detailed balance**," a fundamental physical rule governing energy exchange in systems at rest. The team, including ORNL's **Chengyun Hua**, studied **magnons**, which are quantum quasiparticles representing magnetic waves. The persistence of these lopsided excess magnons indicates a sustained, non-equilibrium steady state, opening new avenues for studying materials systems far from their normal conditions and holding significant promise for advancements in quantum information science.

Furthering our control over these magnetic waves, scientists at the **U.S. Department of Energy's Ames National Laboratory** have discovered a novel method to control the **handedness of magnons**. By studying **Kagome metals**, such as **TbMn6Sn6** and **ErMn6Sn6**, the team observed distinct behaviors in how magnons interact. They found that in **ferrimagnets**, normally separate left-handed and right-handed magnon states can mix, allowing for a switch in a magnon's handedness with relatively low energy input. This ability to manipulate magnon handedness is crucial for future quantum technologies, as magnons can carry information without the movement of electrical charge, offering a pathway to more efficient computing. Researchers **Rob McQueeney**, **Dhurba R. Jaishi**, **Tyler J. Slade**, and **S. X. M. Riberolles** were key to this discovery.

## Precision Tools for the Quantum Age

The pursuit of ever-greater precision continues to yield remarkable tools for scientific exploration. Researchers have made strides in controlling "**forbidden**" excitations of vibrations in single molecules without destroying them. Using a **quantum-logic spectroscopy** protocol, scientists identified individual components of the fundamental vibrational transition of the **nitrogen molecular ion, N2+**, and demonstrated coherent population transfer between energy levels. This ability to precisely manipulate these otherwise inaccessible molecular vibrations could form the basis of extremely accurate clocks, robust quantum bits, and highly sensitive tests of fundamental physical laws.

In another advancement for atomic measurements, an international team led by **Adelaide University** has developed a shoebox-sized color detection device that achieves record resolution. The device, an ultra-high-resolution spectrometer, can measure the color, or wavelength, composition of light with extreme precision, down to a resolution of just six femtometres. Lead author **Gabriel Britto Monteiro** likened this precision to measuring the exact height of Mount Everest and knowing if a single strand of human hair was laid on its peak. The team cleverly exploited an **integrating sphere** and the phenomenon of **laser speckle**, often considered a nuisance, to achieve this feat. They successfully used the spectrometer to measure the **hyperfine structure of rubidium**, an element vital for next-generation atomic clocks and various quantum technologies. This innovation holds promise for applications ranging from environmental sensing and cancer diagnosis to the development of more precise quantum sensors.

Finally, an international collaboration has developed a new mathematical method that significantly sharpens **three-dimensional X-ray images** at the **nanoscale**. This technique, known as **ptychography**, produces significantly higher-quality images than previously possible. Ptychography is a scanning coherent diffraction imaging technique that is highly sensitive to both high- and low-contrast features, providing a quantitative description of complex material structures with nanometer-scale resolution. The method is already routinely employed at many synchrotron facilities worldwide, with advancements like **multibeam ptychography** offering advantages for imaging larger objects while maintaining nanoscale resolution. This improved imaging capability is crucial for diverse scientific domains, including life science, nano-electronics, and the study of functional materials.
