## Chilling Out in Orbit: A New Low-Temperature Record

Meanwhile, pushing the boundaries of experimental physics, an international team of researchers has achieved a new record-breaking low temperature aboard China's **Tiangong space station**. This remarkable feat, accomplished on November sixth, saw temperatures plummet to an astonishing **one hundred picokelvin**, or one ten-trillionth of a degree above absolute zero. Such extreme cold is not merely a technical achievement; it's a gateway to exploring exotic quantum phenomena that only manifest under these conditions. At these ultracold temperatures, matter behaves in fundamentally different ways, allowing scientists to study **Bose-Einstein condensates**, **superfluids**, and other quantum states with unprecedented clarity. The microgravity environment of the space station is crucial for these experiments, as it minimizes disturbances that would otherwise disrupt delicate quantum systems on Earth. This new low-temperature record paves the way for a new generation of quantum physics experiments in space, promising insights into fundamental forces and the very nature of matter. The research, conducted by scientists from China and other nations, highlights the growing capabilities of space-based laboratories for cutting-edge scientific discovery.

## Flat Optics Take Flight with Air Holes

Finally, in the realm of applied physics, a clever innovation is set to revolutionize compact optics. Researchers have developed a method to transform ordinary **silicon wafers** into flat ultraviolet focusing mirrors by strategically introducing **air holes**. Traditional lenses and mirrors rely on curved surfaces to focus light, which adds bulk and complexity to optical systems. **Metasurfaces**, on the other hand, achieve similar optical functions using a flat layer of nanoscale structures that precisely control the phase of light as it passes through or reflects off them. This new approach leverages the precise arrangement of these microscopic air holes within the silicon to create a flat mirror capable of focusing ultraviolet light. This is particularly significant for compact optical systems, where space and weight are at a premium. The ability to create flat focusing mirrors for ultraviolet light opens up possibilities for more compact and efficient sensors, imaging devices, and even advanced lithography techniques. This development, published on November sixth, represents a significant step forward in the field of **flat optics**, demonstrating how intricate nanoscale engineering can lead to macroscopic improvements in technological capabilities.
