## Unraveling the Cosmos: Dark Energy's Dramatic Flip and the Universe's Dawn

Our understanding of the universe's grand narrative might be due for a significant revision, as new research suggests that **dark energy**, the mysterious force accelerating cosmic expansion, may not be constant. Scientists exploring an overlooked branch of an alternative gravity theory have found that dark energy could have dramatically flipped from negative to positive approximately nine-and-a-half billion years ago. This intriguing possibility comes from an international team led by Professor Özgür Akarsu of Istanbul Technical University, who revisited a framework known as **teleparallel f(T) gravity**. Their 2025 study, published in *Physical Review D*, suggests that this sign-switching dark energy could also help resolve the long-standing **Hubble tension**, a discrepancy in measurements of the universe's expansion rate.

Meanwhile, a groundbreaking new supercomputer simulation, aptly named **Lumina**, is offering an unprecedented look at how our universe emerged from its **cosmic dark age**. Developed by researchers at MIT, Harvard University, and other institutions, Lumina recreates two billion years of early cosmic history across a vast region spanning about one-point-six billion light-years. This massive simulation, run on the Frontier supercomputer at Oak Ridge National Laboratory in Tennessee using over fifteen thousand graphics processing units and more than one hundred thousand CPU cores, details how the first stars and black holes drove the **reionization** of hydrogen and helium, transforming a dark, neutral cosmos into the star-filled expanse we see today. The findings, published in *Monthly Notices of the Royal Astronomical Society*, provide a powerful tool for astronomers to test theories about the universe's evolution.

## Quantum Leaps and Material Innovations: From Diamonds to AI Chips

The race for practical quantum computing continues to accelerate, with two distinct approaches showing significant promise. Researchers at Princeton University and the U.S. Department of Energy's Princeton Plasma Physics Laboratory have discovered a faster way to identify **diamond-based materials** that could power **quantum computers** and sensors at **room temperature**. The key lies not in perfect diamonds, but in their tiny **atomic defects**, where missing carbon atoms or substitutions by elements like **boron and oxygen** can form stable **qubits**. Mark Martirez, a staff research scientist at PPPL, developed a computational technique that treats small clusters of sixteen to thirty atoms around a defect as individual molecules, allowing for more efficient analysis and the identification of new promising candidates.

In parallel, engineers at Purdue University are making strides in **photonic quantum computing**, which harnesses the speed of light. They are developing solutions for fabricating **quantum photonic integrated circuits** on a large scale. Using **photons** as **qubits** offers several advantages, including data transfer at the speed of light and stability at room temperature, which could lead to faster and more energy-efficient quantum computers. Professor Vladimir Shalaev, the Bob and Anne Burnett Distinguished Professor in Electrical and Computer Engineering, highlights the ongoing challenge of creating reliable single-photon sources for scaling up these circuits.

Beyond quantum computing, a breakthrough in magnetism from Brown University could dramatically reduce the energy consumption of **AI chips**. Led by Assistant Professor of Engineering Lucas Caretta, researchers have overcome the long-standing "**dead layer**" problem in **multiferroic materials** like **bismuth ferrite**. These materials, which combine electrical and magnetic properties, typically lose their functionality when made into the extremely thin films required for computer chips. The Brown team's new technique allows these films, down to just a few angstroms thick, to retain their properties, potentially leading to **ultra-low-power computer chips** for artificial intelligence applications. Published in *Nature Materials*, this work also revealed an unexpected form of magnetism, **altermagnetism**, in the engineered material.

Finally, in a fundamental materials science discovery, researchers at National Taiwan University (NTU) have reported the first experimental evidence of the **orbital Nernst effect**. This phenomenon involves the **heat-current-driven transverse accumulation of electrons' orbital angular momentum**, a new pathway for generating and manipulating orbital currents. The observation, detailed in an arXiv paper by authors including Yuto Masuda and Kazuya Ando, was made in Titanium using Nickel electrodes, opening new avenues for **orbital caloritronics** and energy conversion technologies.

## The Path to Sustainable Power: Plasma's Self-Heating Secret

The global pursuit of **fusion power** as a clean, sustainable energy source has seen a significant step forward. Experiments have revealed that **unstable plasma** can actually heat itself using waves it generates. This crucial process, known as **self-heating**, is essential for sustaining fusion reactions in devices like tokamaks and stellarators. A research group led by Professor Katsumi Ida, Assistant Professors Tatsuya Kobayashi and Mikiro Yoshinuma of the National Institute for Fusion Science, and Professor Yuto Kato of Tohoku University, observed this phenomenon in the **Large Helical Device (LHD)**. They found that electromagnetic waves produced by high-energy particles carry heat through a process called **Landau damping**, providing vital knowledge for realizing practical fusion power generation.
